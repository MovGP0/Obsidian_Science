title: "Orthogonality and expansion coefficients"

> [!TLDR]
> An inner product isolates the coefficient of each orthogonal mode by making all other modes contribute zero.

## The inner product

For real square-integrable functions on $[0,L]$, define

$$
\langle f,g\rangle=\int_0^L f(x)g(x)\,dx,
\qquad \|f\|_2=\sqrt{\langle f,f\rangle}.
$$

Square-integrable means $\int_0^L|f|^2dx<\infty$. Functions that differ only on a set of measure zero represent the same element of this space. For complex functions, use $\langle f,g\rangle=\int_0^L f\overline g\,dx$ instead. The bar denotes complex conjugation.

Two nonzero functions are orthogonal if their inner product is zero. This does not mean their graphs never intersect. It means their signed product integrates to zero. The inner product extends the dot product of finite-dimensional vectors to functions; see [[Bilinear and quadratic forms - Overview]].

## Why distinct interval modes are orthogonal

Let $-X_m''=\lambda_mX_m$ and $-X_n''=\lambda_nX_n$, with both functions zero at $0,L$. Multiply the first equation by $X_n$, the second by $X_m$, and subtract. Integration gives

$$
(\lambda_m-\lambda_n)\int_0^L X_mX_n\,dx
=\int_0^L(X_mX_n''-X_m''X_n)\,dx
=[X_mX_n'-X_m'X_n]_0^L=0.
$$

The endpoint values remove the boundary term. If $\lambda_m\ne\lambda_n$, divide by their nonzero difference to obtain orthogonality. The same calculation works for homogeneous Neumann conditions. It depends on the boundary conditions as well as the differential expression.

For $X_n(x)=\sin(n\pi x/L)$, the identity $\sin^2z=(1-\cos(2z))/2$ gives

$$
\|X_n\|_2^2=\frac L2.
$$

Thus these modes are orthogonal but not normalized. Multiplication by $\sqrt{2/L}$ makes each norm equal to one.

## Extracting coefficients

If $f=\sum_n b_nX_n$, take the inner product with $X_m$. Orthogonality removes every term with $n\ne m$:

$$
\langle f,X_m\rangle=b_m\|X_m\|_2^2,
\qquad
b_m=\frac2L\int_0^L f(x)\sin\left(\frac{m\pi x}{L}\right)dx.
$$

For a finite sum this is direct algebra. For an infinite series converging in $L^2$, the step follows because the inner product is continuous in that norm. Completeness of the sine system ensures every $L^2(0,L)$ function has such a mean-square expansion. Mean-square convergence alone does not assign endpoint values or guarantee pointwise convergence everywhere.

## Worked coefficient calculation

Take $L=1$ and $f(x)=x(1-x)$. Put $k=n\pi$. Integration by parts first gives

$$
I_n=\int_0^1x(1-x)\sin(kx)\,dx
=\frac1k\int_0^1(1-2x)\cos(kx)\,dx.
$$

The first boundary term vanishes because $f(0)=f(1)=0$. Integrate the remaining expression again. The sine terms at $0,1$ vanish because $k=n\pi$, leaving

$$
I_n=\frac2{k^2}\int_0^1\sin(kx)\,dx
=\frac{2(1-\cos k)}{k^3}.
$$

Consequently

$$
b_n=2I_n=\frac{4(1-(-1)^n)}{n^3\pi^3}
=\begin{cases}
8/(n^3\pi^3),&n\text{ odd},\\
0,&n\text{ even}.
\end{cases}
$$

For zero endpoint heat data, the corresponding solution for $t>0$ is

$$
u(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_ne^{-\kappa n^2\pi^2t}\sin(n\pi x).
$$

For $t\geq\varepsilon>0$, the exponential dominates every derivative's polynomial factor, which permits termwise differentiation. Since $\sum|b_n|<\infty$, the initial series also converges uniformly. Its $L^2$ limit is $f$, so continuity makes that equality pointwise. The corner derivative qualification discussed in [[15.03 Initial and boundary data]] still applies.

## Approximation and common errors

The partial sum $S_N=\sum_{n=1}^N b_nX_n$ is the orthogonal projection onto the first $N$ modes. If $v$ lies in that span, then $f-S_N$ is orthogonal to $S_N-v$. Expanding the squared norm gives

$$
\|f-v\|_2^2=\|f-S_N\|_2^2+\|S_N-v\|_2^2.
$$

Thus the projection has the smallest mean-square error in that span. Do not omit the divisor $\|X_n\|_2^2$, and do not use this orthogonal formula for a system that has not been shown orthogonal.

## Practice

1. Find the sine coefficients of $f=3\sin(\pi x)+2\sin(2\pi x)$ on $[0,1]$.
2. Normalize the constant Neumann eigenfunction $X_0=1$ on $[0,L]$. Find the coefficient of this normalized function for $f(x)=x$.

## Worked answers

1. Orthogonality gives $\langle f,X_1\rangle=3/2$ and $\langle f,X_2\rangle=1$. Divide by each squared norm $1/2$ to obtain $b_1=3$, $b_2=2$. Every other coefficient is zero.
2. The constant's squared norm is $L$, so $\phi_0=1/\sqrt L$ has norm one. Its coefficient is $\langle x,\phi_0\rangle=L^{-1/2}\int_0^Lx\,dx=L^{3/2}/2$. Multiplication by $\phi_0$ gives the constant component $L/2$, the mean of $x$ on the interval.
