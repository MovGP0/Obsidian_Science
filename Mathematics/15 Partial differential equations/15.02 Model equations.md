title: "Laplace, heat, and wave model equations"

> [!TLDR]
> Laplace problems use boundary data, heat problems use one initial profile, and wave problems usually use two initial profiles.

## Three mathematical models

Let $u$ be a scalar function. In $d$ spatial coordinates $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_d)$, define the Laplacian by

$$
\Delta u=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial x_j^2}.
$$

The following equations describe different mathematical relations. Their names are conventional; the examples here concern functions without physical units.

| Equation | Standard form | Typical data on a bounded domain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Laplace | $\Delta u=0$ | One suitable condition along the boundary |
| Heat | $u_t=\kappa\Delta u$, $\kappa>0$ | $u(x,0)=f(x)$ and suitable boundary data for $t>0$ |
| Wave | $u_{tt}=c^2\Delta u$, $c>0$ | $u(x,0)=f(x)$, $u_t(x,0)=g(x)$, and suitable boundary data |

The word “typical” matters. A pure Neumann Laplace problem also needs an integral compatibility condition, and its solution is only unique up to a constant. See [[15.03 Initial and boundary data]]. The data must match the domain and the selected solution class.

## A Laplace problem

On the square $0<x<1$, $0<y<1$, prescribe the boundary values of $u=x^2-y^2$. Direct differentiation gives

$$
u_{xx}=2,\qquad u_{yy}=-2,\qquad\Delta u=0.
$$

The four edge functions are $-y^2$ at $x=0$, $1-y^2$ at $x=1$, $x^2$ at $y=0$, and $x^2-1$ at $y=1$. Their corner values agree. The polynomial is therefore a classical solution of this boundary-value problem. There is no initial parameter in this formulation. Giving only $u(x,0)$ would leave three edges without prescribed data.

## A heat problem

On $0<x<\pi$, impose $u(0,t)=u(\pi,t)=0$ and the initial profile $f(x)=\sin x$. Try $u(x,t)=A(t)\sin x$. Since $u_{xx}=-A(t)\sin x$, substitution gives

$$
A'(t)\sin x=-\kappa A(t)\sin x.
$$

At interior points where $\sin x\ne0$, divide to obtain $A'=-\kappa A$. The initial profile requires $A(0)=1$, so

$$
u(x,t)=e^{-\kappa t}\sin x.
$$

The function $A$ has one integration constant because its equation has one derivative in $t$. The equation itself determines the initial derivative: $u_t(x,0)=-\kappa\sin x$. Prescribing a different initial derivative as extra data would make this classical problem inconsistent.

## A wave problem

Use the same interval and zero endpoint values, but now solve $u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}$. The same product trial gives

$$
A''(t)=-c^2A(t),\qquad A(t)=a\cos(ct)+b\sin(ct).
$$

If $u(x,0)=\sin x$ and $u_t(x,0)=2\sin x$, then $a=1$ and $cb=2$. Thus

$$
u(x,t)=\left(\cos(ct)+\frac{2}{c}\sin(ct)\right)\sin x.
$$

Two independent initial profiles determine two integration constants for every spatial mode. The heat and wave solutions share the same spatial function but have different evolution equations.

## What the comparison explains

For the spatial function $\sin(nx)$ on $(0,\pi)$, its second derivative is $-n^2\sin(nx)$. Heat coefficients therefore decay as $e^{-\kappa n^2t}$. Larger $n$ decays faster. Wave coefficients oscillate with angular parameter $cn$. Neither conclusion follows from the common endpoint values alone; the PDE determines it.

Laplace, heat, and wave equations are standard examples of elliptic, parabolic, and hyperbolic equations, respectively. The classification describes their principal derivative structure. It does not replace a statement of the actual data. In particular, a PDE name alone is not a complete mathematical problem.

## Practice

1. For $u_t=3u_{xx}$ on $(0,\pi)$ with zero endpoint values and $u(x,0)=4\sin(2x)$, find and check the solution.
2. For $u_{tt}=4u_{xx}$ on the same interval, use $u(x,0)=0$ and $u_t(x,0)=6\sin(3x)$ to determine one-mode coefficients.

## Worked answers

1. Write $u=A(t)\sin(2x)$. Then $u_{xx}=-4A\sin(2x)$, so $A'=-12A$. Since $A(0)=4$, the result is $u=4e^{-12t}\sin(2x)$. Its endpoint values vanish and its initial value has the required coefficient.
2. Here $c=2$ and the spatial index is $3$, so $A''=-36A$. Write $A=a\cos(6t)+b\sin(6t)$. The first initial condition gives $a=0$; the second gives $6b=6$. Therefore $u=\sin(6t)\sin(3x)$.
