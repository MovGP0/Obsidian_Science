---
title: "Well-posedness of partial differential equations"
---

> [!TLDR]
> A well-posed problem has a solution, has only one solution, and changes its solution continuously when its data change in the stated norms.

## Three separate questions

A PDE problem includes an equation, a domain, initial or boundary data, and a solution class. It is **well posed** in specified function spaces if it has existence, uniqueness, and continuous dependence on the data.

Existence asks whether an admissible solution satisfies all requirements. Uniqueness asks whether two admissible solutions with the same data must coincide. Continuous dependence asks whether small data changes give small solution changes in the chosen norms. A proof of one property does not prove the others.

For a normed data space and solution space, continuous dependence at data $f$ means: for each $\varepsilon>0$, there is $\delta>0$ such that $\|f-\widetilde f\|_{\mathrm{data}}<\delta$ implies $\|u-\widetilde u\|_{\mathrm{solution}}<\varepsilon$. A stronger useful result is an estimate with a constant $C$ independent of the particular data:

$$
\|u-\widetilde u\|_{\mathrm{solution}}
\leq C\|f-\widetilde f\|_{\mathrm{data}}.
$$

The norms cannot be omitted. An estimate in mean square is a different claim from an estimate on every derivative.

## A forward heat estimate

Consider $u_t=\kappa u_{xx}$ on $0<x<L$, $0<t\leq T$, with $\kappa>0$, zero Dirichlet values, and initial profile $f$. Let $u,v$ be two sufficiently smooth solutions with initial profiles $f,g$. Their difference $w=u-v$ satisfies the same homogeneous PDE and boundary values, with initial profile $f-g$.

Define $\|w(t)\|_2^2=\int_0^Lw(x,t)^2dx$. Differentiation followed by integration by parts yields

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\frac12\|w(t)\|_2^2
=\int_0^Lww_t\,dx
=\kappa\int_0^Lww_{xx}\,dx
=-\kappa\int_0^Lw_x^2\,dx\leq0.
$$

The omitted boundary term is $\kappa[ww_x]_0^L$, and it vanishes because $w=0$ at both endpoints. Integrating the inequality gives

$$
\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|u(t)-v(t)\|_2
\leq\|f-g\|_2.
$$

This proves continuous dependence in the indicated norms. If $f=g$, the right side is zero. Hence $u=v$, proving uniqueness in this smooth class. The argument establishes these two properties without an explicit formula.

## Worked existence in a specified class

Let the initial data belong to the finite-dimensional space spanned by $\sin(\pi x/L),\ldots,\sin(N\pi x/L)$, with fixed $N$. Write

$$
f(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}b_n\sin(n\pi x/L).
$$

Then the finite sum

$$
u(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}b_ne^{-\kappa(n\pi/L)^2t}\sin(n\pi x/L)
$$

is smooth and directly satisfies the equation, endpoints, and initial value. This proves existence for every datum in that specified data space. Combined with the previous uniqueness and stability estimate, it gives a complete well-posedness result for this class.

For example, with $L=\pi$, $\kappa=1$, and $f=2\sin x$, changing the data to $g=(2+\eta)\sin x$ changes the solution by $\eta e^{-t}\sin x$. Since $\int_0^\pi\sin^2x\,dx=\pi/2$, its norm is $|\eta|e^{-t}\sqrt{\pi/2}$, which never exceeds the initial difference. Existence for arbitrary rough data requires a suitable weak or semigroup solution concept; the finite-sum proof alone does not establish that broader theorem.

## Failure of continuous dependence

Reverse the sign and consider $u_t=-u_{xx}$ on $(0,\pi)$ with zero endpoint values. Fix $T>0$. The initial data

$$
f_n(x)=e^{-n^2T}\sin(nx)
$$

have norm $e^{-n^2T}\sqrt{\pi/2}\to0$. Each has a smooth solution

$$
u_n(x,t)=e^{n^2(t-T)}\sin(nx).
$$

But $\|u_n(T)\|_2=\sqrt{\pi/2}$ for every $n$. Therefore the solution map is not continuous from the initial $L^2$ norm to the terminal $L^2$ norm on this family. Each individual formula exists and can be checked. That does not make this reversed problem well posed.

## Practice

1. Does the problem $u''=0$ on $(0,1)$ with $u'(0)=u'(1)=0$ have uniqueness? What extra normalization repairs it?
2. In the forward heat estimate, an initial error has norm at most $0.01$. What bound follows at time $T$? Does the estimate prove existence?

## Worked answers

1. Every constant $u=C$ solves it, so uniqueness fails. Prescribe the mean, for example $\int_0^1u\,dx=0$. The only constant satisfying that normalization is $C=0$.
2. The estimate gives $\|u(T)-v(T)\|_2\leq0.01$. It assumes two solutions already exist, so it does not prove existence. A separate construction or existence theorem is required.
