title: "Superposition of solutions"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Constant linear combinations preserve a linear homogeneous equation; prescribed sources and nonzero data require separate accounting.

## Why linearity matters

Let $L$ be a linear differential operator on functions defined on the same domain. If $L[u_1]=0$ and $L[u_2]=0$, then for constants $a,b$,

$$
L[au_1+bu_2]=aL[u_1]+bL[u_2]=0.
$$

This is the superposition principle. The coefficients must be constant with respect to the independent variables. For example, the derivative of $a(t)u$ contains $a'(t)u$, so a varying coefficient introduces additional terms.

The homogeneous solutions form a vector space: they include zero and are closed under addition and constant scalar multiplication. If the associated boundary and initial conditions are also linear and homogeneous, those conditions are preserved. For instance, $u_1(0,t)=u_2(0,t)=0$ implies $(au_1+bu_2)(0,t)=0$.

For nonzero conditions, calculate the resulting data. If both functions equal $1$ at an endpoint, their sum equals $2$ there. A sum may satisfy the PDE while failing the intended boundary-value problem. This distinction is essential in [[15.09 Checks]].

## Worked finite superposition

On $0<x<\pi$, $t>0$, the functions

$$
u_1=e^{-t}\sin x,\qquad u_2=e^{-4t}\sin(2x)
$$

solve $u_t-u_{xx}=0$ and have zero endpoint values. For the second function,

$$
(u_2)_t=-4e^{-4t}\sin(2x),\qquad
(u_2)_{xx}=-4e^{-4t}\sin(2x).
$$

The derivatives match; the corresponding calculation for $u_1$ has factor $-1$. Therefore

$$
u=2u_1-3u_2
=2e^{-t}\sin x-3e^{-4t}\sin(2x)
$$

solves the same homogeneous equation and zero endpoint conditions. At $t=0$, it gives $2\sin x-3\sin(2x)$. Selecting the constants therefore matches a specified initial profile when that profile is a finite combination of the chosen modes.

## A prescribed source changes the solution set

Suppose $L[u]=f$ with fixed, nonzero $f$. If $L[u_1]=L[u_2]=f$, then

$$
L[au_1+bu_2]=(a+b)f.
$$

The same source is preserved only when $a+b=1$. In particular, the sum has source $2f$. On the other hand, $L[u_1-u_2]=0$. Differences between solutions of one inhomogeneous equation belong to the homogeneous solution space.

Choose one particular solution $u_p$ of $L[u_p]=f$. Then every other solution has the form

$$
u=u_p+v,\qquad L[v]=0.
$$

This follows by setting $v=u-u_p$. Boundary data must also be adjusted: if $u$ must equal $g$ on a boundary, then $v$ must equal $g-u_p$ there. For example, $u_p=t$ satisfies $u_t-u_{xx}=1$. Adding $e^{-t}\sin x$ preserves the source $1$, but its endpoint values become $t$, not zero.

## A nonlinear counterexample

Consider $u_t+uu_x=0$ on $x\in\mathbb R$, $t>0$. Both $u_1=1$ and $u_2=x/(1+t)$ solve it. For $u_2$,

$$
(u_2)_t=-\frac{x}{(1+t)^2},\qquad
u_2(u_2)_x=\frac{x}{(1+t)^2}.
$$

Their sum $w=1+x/(1+t)$ instead has

$$
w_t+ww_x=-\frac{x}{(1+t)^2}
+\left(1+\frac{x}{1+t}\right)\frac1{1+t}
=\frac1{1+t}\ne0.
$$

The product of the unknown and its derivative creates an extra term. Some special combinations of nonlinear solutions can work, but no general superposition rule follows.

## Infinite sums need convergence

A finite sum permits termwise differentiation directly. For an infinite sum, convergence of the functions alone does not justify differentiating the series. One sufficient classical condition is uniform convergence of the relevant derivative series on compact interior sets, together with convergence of the function series. Heat-mode exponential factors often supply these bounds for $t\geq\varepsilon>0$. Initial values still need a separate limit argument; [[15.07 Orthogonality and coefficients]] distinguishes pointwise and mean-square reconstruction.

## Practice

1. If $L[u]=x$ and $L[v]=x$, find $L[3u-2v]$ and $L[u+v]$.
2. Show that $w=t e^{-t}\sin x$ does not solve $w_t=w_{xx}$, even though $e^{-t}\sin x$ does.

## Worked answers

1. Linearity gives $L[3u-2v]=3x-2x=x$. The coefficients sum to one, so the source is preserved. For the second expression, $L[u+v]=2x$, which is a different source.
2. Product differentiation gives $w_t=(1-t)e^{-t}\sin x$, whereas $w_{xx}=-te^{-t}\sin x$. Their difference is $e^{-t}\sin x$, not zero. Multiplication by the variable coefficient $t$ does not preserve the solution.
