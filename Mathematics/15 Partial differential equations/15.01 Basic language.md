> [!TLDR]
> Classify a partial differential equation by its independent variables, highest derivative, linearity, and prescribed right-hand side.

## Unknown functions and domains

A partial differential equation (PDE) relates an unknown function to its partial derivatives. For example, let $u=u(x,t)$ be a real-valued function on the strip $0<x<1$, $t>0$. The symbols $x$ and $t$ are independent variables. The value $u(x,t)$ is the dependent variable. Here $t$ is an evolution parameter; no physical meaning or unit is required.

Write $u_x=\partial u/\partial x$ and $u_{xt}=\partial^2u/(\partial t\partial x)$. A classical solution has the derivatives required by the equation and satisfies it at every interior point. Boundary values require additional regularity at the boundary. These requirements are part of the problem, not consequences of notation.

An ordinary differential equation has one independent variable. A PDE has at least two. Thus $u_t=u_{xx}$ is a PDE even though each derivative differentiates in only one variable at a time. See [[Multivariable calculus - Overview]] for partial differentiation.

## Order and linearity

The **order** is the highest total order of a derivative that occurs. In $u_{xxt}+u_y=0$, the derivative $u_{xxt}$ has total order three. Powers of derivatives do not increase order: $(u_x)^2+u_y=0$ has order one.

A linear differential operator in two variables has the form

$$
L[u]=\sum_{i+j\leq m}a_{ij}(x,y)\frac{\partial^{i+j}u}{\partial x^i\partial y^j}.
$$

The nonnegative integers $i,j$ count derivatives, and the coefficient functions $a_{ij}$ are supplied in advance. A derivative of order zero means $u$ itself. Linearity means

$$
L[\alpha u+\beta v]=\alpha L[u]+\beta L[v]
$$

for constants $\alpha,\beta$ and suitable functions $u,v$. Coefficients may depend on independent variables. They cannot depend on the unknown function in a linear equation. Therefore $xu_x+y^2u_{yy}+\sin(x)u=0$ is linear, but $uu_x+u_y=0$, $(u_x)^2=u_y$, and $\sin(u)=u_{xx}$ are nonlinear.

When classifying an equation, first put every term involving the unknown on the left. The equation $u_x=3u+y$ becomes $u_x-3u=y$. It is linear even though its original right-hand side contains $u$.

## Homogeneous and inhomogeneous equations

For a linear equation written as $L[u]=f$, the function $f$ is prescribed data. The equation is **homogeneous** if $f=0$ throughout the domain. Otherwise it is **inhomogeneous**. Thus $u_{xx}+u_{yy}=0$ is homogeneous, whereas $u_{xx}+u_{yy}=x$ is inhomogeneous.

This terminology concerns the equation, not boundary data. A homogeneous PDE can have nonzero boundary values. The word “homogeneous” also has a different meaning for functions that obey a scaling identity, such as $F(cx,cy)=c^kF(x,y)$. Do not use that scaling definition to classify the right-hand side of a linear PDE.

## Worked classification and verification

Consider

$$
u_t-2u_{xx}+xu=xt+1,
\qquad x\in\mathbb R,\quad t>0.
$$

There are two independent variables. The highest derivative is $u_{xx}$, so the order is two. The coefficient $x$ multiplying $u$ is prescribed, so the equation is linear. Its right-hand side is not identically zero, so it is inhomogeneous.

Test the candidate $u(x,t)=t$. Its derivatives are $u_t=1$ and $u_{xx}=0$. Substitution gives

$$
u_t-2u_{xx}+xu=1-0+xt=xt+1.
$$

Thus it satisfies the PDE. It solves an initial-value problem only if that problem also prescribes $u(x,0)=0$. Further boundary conditions could exclude it; [[15.09 Checks]] treats the complete verification.

## Practice

1. Classify $u_{xx}+2u_{xy}+u_{yy}+yu_x=\cos x$ by order, linearity, and homogeneity.
2. Is $u_xu_y=0$ linear? Check whether the sum of the solutions $u=x$ and $v=y$ is a solution.

## Worked answers

1. Each of $u_{xx},u_{xy},u_{yy}$ has total order two, so the equation has order two. All terms containing $u$ or its derivatives enter to the first power with prescribed coefficients. It is linear. Since $\cos x$ is not identically zero, it is inhomogeneous.
2. The product of two derivatives makes the equation nonlinear. For $u=x$, the product is $1\cdot0=0$. For $v=y$, it is $0\cdot1=0$. Their sum $w=x+y$ has $w_xw_y=1\cdot1=1$, so it fails the equation. This calculation explains why [[15.06 Superposition]] requires linearity.
