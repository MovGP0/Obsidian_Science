---
title: "The Einstein tensor and its divergence"
---

> [!TLDR]
> The Einstein tensor is Ricci curvature minus half its metric trace, and its covariant divergence vanishes by the differential Bianchi identity.

## A particular combination of curvature traces

Let $g$ be a smooth Riemannian metric on an $n$-dimensional manifold. All derivatives below use its Levi-Civita connection, so $\nabla g=0$ and torsion is zero. Use

$$
R(\partial_i,\partial_j)\partial_b=R^a{}_{bij}\partial_a,
\quad \operatorname{Ric}_{bj}=R^a{}_{baj},
\quad S=g^{bj}\operatorname{Ric}_{bj},
$$

where the curvature operator is $R(X,Y)=\nabla_X\nabla_Y-\nabla_Y\nabla_X-\nabla_{[X,Y]}$. Define the Einstein tensor by

$$
G_{ij}=\operatorname{Ric}_{ij}-\frac12 Sg_{ij}.
$$

It is a symmetric covariant two-tensor. The name identifies a mathematical construction; no extra equation or interpretation is required. Its trace is

$$
g^{ij}G_{ij}=S-\frac n2S=\left(1-\frac n2\right)S.
$$

The factor $1/2$ does not make $G$ trace free in every dimension. That property holds identically only in dimension two, where $G$ itself vanishes.

## Derive the contracted Bianchi identity

Lower the output index of curvature, $R_{abij}=g_{ac}R^c{}_{bij}$. The differential Bianchi identity is

$$
\nabla_kR_{abij}+\nabla_iR_{abjk}+\nabla_jR_{abki}=0.
$$

Contract with $g^{ak}$. Covariant differentiation commutes with this contraction because $\nabla g^{-1}=0$. The two algebraic contractions are

$$
g^{ak}R_{abjk}=-\operatorname{Ric}_{bj},\qquad
g^{ak}R_{abki}=\operatorname{Ric}_{bi}.
$$

The minus sign in the first result comes from exchanging the last two curvature slots. Therefore

$$
\nabla^aR_{abij}=\nabla_i\operatorname{Ric}_{bj}
-\nabla_j\operatorname{Ric}_{bi}.
$$

Pair interchange, $R_{abij}=R_{ijab}$, gives the equivalent identity

$$
\nabla^iR_{abij}=\nabla_a\operatorname{Ric}_{bj}
-\nabla_b\operatorname{Ric}_{aj}.
$$

Now contract with $g^{aj}$. On the left, $g^{aj}R_{abij}=-\operatorname{Ric}_{bi}$. On the right, symmetry of Ricci turns the first term into its divergence, and the second term is the derivative of its trace. Hence

$$
-\nabla^i\operatorname{Ric}_{bi}
=\nabla^j\operatorname{Ric}_{bj}-\nabla_bS,
\qquad
\nabla^i\operatorname{Ric}_{ij}=\frac12\nabla_jS.
$$

This two-stage contraction explains both the sign and the factor two. Taking the divergence of $G$ now gives

$$
\nabla^iG_{ij}
=\frac12\nabla_jS-\frac12\nabla_jS=0.
$$

The identity is valid for every smooth metric of the stated type. It is not an additional restriction imposed on a selected metric.

## Worked example: constant sectional curvature

If all sectional curvatures equal a constant $K$, then $\operatorname{Ric}=(n-1)Kg$ and $S=n(n-1)K$. Substitute these into the definition:

$$
G=\left((n-1)-\frac12n(n-1)\right)Kg
=-\frac{(n-1)(n-2)}2Kg.
$$

For a round three-sphere of radius $a$, $K=1/a^2$, so $G=-g/a^2$. This nonzero tensor has zero covariant divergence because its coefficient is constant and $\nabla g=0$.

For any Riemannian surface, even one with varying curvature $K(p)$, $\operatorname{Ric}=Kg$ and $S=2K$. Thus $G=0$ pointwise. A zero Einstein tensor on a surface does not imply a flat metric. The unit two-sphere is an immediate counterexample.

## Limits and common errors

The divergence uses $\nabla^i=g^{ik}\nabla_k$. Replacing it by the sum of ordinary partial derivatives of covariant components generally omits connection terms. Also $G+cg$ has zero divergence for any constant $c$, since the metric is parallel. If $c$ is a function, its divergence is $\partial_jc$ and need not vanish. These statements follow from tensor differentiation alone.

## Practice

1. On a four-dimensional metric with constant sectional curvature $K=-2$, compute $\operatorname{Ric}$, $S$, $G$, and the trace of $G$.
2. Let $H_{ij}=\operatorname{Ric}_{ij}-\alpha Sg_{ij}$ for a constant $\alpha$. Find its divergence. When does the Bianchi identity make it zero for every metric?

## Worked answers

1. Ricci is $3(-2)g=-6g$, scalar curvature is $4\cdot3(-2)=-24$, and $G=-6g+12g=6g$. Its trace is $24$, in agreement with $(1-4/2)S=-S=24$.
2. Metric compatibility gives $\nabla^iH_{ij}=(1/2-\alpha)\nabla_jS$. Choosing $\alpha=1/2$ cancels this for every metric. Other choices can also give zero for a particular metric with constant scalar curvature, but that special case does not establish the universal identity.
