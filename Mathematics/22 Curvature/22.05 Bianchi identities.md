> [!TLDR]
> The Bianchi identities constrain both the algebraic curvature components and their covariant derivatives; their contractions give the divergence identity for the Einstein tensor.

## Two different cyclic identities

Let $\nabla$ be a Levi-Civita connection. Define $R(X,Y)Z=\nabla_X\nabla_YZ-\nabla_Y\nabla_XZ-\nabla_{[X,Y]}Z$ and $R(e_i,e_j)e_b=R^a{}_{bij}e_a$. Repeated upper and lower indices are summed. The algebraic, or first, Bianchi identity is

$$
R(X,Y)Z+R(Y,Z)X+R(Z,X)Y=0.
$$

In our component order it becomes

$$
R^a{}_{bij}+R^a{}_{ijb}+R^a{}_{jbi}=0.
$$

This is an equality at each point. It does not involve derivatives of $R$. Torsion freedom is essential for this form; a general connection has additional terms involving its torsion and the derivative of torsion.

The differential, or second, Bianchi identity is

$$
(\nabla_XR)(Y,Z)+(\nabla_YR)(Z,X)+(\nabla_ZR)(X,Y)=0.
$$

Each term here is an endomorphism acting on a further vector. Its component form is

$$
\nabla_k R^a{}_{bij}+\nabla_i R^a{}_{bjk}
+\nabla_j R^a{}_{bki}=0.
$$

These are covariant derivatives of a tensor with four indices. Ordinary partial derivatives cannot replace them in arbitrary coordinates.

## Why the algebraic identity holds

At any point choose normal coordinates. The connection coefficients vanish there, and their lower indices are symmetric. Thus

$$
R^a{}_{bij}=\partial_i\Gamma^a{}_{jb}-\partial_j\Gamma^a{}_{ib}
$$

at that point. Add the cyclic versions in $b,i,j$. For example, $\partial_i\Gamma^a{}_{jb}$ cancels $-\partial_i\Gamma^a{}_{bj}$ because torsion freedom gives $\Gamma^a{}_{jb}=\Gamma^a{}_{bj}$. The remaining terms cancel in the same way. The resulting tensor identity is valid in every chart.

For the differential identity, normal coordinates again simplify the proof at the chosen point. There $\nabla R=\partial R$. In a derivative of the curvature formula, each derivative of a product $\Gamma\Gamma$ has a factor $\Gamma$, so it vanishes at that point. The cyclic sum becomes paired mixed second derivatives of $\Gamma$. These cancel because partial derivatives commute. This proves the identity for a smooth Levi-Civita connection without treating curvature components as constants.

## Worked example: a varying constant-curvature form

Suppose an $n$-dimensional Riemannian manifold, $n\geq3$, has

$$
R_{abij}=k(p)(g_{ai}g_{bj}-g_{aj}g_{bi})
$$

at each point, where $k$ may initially depend on the point $p$. This says that all tangent two-planes at a given point have the same curvature. Contraction gives

$$
\operatorname{Ric}_{bj}=(n-1)k g_{bj},\qquad S=n(n-1)k.
$$

Contracting the differential Bianchi identity yields

$$
\nabla^b\operatorname{Ric}_{bj}=\frac12\nabla_j S.
$$

Metric compatibility turns this into

$$
(n-1)\partial_j k=\frac{n(n-1)}2\partial_j k,
\qquad (n-1)(n-2)\partial_j k=0.
$$

Because $n\geq3$, every derivative of $k$ is zero. Hence $k$ is constant on each connected component. This is the local calculation behind Schur's theorem. In dimension two the factor $n-2$ vanishes, and surface curvature can vary from point to point. The dimensional condition is part of the theorem.

## What the identities constrain

The algebraic identity rules out many arrays that have only the two antisymmetries. The differential identity further limits how valid curvature tensors can vary through space. The latter does not follow merely by differentiating a single sectional-curvature value. It relates a cyclic set of directional derivatives of the full tensor. The contraction used above is derived in [[22.07 The Einstein tensor]].

## Practice

1. If $X,Y,Z$ are tangent vectors and $R(X,Y)Z=u$, $R(Y,Z)X=v$, determine the third cyclic term.
2. Repeat the worked calculation when $n=2$. Explain what it proves about a smooth function $k$ on a surface.

## Worked answers

1. The algebraic Bianchi identity gives $u+v+R(Z,X)Y=0$. Thus $R(Z,X)Y=-u-v$. It does not require either known term to vanish separately.
2. For $n=2$, $\operatorname{Ric}=kg$ and $S=2k$. The contracted identity reads $\partial_j k=\tfrac12\partial_j(2k)$, which is an equality for every differentiable $k$. It imposes no constancy condition. One cannot divide by $n-2$ in this dimension.
