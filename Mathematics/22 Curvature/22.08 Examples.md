> [!TLDR]
> Polar coordinates give nonzero connection coefficients on a flat plane, while the same calculation on a sphere gives nonzero curvature.

## Geometry versus coordinate effects

Curvature must distinguish a change in coordinates from a change in intrinsic geometry. Compare two Riemannian metrics using their Levi-Civita connections. Adopt

$$
R(\partial_i,\partial_j)\partial_b=R^a{}_{bij}\partial_a,
\quad
R^a{}_{bij}=\partial_i\Gamma^a{}_{jb}-\partial_j\Gamma^a{}_{ib}
+\Gamma^a{}_{ic}\Gamma^c{}_{jb}-\Gamma^a{}_{jc}\Gamma^c{}_{ib}.
$$

This fixes the sign used below. The lower-index order of $\Gamma^a{}_{ib}$ is derivative direction, then input vector. For Levi-Civita connections these lower indices are symmetric in a coordinate basis.

## Worked example: the Euclidean plane in polar coordinates

Write $x=r\cos\phi$ and $y=r\sin\phi$, with $r>0$ and a local angular interval. Differentiation gives

$$
dx=\cos\phi\,dr-r\sin\phi\,d\phi,\qquad
dy=\sin\phi\,dr+r\cos\phi\,d\phi.
$$

Squaring and adding cancels the cross terms. Thus the Euclidean metric becomes

$$
dx^2+dy^2=dr^2+r^2d\phi^2.
$$

Its nonzero connection coefficients, computed from the metric formula, are

$$
\Gamma^r{}_{\phi\phi}=-r,\qquad
\Gamma^\phi{}_{r\phi}=\Gamma^\phi{}_{\phi r}=1/r.
$$

For example, $\Gamma^r{}_{\phi\phi}=-\tfrac12\partial_r(r^2)=-r$, while $\Gamma^\phi{}_{r\phi}=\tfrac12r^{-2}\partial_r(r^2)=1/r$. Evaluate

$$
\begin{aligned}
R^r{}_{\phi r\phi}
&=\partial_r(-r)-\Gamma^r{}_{\phi\phi}\Gamma^\phi{}_{r\phi}\\
&=-1-(-r)(1/r)=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

The other independent surface curvature component is determined by this one, so the full curvature tensor is zero. Connection coefficients record the turning and scaling of the coordinate basis. The product term cancels their derivative effect on this flat metric.

## A curved surface: the round sphere

For a sphere of radius $a>0$, the metric in colatitude $\theta$ and longitude $\phi$ is

$$
g=a^2(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2),\qquad 0<\theta<\pi.
$$

Its coefficients are $\Gamma^\theta{}_{\phi\phi}=-\sin\theta\cos\theta$ and $\Gamma^\phi{}_{\theta\phi}=\cot\theta$, together with the lower-index symmetric partner. Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
R^\theta{}_{\phi\theta\phi}
&=\partial_\theta(-\sin\theta\cos\theta)
-(-\sin\theta\cos\theta)\cot\theta\\
&=\sin^2\theta-\cos^2\theta+\cos^2\theta
=\sin^2\theta.
\end{aligned}
$$

Lowering the output index yields $R_{\theta\phi\theta\phi}=a^2\sin^2\theta$. The coordinate parallelogram has squared area $a^4\sin^2\theta$. Thus

$$
K=\frac{R_{\theta\phi\theta\phi}}{g_{\theta\theta}g_{\phi\phi}-g_{\theta\phi}^2}
=\frac1{a^2}.
$$

The nonzero result cannot be removed by a smooth coordinate change. At the poles the chart is singular, but the value $K=1/a^2$ extends smoothly. A divergent coefficient in a failed chart is not a geometric singularity.

## A useful general surface metric

Both calculations fit $g=du^2+f(u)^2dv^2$, with $f>0$. Here $\Gamma^u{}_{vv}=-ff'$ and $\Gamma^v{}_{uv}=f'/f$. Substitution gives

$$
R^u{}_{vuv}=-(f'^2+ff'')+f'^2=-ff'',
\qquad K=-\frac{f''}{f}.
$$

The polar plane has $f(u)=u$. A sphere uses arc distance $u=a\theta$ and $f(u)=a\sin(u/a)$. This formula concerns intrinsic curvature. A cylinder can bend in three-dimensional space while its intrinsic metric remains locally flat.

## Practice

1. Find $K$ for $g=du^2+\sinh^2u\,dv^2$ on $u>0$.
2. A cylinder of radius $a$ has metric $dz^2+a^2d\phi^2$. Show directly that it is locally flat and explain why the angular periodicity does not change that local conclusion.

## Worked answers

1. With $f=\sinh u$, one has $f''=\sinh u=f$. Hence $K=-f''/f=-1$. The restriction $u>0$ keeps $f>0$ in this chart. This gives a constant negative-curvature surface metric.
2. Set $s=a\phi$ on a local angular interval. Then $g=dz^2+ds^2$, whose coefficients are constant, so all Christoffel coefficients and curvature components vanish there. Globally $s$ is periodic, so this local coordinate map does not identify the whole cylinder with a plane. Local flatness does not determine global topology.
