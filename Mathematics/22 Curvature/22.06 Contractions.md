---
title: "Ricci and scalar curvature as contractions"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Ricci curvature traces the curvature operator, and scalar curvature takes a further trace with the inverse metric, so both discard some directional information.

## Trace an operator, not an index pattern

Let $(M,g)$ be an $n$-dimensional Riemannian manifold with Levi-Civita connection. Use $R(X,Y)Z=\nabla_X\nabla_YZ-\nabla_Y\nabla_XZ-\nabla_{[X,Y]}Z$ and $R(\partial_i,\partial_j)\partial_b=R^a{}_{bij}\partial_a$.

For fixed tangent vectors $Y,Z$, the map $X\mapsto R(X,Y)Z$ is an endomorphism. Its trace defines

$$
\operatorname{Ric}(Z,Y)=\operatorname{tr}\bigl(X\mapsto R(X,Y)Z\bigr).
$$

Set $Z=\partial_b$ and $Y=\partial_j$. Tracing output $a$ against input direction $i$ gives

$$
\operatorname{Ric}_{bj}=R^a{}_{baj}.
$$

This trace already makes sense for an affine connection without a metric. Once a metric is available, lower the output index to obtain $R_{abij}=g_{ac}R^c{}_{bij}$. Recover the trace with the inverse metric:

$$
\operatorname{Ric}_{bj}=g^{ai}R_{abij}.
$$

The inverse metric is defined by $g^{ai}g_{ic}=\delta^a{}_c$. Contracting two lower indices with a Kronecker delta in a general coordinate basis is incorrect; one must use $g^{ai}$. For the Levi-Civita connection, curvature symmetries imply that $\operatorname{Ric}_{bj}=\operatorname{Ric}_{jb}$.

## The second trace and its meaning

Define scalar curvature by

$$
S=g^{bj}\operatorname{Ric}_{bj}
=g^{bj}g^{ai}R_{abij}.
$$

The symbol $S$ distinguishes scalar curvature from the full tensor $R$. In an orthonormal frame $e_1,\ldots,e_n$, the inverse metric is the identity matrix. If $K_{ij}$ is the sectional curvature of the plane spanned by $e_i,e_j$, then

$$
\operatorname{Ric}(e_j,e_j)=\sum_{i\ne j}K_{ij},
\qquad S=2\sum_{i<j}K_{ij}.
$$

Each two-plane occurs twice in the full double sum, which explains the factor two. For a unit vector $v$, complete $v$ to an orthonormal frame. Then $\operatorname{Ric}(v,v)$ sums the sectional curvatures of the $n-1$ frame planes containing $v$. It is a sum, rather than one individual plane curvature.

## Worked example: a sphere in nonorthonormal coordinates

On the sphere of radius $a$, use $g_{\theta\theta}=a^2$, $g_{\phi\phi}=a^2\sin^2\theta$, and $g_{\theta\phi}=0$. Its curvature is

$$
R_{abij}=\frac1{a^2}(g_{ai}g_{bj}-g_{aj}g_{bi}).
$$

Contract the first and third slots:

$$
\begin{aligned}
g^{ai}R_{abij}
&=\frac1{a^2}\left(g^{ai}g_{ai}g_{bj}
-g^{ai}g_{aj}g_{bi}\right)\\
&=\frac1{a^2}(2g_{bj}-g_{bj})
=\frac1{a^2}g_{bj}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The first simplification uses $g^{ai}g_{ai}=2$, the dimension of the surface. The second uses $g^{ai}g_{aj}=\delta^i{}_j$. Hence

$$
\operatorname{Ric}_{\theta\theta}=1,\qquad
\operatorname{Ric}_{\phi\phi}=\sin^2\theta,\qquad
S=\frac1{a^2}+\frac{\sin^2\theta}{a^2\sin^2\theta}
=\frac2{a^2}.
$$

Simply adding the two displayed Ricci components would give $1+\sin^2\theta$, which is not scalar curvature. A coordinate trace requires the inverse metric.

## What a contraction cannot recover

In two dimensions the full tensor is determined by $K=S/2$. In three dimensions Ricci determines the Riemann tensor, although a single scalar still does not. In higher dimensions different curvature tensors can have the same Ricci tensor. These statements concern the full algebraic tensor, rather than only a few chosen components. The explicit examples in [[22.09 Local flatness and information loss]] show why vanishing traces need not imply flatness.

## Practice

1. In dimension $n$, contract $R_{abij}=K(g_{ai}g_{bj}-g_{aj}g_{bi})$ to find Ricci and scalar curvature.
2. At a point let $g=\operatorname{diag}(4,9)$ and $\operatorname{Ric}=\operatorname{diag}(8,18)$ in the same coordinates. Find $S$ and compare with the ordinary matrix trace.

## Worked answers

1. Since $g^{ai}g_{ai}=n$ and $g^{ai}g_{aj}g_{bi}=g_{bj}$, the first contraction gives $\operatorname{Ric}=(n-1)Kg$. A second contraction gives $S=n(n-1)K$.
2. The data satisfy $\operatorname{Ric}=2g$, so they describe surface curvature $K=2$ at the point. The inverse metric is $\operatorname{diag}(1/4,1/9)$. Thus $S=(1/4)8+(1/9)18=2+2=4$, as required by $S=2K$. The matrix trace is $26$, but it is not the metric trace of this covariant tensor. Raising one index first produces the endomorphism $g^{-1}\operatorname{Ric}=\operatorname{diag}(2,2)$, whose ordinary trace is $4$.
