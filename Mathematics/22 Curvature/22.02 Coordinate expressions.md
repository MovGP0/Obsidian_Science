> [!TLDR]
> The coordinate curvature tensor contains derivatives of the connection coefficients and products that correct for the changing vector basis.

## Fix the indices before calculating

Let $x^1,\ldots,x^n$ be local coordinates, with coordinate basis $e_i=\partial/\partial x^i$. Repeated upper and lower indices are summed from $1$ to $n$. Define the connection coefficients by

$$
\nabla_{e_i}e_b=\Gamma^a{}_{ib}e_a.
$$

The first lower index is the derivative direction. The last lower index identifies the vector being differentiated. A torsion-free connection satisfies $\Gamma^a{}_{ib}=\Gamma^a{}_{bi}$ in this coordinate basis. The calculation below does not require that symmetry.

Use

$$
R(X,Y)Z=\nabla_X\nabla_YZ-\nabla_Y\nabla_XZ-\nabla_{[X,Y]}Z,
\qquad
R(e_i,e_j)e_b=R^a{}_{bij}e_a.
$$

Coordinate fields commute, so their bracket term is zero. For a noncoordinate frame this simplification does not apply.

## Derive the component formula

First differentiate $e_b$ in direction $e_j$. Differentiate the result in direction $e_i$, using the product rule on both the coefficient and the basis:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{e_i}\nabla_{e_j}e_b
&=\nabla_{e_i}(\Gamma^c{}_{jb}e_c)\\
&=(\partial_i\Gamma^a{}_{jb})e_a
+\Gamma^c{}_{jb}\Gamma^a{}_{ic}e_a.
\end{aligned}
$$

Interchange $i,j$ and subtract. The coefficient of $e_a$ is

$$
R^a{}_{bij}
=\partial_i\Gamma^a{}_{jb}-\partial_j\Gamma^a{}_{ib}
+\Gamma^a{}_{ic}\Gamma^c{}_{jb}
-\Gamma^a{}_{jc}\Gamma^c{}_{ib}.
$$

The derivative terms measure variation of the connection. The product terms occur because the basis produced by the inner derivative must also be differentiated. Dropping them can give false curvature even for the Euclidean plane in polar coordinates.

For an arbitrary field $V=V^be_b$, a complete expansion cancels all derivatives of $V$ in the commutator, leaving

$$
(\nabla_i\nabla_j-\nabla_j\nabla_i)V^a
=R^a{}_{bij}V^b.
$$

Here $\nabla_i$ denotes the directional operator $\nabla_{e_i}$ on vector fields. If instead one takes the antisymmetric part of the tensorial covariant Hessian, a torsion term must be included for a connection with torsion. Keep these two meanings distinct.

## Worked example: the round sphere

Take a sphere of radius $a>0$, with coordinates $0<\theta<\pi$ and local longitude $\phi$. Its metric is

$$
g=a^2\,d\theta^2+a^2\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2.
$$

The Levi-Civita coefficients follow from

$$
\Gamma^a{}_{ib}=\frac12 g^{ac}
(\partial_i g_{bc}+\partial_b g_{ic}-\partial_c g_{ib}).
$$

Since the only varying metric component is $g_{\phi\phi}$, the nonzero coefficients are

$$
\Gamma^\theta{}_{\phi\phi}=-\sin\theta\cos\theta,
\qquad
\Gamma^\phi{}_{\theta\phi}=\Gamma^\phi{}_{\phi\theta}=\cot\theta.
$$

Calculate one component without skipping the product term:

$$
\begin{aligned}
R^\theta{}_{\phi\theta\phi}
&=\partial_\theta(-\sin\theta\cos\theta)
-\Gamma^\theta{}_{\phi\phi}\Gamma^\phi{}_{\theta\phi}\\
&=(\sin^2\theta-\cos^2\theta)+\cos^2\theta\\
&=\sin^2\theta.
\end{aligned}
$$

Lower the first index with $g_{ab}$ to obtain $R_{\theta\phi\theta\phi}=a^2\sin^2\theta$. Divide by the squared coordinate area $g_{\theta\theta}g_{\phi\phi}-g_{\theta\phi}^2=a^4\sin^2\theta$. The sectional curvature is therefore $K=1/a^2$. The coordinate component depends on $\theta$, but the normalized geometric curvature does not.

The coefficients become singular at the poles because this chart fails there. The sphere and its curvature remain smooth. See [[22.08 Examples]] for a flat comparison.

## Practice

1. Use antisymmetry to find $R^\theta{}_{\phi\phi\theta}$ on the sphere. Explain the source of the sign.
2. Suppose all connection coefficients vanish identically on a coordinate domain. What is its curvature? Does vanishing at only one point give the same conclusion?

## Worked answers

1. Interchanging the last two indices interchanges the derivative directions. Thus $R^\theta{}_{\phi\phi\theta}=-R^\theta{}_{\phi\theta\phi}=-\sin^2\theta$. This is a curvature symmetry, not a symmetry of the connection coefficients.
2. Identical vanishing removes both the derivative and product terms, so $R=0$ on the domain. At one point the products vanish, but $\partial_i\Gamma^a{}_{jb}-\partial_j\Gamma^a{}_{ib}$ can remain nonzero. Normal coordinates on a sphere give exactly this situation, discussed in [[22.09 Local flatness and information loss]].
