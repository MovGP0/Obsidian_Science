---
title: "Coordinate checks for tensors"
---

> [!TLDR]
> A tensor must transform consistently between coordinates; derivatives of varying coordinate factors can create extra terms.

## An array needs a transformation rule

At one fixed basis, every array with the correct shape defines a tensor by its basis expansion. The problem appears when arrays are assigned in several bases independently. To describe one object, these arrays must satisfy the same transformation law. For a covariant two-tensor, if $e'_a=e_iM^i{}_a$, the law is

$$
B'_{ab}=M^i{}_a M^j{}_b B_{ij}.
$$

For example, declare that a matrix is the identity in every basis. In one dimension, change basis from $e$ to $e'=2e$. A bilinear form with $B(e,e)=1$ must have $B(e',e')=4$. Assigning the value $1$ again describes a different form. The symbol $\delta^i{}_j$ for the identity map does remain the same in every basis, because its mixed transformation is $M^{-1}IM=I$. Thus index type matters even for familiar arrays.

## Differentiate a vector transformation

On a smooth coordinate domain, let $x^i$ and $y^a$ be two smooth coordinate systems with an invertible transition. Write

$$
J^a{}_i=\frac{\partial y^a}{\partial x^i},
\qquad K^j{}_b=\frac{\partial x^j}{\partial y^b}.
$$

A vector field has components $v'^a=J^a{}_i v^i$. Apply the chain rule $\partial'_b=K^j{}_b\partial_j$ and the product rule:

$$
\partial'_b v'^a
=K^j{}_b\partial_j(J^a{}_i v^i)
=J^a{}_i K^j{}_b\partial_jv^i
+K^j{}_b(\partial_jJ^a{}_i)v^i.
$$

The first term is the type $(1,1)$ tensor rule. The second term contains second derivatives of the coordinate map. It generally does not vanish. Therefore the ordinary derivatives $\partial_jv^i$ do not generally define a tensor field. They do transform tensorially between affine coordinate systems, where $J$ is constant, but that restricted statement is not a general coordinate test.

## Worked example: a constant vector gets a nonzero derivative

Take the positive real line $x>0$ with new coordinate $y=x^2$. Both coordinates are smooth on this domain, and $dy/dx=2x\ne0$. Let $v=\partial/\partial x$, so $v^x=1$. Its ordinary derivative in the old coordinate is $\partial_xv^x=0$.

In the new coordinate, the vector component is $v^y=(dy/dx)v^x=2x=2\sqrt y$. Differentiate it:

$$
\partial_yv^y=\frac{d}{dy}(2\sqrt y)=\frac1{\sqrt y}=\frac1x.
$$

If these derivatives were components of a type $(1,1)$ tensor, their new value would be $(dy/dx)(dx/dy)0=0$. The actual value is nonzero. The product-rule extra term is exactly $(dx/dy)(d^2y/dx^2)v^x=(1/(2x))(2)(1)=1/x$.

This is a counterexample to the general claim, not a numerical accident. It applies at every point of the chosen domain. The restriction $x>0$ is essential because $x\mapsto x^2$ is not an invertible coordinate map across zero.

## Scalars and connections

For a scalar function $f$, the differential has components $\partial_if$. The chain rule alone gives $\partial'_af=K^i{}_a\partial_if$, so $df$ is a covector. Differentiating again adds an extra term:

$$
\partial'_a\partial'_bf
=K^i{}_aK^j{}_b\partial_i\partial_jf
+K^i{}_a(\partial_iK^j{}_b)\partial_jf.
$$

Thus even the ordinary Hessian is not generally a covariant two-tensor. At a critical point, where $df=0$, this extra term vanishes and the Hessian at that point is well defined as a bilinear form.

A connection supplies correction coefficients $\Gamma^i{}_{jk}$ and defines $\nabla_jv^i=\partial_jv^i+\Gamma^i{}_{jk}v^k$. The coefficients have an inhomogeneous transformation law that cancels the extra term. In the worked example, start with zero connection coefficient in $x$. In $y$, the required coefficient is $\Gamma^y{}_{yy}=-1/(2y)$. Then $\nabla_yv^y=1/\sqrt y-(1/(2y))(2\sqrt y)=0$. The coefficients themselves are not tensor components; their corrected derivative is.

## Practice

1. Let $f(x)=x$ on $x>0$ and use $y=x^2$. Check that $df$ transforms as a covector and that its ordinary second derivative does not follow a covariant two-tensor rule.
2. A covariant two-tensor has matrix $I$ in an old two-dimensional basis. Under $M=\operatorname{diag}(2,1)$, what must its new matrix be? Why is retaining $I$ inconsistent?

> [!example]- Worked answers
> 1. In $x$, $f_x=1$ and $f_{xx}=0$. In $y$, $f=\sqrt y$, so $f_y=1/(2\sqrt y)=(dx/dy)f_x$, as required for a covector. But $f_{yy}=-1/(4y^{3/2})\ne0$, whereas a two-tensor transformation of the old zero second derivative would remain zero.
> 2. The law gives $B'=M^{\mathsf T}IM=\operatorname{diag}(4,1)$. In particular $B(e'_1,e'_1)=B(2e_1,2e_1)=4$. Retaining the value $1$ in that slot violates bilinearity for the same abstract form.
