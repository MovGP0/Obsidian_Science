> [!TLDR]
> A covector is a linear scalar-valued function on a vector space; a dual basis extracts vector coordinates.

## Linear functionals and the natural pairing

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over the real numbers, with dimension $n$. A **linear functional**, or **covector**, is a map $\alpha:V\to\mathbb R$ such that

$$
\alpha(av+bw)=a\alpha(v)+b\alpha(w)
$$

for all $v,w\in V$ and $a,b\in\mathbb R$. The set $V^*$ of all such maps is the **dual space**. Add functions pointwise: $(\alpha+\beta)(v)=\alpha(v)+\beta(v)$. Scalar multiplication is also pointwise. These operations make $V^*$ a vector space.

The **natural pairing** is evaluation, written $\langle\alpha,v\rangle=\alpha(v)$. It is linear in each input separately. It needs no length, angle, or inner product. The order of the arguments matters for their roles: the first is a function, and the second is its input. A row of numbers can represent a covector, but the function itself does not depend on that representation.

For complex spaces, the algebraic dual consists of complex-linear functionals. It does not automatically conjugate coordinates. This article uses real scalars to keep that distinction separate.

## Construct the dual basis

Choose a basis $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ of $V$. Every vector has a unique expansion $v=\sum_i v^i e_i$. Define $\varepsilon^i(v)=v^i$. Uniqueness of coordinates makes this a well-defined linear function. In particular,

$$
\varepsilon^i(e_j)=\delta^i{}_j,
\qquad
\delta^i{}_j=
\begin{cases}1&i=j,\\0&i\ne j.\end{cases}
$$

The functions $\varepsilon^1,\ldots,\varepsilon^n$ form the **dual basis**. To prove that they span, put $\alpha_i=\alpha(e_i)$ and apply linearity:

$$
\alpha(v)=\alpha\!\left(\sum_i v^i e_i\right)
=\sum_i v^i\alpha(e_i)
=\left(\sum_i\alpha_i\varepsilon^i\right)(v).
$$

Thus $\alpha=\sum_i\alpha_i\varepsilon^i$. To prove independence, evaluate $\sum_i a_i\varepsilon^i=0$ on each $e_j$. The result is $a_j=0$. Hence $\dim V^*=n$ and $\alpha(v)=\sum_i\alpha_i v^i$.

## Worked example: coordinates in a slanted basis

In $\mathbb R^2$, take $e_1=(1,0)$ and $e_2=(1,2)$. The equation $(x,y)=a e_1+b e_2$ gives $b=y/2$ and $a=x-y/2$. Therefore

$$
\varepsilon^1(x,y)=x-y/2,
\qquad \varepsilon^2(x,y)=y/2.
$$

For $\alpha(x,y)=2x-y$, its basis coefficients are $\alpha_1=2$ and $\alpha_2=0$, since $\alpha(1,2)=0$. Thus $\alpha=2\varepsilon^1$. The vector $v=(3,4)$ has coordinates $(v^1,v^2)=(1,2)$, so $\alpha(v)=2(1)+0(2)=2$. Direct evaluation gives $6-4=2$ as required.

## Natural identification with the double dual

Each $v\in V$ defines a functional $J(v):V^*\to\mathbb R$ by $J(v)(\alpha)=\alpha(v)$. If $J(v)=0$, all coordinate functions vanish on $v$, so $v=0$. Thus $J$ is injective. Since $V$ and $V^{**}$ have equal finite dimension, $J$ is an isomorphism. This map is canonical: it uses evaluation alone. A map from $V$ to $V^*$ needs extra data, such as the metric in [[18.08 Forms and metrics]].

## Practice

1. In the standard basis of $\mathbb R^3$, find the covector $\alpha$ with $\alpha(e_1)=3$, $\alpha(e_2)=-2$, and $\alpha(e_3)=1$. Evaluate it on $(2,1,-1)$.
2. For $f_1=(2,0)$ and $f_2=(0,3)$, construct the dual basis and evaluate both dual functions on $(6,9)$.

> [!example]- Worked answers
> 1. The dual-basis expansion is $\alpha=3\varepsilon^1-2\varepsilon^2+\varepsilon^3$. Thus $\alpha(x,y,z)=3x-2y+z$, and $\alpha(2,1,-1)=6-2-1=3$.
> 2. Solve $(x,y)=a(2,0)+b(0,3)$ to obtain $a=x/2$ and $b=y/3$. The dual functions are $\eta^1(x,y)=x/2$ and $\eta^2(x,y)=y/3$. Their values on $(6,9)$ are $3$ and $3$, which are the coordinates in this basis.
