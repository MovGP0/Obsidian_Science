> [!TLDR]
> A basis change applies an inverse matrix to each upper component index and the basis matrix to each lower index.

## Fix the basis-change convention

Let $e_i$ and $e'_a$ be bases of the same $n$-dimensional real vector space. Define the invertible matrix $M$ by

$$
e'_a=e_i M^i{}_a.
$$

The columns of $M$ are the new basis vectors expressed in the old basis. This convention must be fixed before any transformation formula is used. Write the same vector as $v=e_i v^i=e'_a v'^a$. Substitute the basis change to obtain $v^i=M^i{}_a v'^a$, hence

$$
v'^a=(M^{-1})^a{}_i v^i.
$$

The dual basis must still extract coordinates. Solving $\varepsilon'^a(e'_b)=\delta^a{}_b$ gives $\varepsilon'^a=(M^{-1})^a{}_i\varepsilon^i$. For a covector, its new components are evaluations:

$$
\alpha'_a=\alpha(e'_a)=M^i{}_a\alpha_i.
$$

These rules preserve evaluation: $\alpha'_a v'^a=\alpha_i M^i{}_a(M^{-1})^a{}_j v^j=\alpha_i v^i$. The cancellation follows from $MM^{-1}=I$. The abstract scalar does not change.

This is a passive change: the basis changes while the vector and covector stay fixed. An active linear map instead changes a vector inside a fixed basis. Both calculations can use matrices, but their meanings differ. Always state what the matrix columns represent.

## General tensors

A type $(r,s)$ tensor has $r$ vector factors and $s$ covector factors. Apply the preceding rule independently in every slot:

$$
T'^{a_1\cdots a_r}{}_{b_1\cdots b_s}
=(M^{-1})^{a_1}{}_{i_1}\cdots(M^{-1})^{a_r}{}_{i_r}
M^{j_1}{}_{b_1}\cdots M^{j_s}{}_{b_s}
T^{i_1\cdots i_r}{}_{j_1\cdots j_s}.
$$

This formula follows either by expanding the tensor basis or by evaluating on the new basis arguments. For a linear map, it reads $A'=M^{-1}AM$. For a bilinear form with matrix $B$, it reads $B'=M^{\mathsf T}BM$. Similarity and congruence are different because these tensors have different types.

## Worked example: a shear basis

In $\mathbb R^2$, set $e'_1=e_1$ and $e'_2=e_1+e_2$. Then

$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad M^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The vector with old coordinates $(3,2)$ has new coordinates $(1,2)$. A covector with old components $(2,-1)$ has new components $(2,1)$ because its component column transforms by $M^{\mathsf T}$. The pairing is $2(3)-2=4$ in the old basis and $2(1)+1(2)=4$ in the new basis.

For the usual Euclidean bilinear form, $B=I$. In the shear basis,

$$
B'=M^{\mathsf T}M=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The squared length of the vector is $3^2+2^2=13$. In new coordinates it is $(1,2)B'(1,2)^{\mathsf T}=1+4+8=13$. The matrix changes because the new basis is not orthonormal. Keeping the identity matrix would describe a different form.

## Coordinate Jacobians

On a smooth manifold, take coordinates $x^i$ and $y^a$ with a smooth invertible transition. At a fixed point, the chain rule gives

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial y^a}
=\frac{\partial x^i}{\partial y^a}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}.
$$

Thus $M^i{}_a=\partial x^i/\partial y^a$ in the tangent basis. Vector components transform as $v'^a=(\partial y^a/\partial x^i)v^i$, and covector components as $\alpha'_a=(\partial x^i/\partial y^a)\alpha_i$. Each general tensor slot uses the corresponding Jacobian factor. These are pointwise linear transformations even when the coordinate map is nonlinear. Derivatives of the varying Jacobian explain the extra terms in [[18.09 Coordinate checks]].

## Practice

1. For $M=\operatorname{diag}(2,3)$, transform $v=(4,6)$ and $\alpha=(1,2)$ and check their pairing.
2. Under the same change, transform the bilinear form $B=\operatorname{diag}(1,2)$.

> [!example]- Worked answers
> 1. The inverse is $\operatorname{diag}(1/2,1/3)$, so $v'=(2,2)$. Covector components are $\alpha'=M^{\mathsf T}\alpha=(2,6)$. The old evaluation is $4+12=16$ and the new one is $4+12=16$.
> 2. Use congruence: $B'=M^{\mathsf T}BM=\operatorname{diag}(2,3)\operatorname{diag}(1,2)\operatorname{diag}(2,3)=\operatorname{diag}(4,18)$. Each lower slot contributes one basis matrix factor.
