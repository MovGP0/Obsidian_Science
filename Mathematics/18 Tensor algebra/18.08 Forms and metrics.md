title: "Bilinear forms and metrics"

> [!TLDR]
> A nondegenerate bilinear form converts vectors to covectors; its inverse performs the reverse conversion.

## Bilinear forms as tensors

Let $V$ be an $n$-dimensional real vector space. A bilinear form is a map $B:V\times V\to\mathbb R$ that is linear separately in each input. In a basis $e_i$ with dual basis $\varepsilon^i$, write $B_{ij}=B(e_i,e_j)$. Then

$$
B=B_{ij}\varepsilon^i\otimes\varepsilon^j,
\qquad B(v,w)=B_{ij}v^i w^j.
$$

It therefore has type $(0,2)$. Every bilinear form defines a linear map $V\to V^*$ by $v\mapsto B(v,\cdot)$. It is **nondegenerate** when the only $v$ for which $B(v,w)=0$ for every $w$ is $v=0$. In finite dimension this is equivalent to an invertible component matrix.

A **metric** here means a symmetric nondegenerate bilinear form $g$. If also $g(v,v)>0$ for every nonzero $v$, it is positive definite and defines an inner product. Nondegeneracy does not imply positive definiteness. For $g=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$, the nonzero vector $(1,1)$ has $g(v,v)=0$, but $g$ is still invertible.

## Lowering and raising indices

Define the map $\flat:V\to V^*$ by $v^\flat(w)=g(v,w)$. Symmetry lets its components be written

$$
v_i=g_{ij}v^j.
$$

Here $v_i$ denotes the components of the new covector, not an alternative placement of the old vector coordinates. Nondegeneracy makes $\flat$ an isomorphism. Its inverse $\sharp:V^*\to V$ has components

$$
\alpha^i=g^{ij}\alpha_j,
\qquad g^{ik}g_{kj}=\delta^i{}_j,
$$

where $(g^{ij})$ is the inverse matrix. Composing the maps gives $(v^\flat)^\sharp=v$ by the matrix-inverse identity. Neither map exists canonically from the vector space alone. A different metric usually gives a different covector for the same vector.

## Worked example: a nonorthonormal metric matrix

Take

$$
G=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix},
\qquad v=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The determinant is $6-1=5$, so the form is nondegenerate. It is positive definite because

$$
2x^2+2xy+3y^2=2(x+y/2)^2+\frac52y^2>0
$$

for every nonzero $(x,y)$. Lowering the vector gives $v^\flat=Gv=(1,-2)^{\mathsf T}$. This is the functional $w\mapsto w^1-2w^2$. Its value on $v$ is $1+2=3$, equal to $g(v,v)$.

The inverse metric is

$$
G^{-1}=\frac15\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Raising the covector recovers $G^{-1}(1,-2)^{\mathsf T}=\frac15(5,-5)^{\mathsf T}=(1,-1)^{\mathsf T}$. A covariant two-tensor can also have one index raised: $A^i{}_j=g^{ik}B_{kj}$. This produces a linear map. Its matrix is $G^{-1}B$, not simply $B$ with a new label.

## Limits and common errors

For a degenerate form, lowering can send a nonzero vector to zero, so there is no inverse metric. For a nonsymmetric nondegenerate form, the maps $B(v,\cdot)$ and $B(\cdot,v)$ can differ; the symmetry assumption removes that ambiguity here. In an orthonormal basis of a positive-definite metric, $G=I$ and lowering preserves the numerical coordinates. This special case can hide the distinction between vectors and covectors. The distinction returns under the basis changes in [[18.05 Transformation laws]].

## Practice

1. For $G=\operatorname{diag}(4,1)$ and $v=(1,2)$, find $v^\flat$, $g(v,v)$, and the vector associated with $\alpha=(8,-3)$.
2. Explain why $B=\operatorname{diag}(1,0)$ cannot identify all covectors with vectors through lowering and raising.

> [!example]- Worked answers
> 1. Lowering gives $(4,2)$ and evaluation gives $4(1)+2(2)=8$. The inverse is $\operatorname{diag}(1/4,1)$, so $\alpha^\sharp=(2,-3)$. Applying $G$ to this vector gives $(8,-3)$ again.
> 2. The nonzero vector $(0,1)$ maps to the zero covector. All image covectors have second component zero, so a covector such as $(0,1)$ is not in the image. The map is neither injective nor surjective, and its zero determinant prevents a matrix inverse.
