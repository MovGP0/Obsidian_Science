> [!TLDR]
> The tensor product turns bilinear dependence on two inputs into linear dependence on one new object.

## Why introduce a new product?

Let $V$ and $W$ be finite-dimensional real vector spaces, of dimensions $n$ and $m$. An expression $v\otimes w$ keeps both vector inputs. Unlike an inner product, it does not produce a scalar. The space $V\otimes W$ is generated by such expressions subject to the bilinear relations

$$
(v_1+v_2)\otimes w=v_1\otimes w+v_2\otimes w,
\qquad v\otimes(w_1+w_2)=v\otimes w_1+v\otimes w_2,
$$

$$
(av)\otimes w=a(v\otimes w)=v\otimes(aw).
$$

One construction starts with a vector space whose formal basis consists of all pairs $(v,w)$. Take the quotient by the subspace generated by the differences in these relations. The class of $(v,w)$ is $v\otimes w$. This construction makes the relations exact without requiring a formula for vector multiplication.

The defining **universal property** says that every bilinear map $b:V\times W\to U$ has a unique linear map $\widetilde b:V\otimes W\to U$ with $\widetilde b(v\otimes w)=b(v,w)$. Bilinearity makes the formal-pair map vanish on the quotient relations, so it descends to the quotient. Uniqueness follows because simple tensors span. This explains the purpose of the construction: one linear map can encode any bilinear calculation.

## Basis and dimension

For bases $e_i$ of $V$ and $f_a$ of $W$, expand $v=\sum_i v^i e_i$ and $w=\sum_a w^a f_a$. The relations give

$$
v\otimes w=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{a=1}^m v^i w^a e_i\otimes f_a.
$$

Thus the $nm$ products $e_i\otimes f_a$ span. They are independent: the bilinear coordinate maps $(v,w)\mapsto\varepsilon^i(v)\eta^a(w)$ induce linear maps that extract each coefficient. Applying these maps to a zero expansion forces every coefficient to vanish. Therefore $\dim(V\otimes W)=nm$.

A tensor of the form $v\otimes w$ is **simple** or **decomposable**. A general tensor is a sum of simple tensors. It need not be simple itself.

## Worked example: an outer product and a nonexample

Use standard bases for $V=\mathbb R^2$ and $W=\mathbb R^3$. For $v=(1,2)$ and $w=(3,-1,4)$, the coefficients of $v\otimes w$ form the rectangular array

$$
\begin{pmatrix}3&-1&4\\6&-2&8\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Each entry is $v^i w^a$. The second row is twice the first because both rows use the same $w$. This array representation is often called an outer product.

In $\mathbb R^2\otimes\mathbb R^2$, consider $T=e_1\otimes e_1+e_2\otimes e_2$. Its coefficient matrix is the identity. If $T=(a e_1+b e_2)\otimes(c e_1+d e_2)$, then $ac=bd=1$ and $ad=bc=0$. The first two equations make all four numbers nonzero, which contradicts the last two. Thus $T$ is not simple. For two-factor tensors represented by matrices, simplicity is equivalent to matrix rank one, apart from the zero tensor.

## Vectors, covectors, and scalar products

The same construction works for $V^*\otimes W^*$ and mixed factors. A simple covariant tensor $\alpha\otimes\beta$ is the bilinear function $(v,w)\mapsto\alpha(v)\beta(w)$. A mixed tensor $v\otimes\alpha\in V\otimes V^*$ defines the linear map $x\mapsto\alpha(x)v$. These interpretations follow from the universal property and extend to sums.

An inner product $g(v,w)$ is instead a bilinear map to $\mathbb R$. It induces a linear functional on $V\otimes V$, but it is additional data. Do not replace $\otimes$ with a dot. Factor order also matters: $V\otimes W$ and $W\otimes V$ have a natural swap map, rather than literal equality of their ordered factors. Tensor types in [[18.03 Tensor type]] record these distinctions.

## Practice

1. Expand $(e_1-e_2)\otimes(2f_1+f_2)$.
2. Let $\alpha(x,y)=x+2y$ and $v=(2,-1)$. Compute the linear map represented by $v\otimes\alpha$ and its value on $(3,4)$.

> [!example]- Worked answers
> 1. Distribute in both slots to obtain $2e_1\otimes f_1+e_1\otimes f_2-2e_2\otimes f_1-e_2\otimes f_2$. The coefficient array is $\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\-2&-1\end{pmatrix}$.
> 2. The map sends $(x,y)$ to $(x+2y)(2,-1)=(2x+4y,-x-2y)$. Its matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}2&4\\-1&-2\end{pmatrix}$. Since $\alpha(3,4)=11$, the output is $(22,-11)$.
