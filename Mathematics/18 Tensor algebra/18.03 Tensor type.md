> [!TLDR]
> Tensor type counts vector and covector factors and determines the input slots and component-array dimensions.

## The type convention

Let $V$ be an $n$-dimensional real vector space. This article calls an element of

$$
T^r{}_s(V)=V^{\otimes r}\otimes(V^*)^{\otimes s}
$$

a tensor of **type $(r,s)$**. There are $r$ vector factors and $s$ covector factors. The total order is $r+s$. Some books call this total the tensor rank. That word can also mean the minimum number of simple tensors needed in a sum, so state which meaning is intended.

The exponents $\otimes r$ mean repeated tensor products, not numerical powers of a vector. When $r=s=0$, the space is $\mathbb R$: scalars are tensors of order zero.

Choose a basis $e_i$ with dual basis $\varepsilon^j$. A type $(r,s)$ tensor has an expansion

$$
T=T^{i_1\cdots i_r}{}_{j_1\cdots j_s}
e_{i_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{i_r}
\otimes\varepsilon^{j_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\varepsilon^{j_s},
$$

where repeated indices are summed from $1$ to $n$. Upper component indices correspond to vector factors and are called contravariant. Lower indices correspond to covector factors and are called covariant. These words describe their basis-transformation laws; they do not describe positive or negative values.

## Slots reverse the factor roles

A vector $v$ can accept a covector $\alpha$ through the scalar $\alpha(v)$. A covector accepts a vector. Consequently a tensor of type $(r,s)$ can be viewed as a multilinear map

$$
T:(V^*)^r\times V^s\longrightarrow\mathbb R.
$$

For a simple tensor, evaluate each covector argument on its corresponding vector factor and each covector factor on its vector argument, then multiply the results. Extend by addition. Finite dimension ensures that this interpretation gives every multilinear scalar-valued map of the stated kind. It is easy to reverse this convention: a covariant tensor has vector input slots, even though its factors are covectors.

For example, a bilinear form $B(v,w)$ has type $(0,2)$. A linear map $A:V\to V$ corresponds to type $(1,1)$ through $T_A(\alpha,v)=\alpha(Av)$. A vector itself has type $(1,0)$, and a covector has type $(0,1)$.

## Component counts and symmetry

Each index can take $n$ values. Thus the full tensor space has dimension $n^{r+s}$. Its component array has one axis of length $n$ for each slot. A type $(2,1)$ tensor in dimension three has $3\times3\times3=27$ components. The type is not determined by that shape alone: type $(3,0)$ has the same shape but different input spaces and transformation laws.

Constraints can reduce the number of independent components. A symmetric covariant two-tensor satisfies $B_{ij}=B_{ji}$. Its diagonal has $n$ choices and its entries above the diagonal have $n(n-1)/2$ choices. The total is $n(n+1)/2$. An alternating two-tensor satisfies $F_{ij}=-F_{ji}$, so its diagonal vanishes over $\mathbb R$, and only $n(n-1)/2$ independent choices remain.

## Worked example: build and evaluate a mixed tensor

In dimension two, let

$$
T=e_1\otimes e_2\otimes\varepsilon^1
+2e_2\otimes e_1\otimes\varepsilon^2.
$$

This has type $(2,1)$ and total order three. Its eight-entry array has only $T^{12}{}_1=1$ and $T^{21}{}_2=2$ nonzero. With $\alpha=\varepsilon^1+3\varepsilon^2$, $\beta=2\varepsilon^1-\varepsilon^2$, and $v=4e_1+5e_2$, evaluation gives

$$
T(\alpha,\beta,v)
=\alpha(e_1)\beta(e_2)\varepsilon^1(v)
+2\alpha(e_2)\beta(e_1)\varepsilon^2(v)
=1(-1)4+2(3)(2)5=56.
$$

Each input was used once in each term. This separate linearity is the defining test for multilinearity. Adding a metric can change type by an explicit identification, as explained in [[18.08 Forms and metrics]]; moving an index on paper alone cannot do that.

## Practice

1. Identify the type and full component count of a trilinear map $C:V\times V\times V\to\mathbb R$ when $\dim V=4$.
2. How many independent components does a symmetric bilinear form on a five-dimensional real vector space have? Compare with an alternating form.

> [!example]- Worked answers
> 1. Each vector input requires a covector factor, so $C$ has type $(0,3)$. Its full array has $4^3=64$ entries. Trilinearity alone does not impose symmetry.
> 2. For a symmetric form, choose five diagonal entries and $5\cdot4/2=10$ off-diagonal entries, for a total of $15$. For an alternating form the diagonal is zero and the same ten off-diagonal entries determine their negatives below the diagonal, so the answer is $10$.
