title: "The unit circle"

> [!TLDR]
> Cosine and sine are the horizontal and vertical coordinates of the point reached by a directed angle on the unit circle.

## A definition valid for every real angle

The unit circle in a Cartesian plane is the set of points $(x,y)$ satisfying $x^2+y^2=1$. Its center is the origin and its radius is one. Start at $(1,0)$ and turn through an angle $\theta$, measured in radians unless a degree symbol is shown. Positive angles turn counterclockwise. Define

$$
P(\theta)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta).
$$

The definition applies to every real $\theta$, including negative angles and angles greater than a full turn. The coordinates are signed. They are not always the positive lengths of two triangle sides.

![[math-04-unit-circle.svg|**Fig. 4.2** A unit-radius point has coordinates (cos θ, sin θ). Its projections give the signed coordinate values, and the quadrants determine their signs.]]

For $0<\theta<\pi/2$, dropping a perpendicular to the $x$-axis forms a right triangle with hypotenuse one. Thus cosine is adjacent leg divided by hypotenuse and sine is opposite leg divided by hypotenuse. Similarity extends these ratios to a right triangle of any positive size. The circle definition also handles angles for which an acute right triangle is insufficient.

## Signs, axes, and periodicity

In quadrant I, both coordinates are positive. In quadrant II, cosine is negative and sine positive. In quadrant III, both are negative. In quadrant IV, cosine is positive and sine negative. On an axis, one coordinate is zero, so use the actual point rather than a quadrant sign rule.

The four axis points give

$$
\begin{array}{c|rrrr}
\theta&0&\pi/2&\pi&3\pi/2\\\hline
\cos\theta&1&0&-1&0\\
\sin\theta&0&1&0&-1
\end{array}
$$

A full turn returns to the same point. For every integer $k$,

$$
\cos(\theta+2k\pi)=\cos\theta,\qquad
\sin(\theta+2k\pi)=\sin\theta.
$$

Reflection across the $x$-axis replaces $\theta$ by $-\theta$. It leaves $x$ unchanged and reverses $y$. Therefore $\cos(-\theta)=\cos\theta$ and $\sin(-\theta)=-\sin\theta$. Cosine is an even function and sine is an odd function.

## Exact values and the circle identity

An isosceles right triangle has equal legs. On the unit circle they satisfy $2x^2=1$, so at $\pi/4$ both coordinates equal $\sqrt2/2$. Bisect an equilateral triangle of side two to obtain a right triangle with hypotenuse two and legs one and $\sqrt3$. This gives

$$
\sin(\pi/6)=\frac12,\quad \cos(\pi/6)=\frac{\sqrt3}{2},
\qquad
\sin(\pi/3)=\frac{\sqrt3}{2},\quad \cos(\pi/3)=\frac12.
$$

Every unit-circle point satisfies its defining equation. Substitution gives the identity

$$
\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1.
$$

Here $\cos^2\theta$ means $(\cos\theta)^2$, not $\cos(\theta^2)$. The coordinate bounds also show that each function takes values only in $[-1,1]$. Tangent is the ratio $\tan\theta=\sin\theta/\cos\theta$ where $\cos\theta\ne0$. It is undefined at $\pi/2+k\pi$.

## Worked example

Find the circle point at $\theta=7\pi/6$. This is $\pi+\pi/6$, so its terminal ray lies in quadrant III with reference angle $\pi/6$. Both coordinates are negative:

$$
P(7\pi/6)=\left(-\frac{\sqrt3}{2},-\frac12\right).
$$

Its squared radius is $3/4+1/4=1$, as required. Its tangent is $(-1/2)/(-\sqrt3/2)=1/\sqrt3$. The angle $-5\pi/6$ is coterminal with $7\pi/6$ because their difference is $2\pi$, so it gives the same point.

To find all angles with a prescribed sine or cosine, include every compatible point on the circle and all full turns. An inverse trigonometric function returns one selected angle; it does not automatically return every solution.

## Practice

1. Find $\sin(-\pi/3)$ and $\cos(11\pi/6)$ exactly.
2. Solve $\sin\theta=1/2$ for $0\leq\theta<2\pi$, then state all real solutions.

## Worked answers

1. Reflection gives $\sin(-\pi/3)=-\sin(\pi/3)=-\sqrt3/2$. The angle $11\pi/6$ lies in quadrant IV, with reference angle $\pi/6$, so its positive horizontal coordinate is $\cos(11\pi/6)=\sqrt3/2$.
2. The horizontal line $y=1/2$ meets the unit circle in quadrants I and II. The reference angle is $\pi/6$, so the interval solutions are $\pi/6$ and $\pi-\pi/6=5\pi/6$. All solutions are $\theta=\pi/6+2k\pi$ or $\theta=5\pi/6+2k\pi$, where $k\in\mathbb Z$.
