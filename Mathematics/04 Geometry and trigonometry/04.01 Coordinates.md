> [!TLDR]
> Cartesian coordinates locate points relative to perpendicular axes; differences between coordinates give displacements, distances, and midpoints.

## Points and a coordinate system

A Cartesian coordinate system in a Euclidean plane has an origin $O$, a horizontal $x$-axis, and a perpendicular $y$-axis. Choose a positive direction and the same scale on both axes. The ordered pair $P=(x,y)$ gives the signed steps from $O$ to $P$: first $x$ along the first axis, then $y$ parallel to the second axis. The order matters: $(2,5)$ and $(5,2)$ usually name different points.

The origin is $(0,0)$. A point on the $x$-axis has $y=0$. A point on the $y$-axis has $x=0$. Negative coordinates specify the direction opposite to the selected positive direction. They do not specify a negative distance.

In Euclidean three-dimensional space, add a $z$-axis perpendicular to the other two axes. A point then has three coordinates $(x,y,z)$. The formulas below extend by including the third component.

## From points to displacement

Let $A=(x_1,y_1)$ and $B=(x_2,y_2)$. The displacement from $A$ to $B$ is the ordered difference

$$
\overrightarrow{AB}=B-A=(\Delta x,\Delta y),\qquad
\Delta x=x_2-x_1,\quad \Delta y=y_2-y_1.
$$

Here $\Delta$ means final value minus initial value. A displacement has a direction as well as a magnitude. Reversing the endpoints reverses both components: $\overrightarrow{BA}=-\overrightarrow{AB}$. By contrast, the distance between the endpoints stays the same.

![[math-04-coordinate-distance.svg|**Fig. 4.1** The displacement from A to B has perpendicular components 6 and 8. The midpoint divides both coordinate changes in half.]]

In the figure, the horizontal and vertical changes form the legs of a right triangle. The Pythagorean theorem gives the square of its hypotenuse. Thus the Euclidean distance is

$$
d(A,B)=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}.
$$

Squaring removes the signs of the components; the square root selects the nonnegative length. In three dimensions, first combine the $x$- and $y$-changes, then combine that length with the perpendicular $z$-change:

$$
d(A,B)=\sqrt{(\Delta x)^2+(\Delta y)^2+(\Delta z)^2}.
$$

These formulas require perpendicular axes and a common scale. A coordinate sketch with unequal display scales can make a correct length look incorrect. The derivation is explained further in [[04.03 Triangles]].

## Midpoints and points along a segment

The midpoint $M$ is halfway along the displacement from $A$ to $B$. Therefore

$$
M=A+\frac12(B-A)
=\left(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2},\frac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right).
$$

The average of the endpoints is a consequence of this displacement calculation. More generally,

$$
P(t)=A+t(B-A)
$$

gives the point a fraction $t$ along the segment when $0\leq t\leq1$. The values $t=0$ and $t=1$ give the endpoints. Values outside this interval lie on the same line, beyond the segment. This distinction is useful in [[04.02 Lines and planes]].

## Worked example

Take $A=(-2,-3)$ and $B=(4,5)$. Subtract the coordinates in the same order:

$$
\overrightarrow{AB}=(4-(-2),5-(-3))=(6,8).
$$

The distance is $\sqrt{6^2+8^2}=\sqrt{100}=10$. The midpoint is

$$
M=\left(\frac{-2+4}{2},\frac{-3+5}{2}\right)=(1,1).
$$

As a check, $M-A=(3,4)$ and $B-M=(3,4)$. Both smaller segments have length $5$, so each is half the full segment. A point one quarter of the way from $A$ to $B$ is $A+\tfrac14(6,8)=(-\tfrac12,-1)$.

## A change of origin

If the origin moves by a fixed vector $\mathbf c$, both endpoint coordinates change by subtracting $\mathbf c$. Their difference is $(B-\mathbf c)-(A-\mathbf c)=B-A$. Thus a translation changes point coordinates but preserves displacement and distance. A point and a displacement can have the same pair of numbers, but they have different geometric roles.

## Common errors

Do not add the two component lengths to calculate the straight-line distance. The value $|\Delta x|+|\Delta y|$ measures a route along two perpendicular segments. Also, preserve the order of subtraction in both components. Mixing $x_2-x_1$ with $y_1-y_2$ can give the correct distance by accident but the wrong displacement.

## Practice

1. For $C=(3,-1)$ and $D=(-1,2)$, calculate $\overrightarrow{CD}$, the distance, and the midpoint.
2. One endpoint is $A=(-2,7)$ and the midpoint is $M=(3,1)$. Find the other endpoint $B$ and check its distance from $M$.

## Worked answers

1. Subtraction gives $\overrightarrow{CD}=(-1-3,2-(-1))=(-4,3)$. Its magnitude is $\sqrt{16+9}=5$. Averaging gives $M=((3-1)/2,(-1+2)/2)=(1,\tfrac12)$.
2. The midpoint equation is $2M=A+B$, so $B=2M-A=(6,2)-(-2,7)=(8,-5)$. The displacements $M-A=(5,-6)$ and $B-M=(5,-6)$ agree. Each has length $\sqrt{25+36}=\sqrt{61}$.
