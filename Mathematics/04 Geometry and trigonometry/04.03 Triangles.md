> [!TLDR]
> Similarity compares shape, congruence compares shape and size, and triangle identities connect side lengths with included or opposite angles.

## Names, similarity, and congruence

A nondegenerate Euclidean triangle has vertices $A,B,C$ that do not lie on one line. Let $a,b,c$ be its positive side lengths opposite angles $A,B,C$, respectively. Here a vertex letter also denotes its interior angle when used in a trigonometric expression. The angles satisfy $A+B+C=\pi$ radians, or $180^\circ$. Each side must be shorter than the sum of the other two sides.

Two triangles are similar if their corresponding angles agree and corresponding side lengths have a common positive ratio. Two equal angle pairs are sufficient: the angle sum fixes the third pair, and the triangles then have the same shape. Similarity also follows from three proportional sides, or two proportional sides with equal included angles. The included angle is the angle between the stated sides.

Congruent triangles have matching side lengths as well as angles. One can carry one triangle onto the other by translations, rotations, and, if needed, a reflection. Common sufficient data are three sides (SSS), two sides and their included angle (SAS), or two angles and a corresponding side. Equal angles alone give similarity, not congruence. Two sides and an angle opposite one of them (SSA) need not determine a unique triangle.

## The Pythagorean theorem

For a right triangle with legs $a,b$ and hypotenuse $c$,

$$
a^2+b^2=c^2.
$$

One area proof places four copies of the triangle inside a square of side $a+b$, with the hypotenuses bounding an inner square of side $c$. The area of the large square is also the area of four triangles and that inner square:

$$
(a+b)^2=4\left(\frac{ab}{2}\right)+c^2.
$$

Expand the left side and cancel $2ab$ to get the theorem. The right angle is essential; arbitrary triangles require an angle correction.

## The cosine rule

Place $A=(0,0)$ and $B=(c,0)$, with $AC=b$ making angle $A$ with the positive horizontal axis. Then $C=(b\cos A,b\sin A)$. The squared distance from $B$ to $C$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
a^2&=(b\cos A-c)^2+(b\sin A)^2\\
&=b^2(\cos^2 A+\sin^2 A)-2bc\cos A+c^2\\
&=b^2+c^2-2bc\cos A.
\end{aligned}
$$

The unit-circle identity supplies the last step; see [[04.05 The unit circle]]. Relabeling the vertices gives the other two forms. For $A=90^\circ$, the correction vanishes. For an obtuse $A$, the cosine is negative, so the side opposite it has squared length greater than $b^2+c^2$.

## The sine rule

Let $K$ denote area. A base times its perpendicular height gives $K=\tfrac12bc\sin A$. The same calculation with the other bases gives

$$
K=\frac12bc\sin A=\frac12ca\sin B=\frac12ab\sin C.
$$

For an obtuse included angle, the altitude meets an extended side; its positive height still equals the corresponding side times the sine. Divide these equalities by $abc/2$ and invert the positive ratios:

$$
\frac{a}{\sin A}=\frac{b}{\sin B}=\frac{c}{\sin C}.
$$

Use the cosine rule for two sides and their included angle, or three known sides. Use the sine rule when a known side has a known opposite angle.

## Worked example

Suppose $b=5$, $c=7$, and $A=60^\circ$. The cosine rule gives

$$
a^2=25+49-2(5)(7)\frac12=39,
\qquad a=\sqrt{39}.
$$

To find $B$ without an inverse-sine ambiguity, solve the cosine rule at $B$:

$$
\cos B=\frac{a^2+c^2-b^2}{2ac}
=\frac{39+49-25}{14\sqrt{39}}
=\frac{63}{14\sqrt{39}}.
$$

Thus $B\approx43.90^\circ$ and $C=180^\circ-60^\circ-B\approx76.10^\circ$. The area is $K=\tfrac12(5)(7)\sin60^\circ=35\sqrt3/4$.

When using $\sin B=q$, both $B$ and $180^\circ-B$ have that sine. Check side information and the angle sum before selecting a solution. Also set a calculator to the angle convention used in the calculation.

## Practice

1. A right triangle has legs $6$ and $8$. A similar triangle has hypotenuse $15$. Find its legs.
2. A triangle has sides $a=7$, $b=8$, $c=9$. Find $\cos C$ and determine whether $C$ is acute.

## Worked answers

1. The first hypotenuse is $\sqrt{36+64}=10$. The scale factor is $15/10=3/2$. Multiply both legs by that factor to obtain $9$ and $12$. Their squares sum to $81+144=225=15^2$.
2. Solve $c^2=a^2+b^2-2ab\cos C$: $\cos C=(49+64-81)/(2\cdot7\cdot8)=32/112=2/7$. Since an interior angle lies between $0$ and $180^\circ$, a positive cosine means it is acute. In fact $C=\arccos(2/7)\approx73.40^\circ$.
