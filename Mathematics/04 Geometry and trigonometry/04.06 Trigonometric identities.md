> [!TLDR]
> Unit-circle geometry gives the Pythagorean identity, and composition of rotations gives the sine and cosine angle-addition identities.

## What an identity states

An identity is an equality that holds for every input in its stated domain. For example, $\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1$ holds for every real angle $\theta$. The equation $\sin\theta=1/2$ holds only for certain angles, so it is not an identity. When a formula has a denominator, its domain excludes inputs that make that denominator zero.

Use the geometric definitions from [[04.05 The unit circle]]: the point at angle $\theta$ is $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$. Since the squared distance from the origin is one,

$$
\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1.
$$

Where $\cos\theta\ne0$, divide every term by $\cos^2\theta$ to obtain $1+\tan^2\theta=1/\cos^2\theta$. The new formula has a smaller domain than the original identity. Division does not preserve inputs where the divisor is zero.

## Derive angle addition with a rotation

Let $R_\alpha$ be the Euclidean rotation about the origin through angle $\alpha$. Write $\mathbf e_x=(1,0)$ and $\mathbf e_y=(0,1)$. Rotating the first axis gives $R_\alpha\mathbf e_x=(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)$. The second rotated axis is perpendicular to the first and lies one positive quarter-turn from it, so

$$
R_\alpha\mathbf e_y=(-\sin\alpha,\cos\alpha).
$$

A rotation preserves vector sums and scalar multiples: the image of a parallelogram is the parallelogram formed by the images of its sides. Therefore the point at angle $\beta$, written $\cos\beta\,\mathbf e_x+\sin\beta\,\mathbf e_y$, is sent to

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_\alpha(\cos\beta,\sin\beta)
&=\cos\beta(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)
+\sin\beta(-\sin\alpha,\cos\alpha)\\
&=(\cos\alpha\cos\beta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta,
\sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta).
\end{aligned}
$$

This point is at angle $\alpha+\beta$. Equate its coordinates with $(\cos(\alpha+\beta),\sin(\alpha+\beta))$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\cos(\alpha+\beta)&=\cos\alpha\cos\beta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta,\\
\sin(\alpha+\beta)&=\sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta.
\end{aligned}
$$

This derivation uses signed coordinates, so it covers all real angles rather than only acute ones.

## Subtraction and double angles

Replace $\beta$ by $-\beta$. Reflection makes cosine even and sine odd. Hence

$$
\cos(\alpha-\beta)=\cos\alpha\cos\beta+\sin\alpha\sin\beta,
$$

$$
\sin(\alpha-\beta)=\sin\alpha\cos\beta-\cos\alpha\sin\beta.
$$

Taking $\alpha=\beta=\theta$ in the addition formulas gives

$$
\sin2\theta=2\sin\theta\cos\theta,\qquad
\cos2\theta=\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta.
$$

Use the Pythagorean identity to replace one squared term. Thus $\cos2\theta=2\cos^2\theta-1=1-2\sin^2\theta$. Select the form that uses the information available. Taking square roots later requires a sign choice; a squared identity alone cannot determine the quadrant.

## Worked example

Calculate $\sin75^\circ$ exactly by writing $75^\circ=45^\circ+30^\circ$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sin75^\circ
&=\sin45^\circ\cos30^\circ+\cos45^\circ\sin30^\circ\\
&=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\frac{\sqrt3}{2}
+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\frac12
=\frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Similarly, $\cos75^\circ=(\sqrt6-\sqrt2)/4$. Squaring and adding gives $((8+4\sqrt3)+(8-4\sqrt3))/16=1$, a useful arithmetic check.

For simplification, consider $(1-\cos2\theta)/\sin\theta$. On its original domain $\sin\theta\ne0$, replace the numerator by $2\sin^2\theta$ and cancel one factor to obtain $2\sin\theta$. The simpler expression is defined at extra points, but the original fraction remains undefined there.

## How to check an identity argument

A proof must retain every allowed input at each step. Factoring and applying an established identity are valid on the original domain. Cancellation requires a nonzero factor and must carry that restriction forward. Numerical substitution can reveal a false claim, but a few matching values cannot establish an identity for all real inputs. For example, $\sin(\alpha+\beta)=\sin\alpha+\sin\beta$ fails at $\alpha=\beta=\pi/2$: its two sides are zero and two.

## Practice

1. Given $\cos\theta=-3/5$ and $\theta$ in quadrant II, find $\sin\theta$, $\sin2\theta$, and $\cos2\theta$.
2. Derive $\sin(\pi/2-\theta)=\cos\theta$ from an angle-subtraction identity.

## Worked answers

1. The Pythagorean identity gives $\sin^2\theta=1-9/25=16/25$. Sine is positive in quadrant II, so $\sin\theta=4/5$. Then $\sin2\theta=2(4/5)(-3/5)=-24/25$ and $\cos2\theta=9/25-16/25=-7/25$.
2. Use $\sin(\alpha-\beta)=\sin\alpha\cos\beta-\cos\alpha\sin\beta$ with $\alpha=\pi/2$ and $\beta=\theta$. Since $\sin(\pi/2)=1$ and $\cos(\pi/2)=0$, the result is $1\cos\theta-0\sin\theta=\cos\theta$. There are no denominators, so this holds for every real $\theta$.
