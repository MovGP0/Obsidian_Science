> [!TLDR]
> A perpendicular projection gives a signed component along an axis; use the angle measured from that axis when selecting cosine.

## A segment and its components

Let a directed segment have displacement $\mathbf v=(v_x,v_y)$ in a Cartesian plane. Its length is $L=\sqrt{v_x^2+v_y^2}$. For $L>0$, let $\theta$ be the directed angle from the positive $x$-axis to $\mathbf v$. Scaling the unit-circle point by $L$ gives

$$
\mathbf v=L(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),
\qquad v_x=L\cos\theta,\quad v_y=L\sin\theta.
$$

The scalar components $v_x$ and $v_y$ are signed. The lengths of the corresponding projected segments are $|v_x|$ and $|v_y|$. The zero vector has both components zero but has no unique direction angle.

![[math-04-projection-angles.svg|**Fig. 4.3** The same segment has angle θ from the horizontal and complementary angle φ from the vertical. Its horizontal component is L cos θ = L sin φ.]]

In quadrant I, the two acute angles in the right triangle satisfy $\phi=\pi/2-\theta$. Therefore

$$
v_x=L\cos\theta=L\sin\phi,
\qquad v_y=L\sin\theta=L\cos\phi.
$$

The projection formula did not change. The stated reference angle changed. Always mark the angle's initial axis before selecting sine or cosine. For directions outside quadrant I, an unsigned acute angle may lose the sign information. Signed dot products avoid that problem.

## Projection onto any direction

A unit vector $\mathbf u$ specifies a positive axis direction and satisfies $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u=1$. The dot product in the plane is $(a,b)\cdot(c,d)=ac+bd$. Resolve $\mathbf v$ as

$$
\mathbf v=p\mathbf u+\mathbf w,
\qquad \mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u=0.
$$

Here $p\mathbf u$ is parallel to the axis and $\mathbf w$ is perpendicular to it. Take the dot product with $\mathbf u$:

$$
\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u
=p(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u)+\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u=p.
$$

Thus the scalar projection is $p=\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u$, and the vector projection is $\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf u}\mathbf v=(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u)\mathbf u$. For a nonunit vector $\mathbf a\ne0$, normalize it or use

$$
\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf a}\mathbf v
=\frac{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a}{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf a}\mathbf a.
$$

If $\alpha$ is the angle between nonzero $\mathbf v$ and unit $\mathbf u$, this scalar projection is also $L\cos\alpha$. A negative value means the projection points opposite to $\mathbf u$. The vector projection is unchanged if the axis direction is reversed, but its scalar coordinate changes sign.

## Worked examples

A directed segment of length $10$ makes $30^\circ$ with the positive horizontal axis in quadrant I. Its components are

$$
v_x=10\cos30^\circ=5\sqrt3,\qquad
v_y=10\sin30^\circ=5.
$$

If the diagram instead labels the angle $60^\circ$ from the vertical, then $v_x=10\sin60^\circ=5\sqrt3$. Using $10\cos60^\circ$ for the horizontal component would interchange the two components. Check the result with $(5\sqrt3)^2+5^2=100$.

For an axis that is not horizontal, take $\mathbf v=(4,2)$ and $\mathbf u=(3/5,4/5)$. The latter is a unit vector because $9/25+16/25=1$. Then

$$
p=(4,2)\cdot(3/5,4/5)=12/5+8/5=4,
$$

so the vector projection is $4\mathbf u=(12/5,16/5)$. The residual is $\mathbf w=(8/5,-6/5)$. Its dot product with $\mathbf u$ is $24/25-24/25=0$, which checks perpendicularity.

## Why perpendicular projection gives the nearest point

Every vector on the axis has form $q\mathbf u$ for some real $q$. Write $\mathbf v=p\mathbf u+\mathbf w$ with $\mathbf w$ perpendicular to $\mathbf u$. The squared distance from $\mathbf v$ to that axis point is

$$
\|\mathbf v-q\mathbf u\|^2=(p-q)^2+\|\mathbf w\|^2.
$$

Here $\|\cdot\|$ denotes Euclidean length. The two terms come from perpendicular components. The second is fixed and the first is nonnegative, with minimum zero at $q=p$. Thus the perpendicular foot is the unique nearest point on the whole axis. For a finite segment, the foot can lie outside its endpoints; the nearest point on the segment is then an endpoint.

## Limitations and checks

The components of a vector add as vectors, not as unsigned lengths. The right-triangle relation is $L^2=v_x^2+v_y^2$, whereas $L=|v_x|+|v_y|$ is generally false. The projection formula also requires a nonzero axis vector. Division by $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf a$ is impossible for $\mathbf a=0$.

## Practice

1. A segment of length $12$ points into quadrant II at $30^\circ$ above the negative horizontal axis. Find its signed Cartesian components.
2. Project $\mathbf v=(1,3)$ onto the direction $\mathbf a=(2,1)$ and check the perpendicular remainder.

## Worked answers

1. The standard angle is $150^\circ$, so $v_x=12\cos150^\circ=-6\sqrt3$ and $v_y=12\sin150^\circ=6$. The signs agree with quadrant II, and the squared components sum to $108+36=144$.
2. Compute $\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf a=2+3=5$ and $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf a=4+1=5$. The projection is $(5/5)(2,1)=(2,1)$. The remainder is $(1,3)-(2,1)=(-1,2)$; its dot product with $(2,1)$ is $-2+2=0$.
