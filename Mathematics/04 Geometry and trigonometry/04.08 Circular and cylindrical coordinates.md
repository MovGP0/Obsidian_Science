title: "Circular and cylindrical coordinates"

> [!TLDR]
> Polar and cylindrical coordinates use radius and angle to locate points; local angular displacement has length r dθ along the circle.

## Polar coordinates in the plane

Choose an origin and a positive reference ray along the Cartesian $x$-axis. Polar coordinates $(r,\theta)$ specify a radius $r\geq0$ and a counterclockwise angle $\theta$, measured in radians. The same system is also called circular coordinates. Its Cartesian coordinates are

$$
x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta.
$$

For $r>0$, adding $2k\pi$ to $\theta$ leaves the point unchanged for every integer $k$. A restricted angle interval selects one representative, apart from the chosen boundary convention. At $r=0$, every angle represents the origin; the angle is undefined there. Some texts allow negative $r$ with $(r,\theta)=(-r,\theta+\pi)$. This article uses $r\geq0$ to avoid that extra duplication.

To convert a Cartesian point, set $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$. Select $\theta$ using the signs of both coordinates. A two-argument function $\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)$ does this with a declared range. The value $\arctan(y/x)$ alone loses quadrant information and cannot handle $x=0$.

## Cylindrical coordinates

In three-dimensional Euclidean space, retain the Cartesian height $z$ and use polar coordinates in each horizontal plane. Cylindrical coordinates are $(r,\theta,z)$, with

$$
(x,y,z)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,z).
$$

A surface $r=a>0$ is a circular cylinder around the $z$-axis. A fixed $z$ gives a horizontal plane. A fixed angle gives a vertical half-plane when $r\geq0$. On the $z$-axis, $r=0$ and the angle is not unique, just as at the polar origin.

## Local directions and local displacement

At angle $\theta$, define perpendicular unit vectors

$$
\mathbf e_r=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0),\qquad
\mathbf e_\theta=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta,0),\qquad
\mathbf e_z=(0,0,1).
$$

The first points outward from the axis; the second points in the direction of increasing angle. Their dot products are zero and their squared lengths are one. Unlike the Cartesian basis, the first two vectors change when $\theta$ changes.

The position vector is $\mathbf R=r\mathbf e_r+z\mathbf e_z$. To find its differential, use $d\mathbf e_r=\mathbf e_\theta\,d\theta$, which follows by differentiating sine and cosine with radian arguments. The product rule gives

$$
d\mathbf R=\mathbf e_r\,dr+r\mathbf e_\theta\,d\theta+\mathbf e_z\,dz.
$$

The local coordinate changes therefore contribute lengths $dr$, $r\,d\theta$, and $dz$ in perpendicular directions. In particular, $d\theta$ alone is not an arc length. The angular scale factor is the current radius. Orthogonality gives the Euclidean line element

$$
ds^2=dr^2+r^2d\theta^2+dz^2.
$$

Omit the $dz^2$ term for a plane. This differential statement uses local changes, not arbitrary finite differences. It is one reason radian measure, defined in [[04.04 Angular measure]], is natural.

## Worked example and a finite-change check

Convert $(x,y,z)=(-3,3,2)$. Its radius is $r=\sqrt{9+9}=3\sqrt2$. The point lies in quadrant II, so choose $\theta=3\pi/4$. The cylindrical coordinates are $(3\sqrt2,3\pi/4,2)$, and substitution gives $r\cos\theta=-3$ and $r\sin\theta=3$.

Now consider a small coordinate change at $r=3$: $\Delta r=0.02$, $\Delta\theta=0.01$, and $\Delta z=0.04$. The first-order displacement has local components $(0.02,0.03,0.04)$, so its length is approximately

$$
\sqrt{0.02^2+0.03^2+0.04^2}=\sqrt{0.0029}\approx0.05385.
$$

For a finite angle at constant radius, distinguish arc and chord. Two points with angular separation $\Delta\theta$ have chord length $2r|\sin(\Delta\theta/2)|$, while the circular arc has length $r|\Delta\theta|$. At $r=3$ and $\Delta\theta=\pi/2$, these are $3\sqrt2$ and $3\pi/2$. The local formula approximates the chord only for small angular differences; it gives the arc length exactly along a constant-radius monotone arc.

## Practice

1. Convert polar coordinates $(4,5\pi/6)$ to Cartesian coordinates. Find a coterminal angle.
2. On a cylinder $r=5$, a point follows a constant-height arc with angular change $-\pi/3$. Find its arc length and its endpoint chord length.

## Worked answers

1. Use $\cos(5\pi/6)=-\sqrt3/2$ and $\sin(5\pi/6)=1/2$. Thus $(x,y)=(-2\sqrt3,2)$. Adding $2\pi$ gives $17\pi/6$, which specifies the same point at radius four.
2. The negative sign records clockwise orientation. The length is nonnegative: $5|{-\pi/3}|=5\pi/3$. The chord is $2(5)|\sin(-\pi/6)|=10(1/2)=5$. The zero height change makes this the same planar chord calculation at the stated height.
