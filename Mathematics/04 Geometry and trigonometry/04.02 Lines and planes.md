> [!TLDR]
> Direction vectors describe lines, normal vectors describe planes, and simultaneous equations determine their intersections.

## Equations of a line in a plane

A line through $P=(x_0,y_0)$ with nonzero direction vector $\mathbf d=(a,b)$ consists of the points

$$
(x,y)=(x_0,y_0)+t(a,b),\qquad t\in\mathbb R.
$$

The parameter $t$ can be any real number. Restricting it to an interval gives a segment. If $a\ne0$, solve $x=x_0+ta$ for $t=(x-x_0)/a$ and substitute into $y=y_0+tb$. The result is the point-slope equation

$$
y-y_0=m(x-x_0),\qquad m=\frac ba.
$$

The slope $m$ is the vertical change divided by the horizontal change. A vertical line has $a=0$ and equation $x=x_0$; it has no finite slope. The form $y=mx+c$ therefore cannot represent every line.

The general equation $Ax+By=C$, with $(A,B)\ne(0,0)$, also represents a line. Its normal vector $\mathbf n=(A,B)$ is perpendicular to the line. To check this, two points on the line have differences satisfying $A\Delta x+B\Delta y=0$. The dot product of vectors is the sum of the products of corresponding components, so this is $\mathbf n\mathbin\cdot\mathbf d=0$.

## Parallel and perpendicular directions

Two nonzero direction vectors are parallel if one is a nonzero scalar multiple of the other. Distinct parallel lines do not meet; two line equations can also describe the same line. In a Cartesian plane, vectors $(a,b)$ and $(c,d)$ are perpendicular exactly when $ac+bd=0$.

For two nonvertical lines with slopes $m_1,m_2$, use direction vectors $(1,m_1)$ and $(1,m_2)$. Their dot product is $1+m_1m_2$, so perpendicularity gives $m_1m_2=-1$. This slope rule does not cover a vertical line paired with a horizontal line; the vector test does.

To find an intersection, solve the two line equations together. There is one solution for distinct nonparallel lines, no solution for distinct parallel lines, and infinitely many solutions for coincident lines.

## Lines and planes in three dimensions

In $\mathbb R^3$, a line still has the form $\mathbf r=\mathbf p+t\mathbf d$. A plane through $\mathbf p=(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ with nonzero normal $\mathbf n=(A,B,C)$ has equation

$$
\mathbf n\mathbin\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf p)=0,
\qquad A(x-x_0)+B(y-y_0)+C(z-z_0)=0.
$$

The dot product is $\mathbf u\mathbin\cdot\mathbf v=u_xv_x+u_yv_y+u_zv_z$. Its zero value says that every displacement within the plane is perpendicular to the normal. A line direction $\mathbf d$ is parallel to a plane when $\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf d=0$. The line then either lies in the plane or has no intersection with it.

Two planes with parallel normals are parallel or coincident. With nonparallel normals, they meet in a line. Two lines in space can be skew: neither parallel nor intersecting. Thus the planar classification of two lines does not extend unchanged to space.

## Worked intersections

Consider $y=2x+1$ and $x+y=7$. Substitute the first equation into the second:

$$
x+(2x+1)=7,\qquad 3x=6,\qquad x=2.
$$

Then $y=5$, and substitution confirms both original equations. The slopes are $2$ and $-1$, whose product is $-2$; these lines intersect but are not perpendicular.

For a spatial example, intersect $\mathbf r=(1,0,2)+t(1,2,-1)$ with $x+y+z=6$. Substitute $x=1+t$, $y=2t$, and $z=2-t$:

$$
(1+t)+2t+(2-t)=6,\qquad 3+2t=6.
$$

Thus $t=3/2$ and the intersection is $(5/2,3,1/2)$. The coefficient $2$ of $t$ equals $(1,1,1)\cdot(1,2,-1)$, so it also confirms that the line is not parallel to the plane.

For two planes, elimination can expose their line of intersection. Intersect $x+y+z=6$ with $x-y+z=2$. Subtract the second equation from the first to obtain $2y=4$, so $y=2$. Either equation then gives $x+z=4$. Set $z=t$ to obtain

$$
(x,y,z)=(4,2,0)+t(-1,0,1).
$$

Both plane equations hold for every $t$. Their normals $(1,1,1)$ and $(1,-1,1)$ are nonparallel. In general, two planes are perpendicular when their normals have zero dot product. The normals in this example have dot product $1-1+1=1$, so the planes meet but are not perpendicular.

## Practice

1. Find the line through $(2,-1)$ perpendicular to $3x+4y=5$.
2. Determine whether $\mathbf r=(0,1,0)+t(1,-1,2)$ intersects the plane $x+y=3$.

## Worked answers

1. The given line has normal $(3,4)$. A perpendicular line can use that normal as its direction, so $(x,y)=(2,-1)+t(3,4)$. Its slope is $4/3$ and its equation is $y+1=\tfrac43(x-2)$. A direction of the original line is $(4,-3)$; its dot product with $(3,4)$ is $12-12=0$.
2. Along the line, $x=t$ and $y=1-t$, so $x+y=1$ for every $t$. This cannot equal $3$. The normal $(1,1,0)$ has zero dot product with $(1,-1,2)$, so the line is parallel to the plane and lies outside it.
