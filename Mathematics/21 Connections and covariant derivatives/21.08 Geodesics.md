> [!TLDR]
> An affinely parameterized geodesic transports its own tangent vector in parallel.

## The parallel-tangent definition

Let $M$ have a smooth affine connection, and let $\gamma:I\to M$ be a twice differentiable curve. A **geodesic with affine parameter** $t$ satisfies

$$
\frac{D\dot\gamma}{dt}=\nabla_{\dot\gamma}\dot\gamma=0.
$$

This means that its tangent vector is parallel along the curve. The definition applies without a metric. For the Levi-Civita connection of a Riemannian metric, these curves also describe locally length-minimizing paths on sufficiently short segments. A geodesic need not minimize distance over an arbitrarily long segment.

Write the curve as $x^k(t)$ in a chart and use $\nabla_{\partial_i}\partial_j=\Gamma^k{}_{ij}\partial_k$. Apply the transport formula from [[21.07 Parallel transport]] to the field with components $V^k=\dot x^k$. Then

$$
\boxed{\ddot x^k+\Gamma^k{}_{ij}(x(t))\dot x^i\dot x^j=0.}
$$

This is a second-order system. Its initial data are a point $x(t_0)=p$ and a tangent vector $\dot\gamma(t_0)=v$. A smooth connection gives a unique local solution for these data. Global extension requires an additional completeness property.

Only the symmetric part $\Gamma^k{}_{(ij)}$ enters this equation, since $\dot x^i\dot x^j$ is symmetric. Thus the geodesic equation alone does not determine the torsion of a general connection.

## Why the parameter matters

Let $t=t(s)$ be a smooth regular reparameterization, with $t'(s)\ne0$. The new tangent is $d\gamma/ds=t'\dot\gamma$. Apply the connection product rule along the curve:

$$
\frac{D}{ds}\frac{d\gamma}{ds}
=(t')^2\frac{D\dot\gamma}{dt}+t''\dot\gamma.
$$

If $t$ is affine, the first term vanishes. The reparameterized curve then satisfies

$$
\frac{D}{ds}\frac{d\gamma}{ds}
=\frac{t''}{t'}\frac{d\gamma}{ds}.
$$

For a nonconstant geodesic, the new parameter is also affine exactly when $t''=0$, or $t=as+b$ with constants $a\ne0,b$. Other regular parameters describe the same image but add a tangent-proportional term to the equation. Constant curves are an exception because their tangent vanishes.

## Worked example: a straight line in polar coordinates

In the flat Euclidean plane, take the line $x=t$, $y=b$ with fixed $b>0$. Its Cartesian connection coefficients are zero, so this is a geodesic with affine parameter $t$. In polar coordinates,

$$
r=\sqrt{t^2+b^2},\qquad
\dot r=\frac{t}{r},\qquad
\ddot r=\frac{b^2}{r^3},
$$

$$
\dot\theta=-\frac{b}{r^2},\qquad
\ddot\theta=\frac{2bt}{r^4}.
$$

Choose the continuous angular branch appropriate to the upper half-plane. The polar geodesic equations are

$$
\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2=0,
\qquad
\ddot\theta+\frac2r\dot r\dot\theta=0.
$$

The first left side is $b^2/r^3-rb^2/r^4=0$. The second is $2bt/r^4-2bt/r^4=0$. Thus nonzero coordinate second derivatives do not prevent a curve from being a geodesic; the connection terms balance them.

## Speed and length

For a metric-compatible connection and an affine geodesic, $d[g(\dot\gamma,\dot\gamma)]/dt=2g(D\dot\gamma/dt,\dot\gamma)=0$. Its squared speed is constant. In a Riemannian metric a nonconstant affine geodesic therefore has constant positive speed and can be rescaled to unit speed. Constant speed by itself does not imply the parallel-tangent condition.

## Practice

1. In the Euclidean line, compare $x(s)=s$ with $x(s)=s^2$ on $s>0$. Which parameterization is affine?
2. Show that the polar circle $r=R>0$, $\theta=t$ is not a Euclidean geodesic.

## Worked answers

1. With zero Cartesian coefficients, the affine equation is $x''=0$. The first curve has $x''=0$. The second has $x''=2$, so its parameter is not affine. It follows the same positive-ray image but satisfies $x''=(1/s)x'$. This is the tangent-proportional term produced by $t=s^2$.
2. Its radial equation has left side $0-R\cdot1=-R$, which is nonzero. Its angular equation is zero, but both equations must hold. Equivalently, its tangent changes direction in the Cartesian plane. A constant radius alone does not make a path straight for the connection.
