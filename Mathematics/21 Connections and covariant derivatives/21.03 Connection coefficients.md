> [!TLDR]
> Connection coefficients record the derivative of the coordinate basis and supply the correction to component derivatives.

## Fix the index convention

Let $x^1,\ldots,x^n$ be smooth coordinates and $\partial_i=\partial/\partial x^i$. A connection maps each pair of coordinate fields to a vector field. Define its coefficients by

$$
\nabla_{\partial_i}\partial_j=\Gamma^k{}_{ij}\partial_k.
$$

The first lower index $i$ specifies the derivative direction. The second lower index $j$ specifies the vector being differentiated. The upper index $k$ selects the output component. Sum each index that occurs once up and once down. An index that occurs once on each side of an equation is free and has the same range on both sides.

These are coefficients of the connection in one coordinate chart. They are not the components of a tensor; [[21.05 Transformation behaviour]] explains the extra coordinate term. Without a torsion assumption, do not interchange $i$ and $j$.

## Derive the vector formula

Write $X=X^i\partial_i$ and $Y=Y^j\partial_j$. Function-linearity in the direction and the Leibniz rule in the field give

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_XY
&=X^i\nabla_{\partial_i}(Y^j\partial_j)\\
&=X^i\bigl((\partial_iY^j)\partial_j+Y^j\nabla_{\partial_i}\partial_j\bigr)\\
&=X^i\bigl(\partial_iY^k+\Gamma^k{}_{ij}Y^j\bigr)\partial_k.
\end{aligned}
$$

In the last line, rename the summed output index in the first term so that both terms use $\partial_k$. Define

$$
\nabla_iY^k=\partial_iY^k+\Gamma^k{}_{ij}Y^j.
$$

Then $(\nabla_XY)^k=X^i\nabla_iY^k$. The array $\nabla_iY^k$ has one upper and one lower index because, for a fixed $Y$, its derivative is a vector-valued one-form. It accepts one direction and returns one vector.

## Worked example in polar coordinates

For the standard flat connection in the Euclidean plane, use coordinates $(r,\theta)$ with $r>0$. Its only nonzero polar coefficients are

$$
\Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta}=-r,
\qquad
\Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta}=\Gamma^\theta{}_{\theta r}=\frac1r.
$$

These follow from the changing basis calculation in [[21.01 The differentiation problem]]. Let $X=\partial_\theta$ and $Y=r\partial_r+\theta\partial_\theta$. Calculate each output component separately:

$$
(\nabla_XY)^r=\partial_\theta r+
\Gamma^r{}_{\theta r}r+
\Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta}\theta=-r\theta,
$$

$$
(\nabla_XY)^\theta=\partial_\theta\theta+
\Gamma^\theta{}_{\theta r}r+
\Gamma^\theta{}_{\theta\theta}\theta=1+1=2.
$$

At $(r,\theta)=(2,1)$ the result is $-2\partial_r+2\partial_\theta$. The ordinary component derivative would give only $\partial_\theta$ and would miss both the radial term and half of the angular coefficient.

## Derivatives along a curve

For a smooth curve $x^i=x^i(t)$, its tangent has components $\dot x^i$. If $V(t)=V^j(t)\partial_j$ is a vector field along the curve, the corresponding expression is

$$
\frac{DV^k}{dt}=\dot V^k+
\Gamma^k{}_{ij}(x(t))\dot x^iV^j.
$$

The total derivative $\dot V^k$ already includes every component change along the curve. Do not add a second chain-rule term to it. The definition also applies when $V$ is given only along the curve, rather than on an open region.

## Common errors

The angular component $Y^\theta$ multiplies $\partial_\theta$, whose length is $r$. It is not the component in the angular unit direction. Also, a coefficient with zero derivative can still contribute to a covariant derivative because it multiplies a changing basis. Check the lower-index convention before copying a formula from another source.

## Practice

1. In the polar chart, find $\nabla_{\partial_r}(r^2\partial_\theta)$.
2. Find $\nabla_{\partial_\theta}\partial_r$ and explain why this does not contradict $[\partial_r,\partial_\theta]=0$.

## Worked answers

1. The component derivative is $2r\partial_\theta$. The basis derivative adds $r^2(1/r)\partial_\theta=r\partial_\theta$. Their sum is $3r\partial_\theta$.
2. The result is $(1/r)\partial_\theta$. The Lie bracket is the difference of two directional operator compositions, not either covariant derivative alone. For this torsion-free connection, $\nabla_{\partial_r}\partial_\theta-\nabla_{\partial_\theta}\partial_r=[\partial_r,\partial_\theta]=0$, because both covariant derivatives equal $(1/r)\partial_\theta$.
