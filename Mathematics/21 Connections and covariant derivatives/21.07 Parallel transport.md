> [!TLDR]
> Parallel transport compares tangent vectors along a specified curve by solving a linear differential equation.

## A vector field along a curve

Let $\gamma:I\to M$ be a smooth curve on a manifold with a smooth affine connection. A vector field along $\gamma$ assigns $V(t)\in T_{\gamma(t)}M$ smoothly for each parameter $t\in I$. The vectors need not be tangent to the curve. In local coordinates $x^i(t)$, write

$$
V(t)=V^j(t)\partial_j\big|_{\gamma(t)},
\qquad
\dot\gamma=\dot x^i\partial_i.
$$

Use the convention $\nabla_{\partial_i}\partial_j=\Gamma^k{}_{ij}\partial_k$. The covariant derivative along the curve has components

$$
\frac{DV^k}{dt}=\frac{dV^k}{dt}
+\Gamma^k{}_{ij}(x(t))\dot x^iV^j.
$$

It combines component change and basis change. It is intrinsic even though its displayed terms depend on a chart. For a field extended to a neighborhood of the curve, it agrees with $\nabla_{\dot\gamma}V$; it does not depend on how that extension is chosen.

## The transport equation

A field is **parallel** along $\gamma$ when $DV/dt=0$. Thus parallel transport is the initial-value problem

$$
\boxed{\dot V^k=-\Gamma^k{}_{ij}(x(t))\dot x^iV^j,
\qquad V(t_0)=v_0.}
$$

After the curve is given, the coefficient matrix $A^k{}_j(t)=\Gamma^k{}_{ij}(x(t))\dot x^i$ is known. The equation is linear in $V$, although the connection coefficients can be nonlinear functions of position. Standard existence and uniqueness for linear ordinary differential equations gives a unique solution along a compact smooth curve segment; overlapping charts extend the solution through chart boundaries.

The endpoint map $P_\gamma:T_{\gamma(t_0)}M\to T_{\gamma(t_1)}M$ is linear. Reversing the curve gives its inverse, and transport along a concatenation composes the transport maps. A smooth regular change of parameter does not change the endpoint map: the chain rule multiplies both the derivative and the connection term by the same factor.

## Worked example: transport around a polar circle

In the Euclidean plane, take $r=R>0$ and $\theta=t$. Use the flat polar coefficients

$$
\Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta}=-R,
\qquad
\Gamma^\theta{}_{\theta r}=1/R.
$$

The two transport equations are

$$
\dot V^r-RV^\theta=0,
\qquad
\dot V^\theta+\frac1R V^r=0.
$$

Set $a=V^r$ and $b=RV^\theta$, the components in the polar unit frame. Then $\dot a=b$ and $\dot b=-a$. With initial data $a(0)=1$, $b(0)=0$, solve $\ddot a=-a$ to obtain

$$
a(t)=\cos t,
\qquad b(t)=-\sin t.
$$

Consequently $V^r=\cos t$ and $V^\theta=-\sin t/R$. Converting back to Cartesian vectors gives

$$
V=\cos t\,e_r-\sin t\,e_\theta=e_x.
$$

The vector is constant in the plane, while its coordinate components change. This is the behavior shown in Fig. 21.1 of [[21.01 The differentiation problem]]. One full turn returns the same vector; use overlapping angular charts to describe the whole circle smoothly.

## Metric and path dependence

For the Levi-Civita connection, metric compatibility gives $d[g(V,W)]/dt=0$ for parallel fields. Thus transport preserves inner products. An arbitrary affine connection need not preserve a specified metric.

In general the endpoint map depends on the chosen curve, not just its endpoints. Transport around a closed loop gives an automorphism of one tangent space, called a holonomy map. Curvature measures the local failure of path independence. Nonzero coordinate coefficients alone do not establish this failure: the polar example has nonzero coefficients and ordinary flat transport.

## Practice

1. In the polar plane, transport $V$ along the ray $r=t>0$, $\theta=\theta_0$. Solve for both components from their values at $t=t_0$.
2. On $\mathbb R$ with $\Gamma^x{}_{xx}=1$, transport $V=v(t)\partial_x$ along $x(t)=t$ from $v(0)=3$.

## Worked answers

1. The radial equation is $\dot V^r=0$, so $V^r(t)=V^r(t_0)$. The angular equation is $\dot V^\theta+V^\theta/t=0$. Multiplication by $t$ gives $d(tV^\theta)/dt=0$, so $V^\theta(t)=t_0V^\theta(t_0)/t$. Its unit-frame angular component $tV^\theta$ stays constant.
2. The equation is $\dot v+v=0$. Separating variables, or differentiating $e^tv$, gives $v(t)=3e^{-t}$. Its length in the standard metric $dx^2$ changes. This connection is not compatible with that metric, so parallel transport is not required to preserve its length.
