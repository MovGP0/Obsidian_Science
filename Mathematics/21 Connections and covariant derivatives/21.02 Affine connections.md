title: "Affine connections"
---

> [!TLDR]
> An affine connection differentiates a vector field along another vector field and obeys a product rule.

## Definition and domain

Let $M$ be a smooth real manifold. Write $C^\infty(M)$ for its smooth scalar functions and $\mathfrak X(M)$ for its smooth vector fields. An **affine connection** on the tangent bundle is a map

$$
\nabla:\mathfrak X(M)\times\mathfrak X(M)\longrightarrow\mathfrak X(M),
\qquad (X,Y)\longmapsto\nabla_XY.
$$

Read $\nabla_XY$ as the covariant derivative of $Y$ in direction $X$. The word affine does not assert that $M$ is an affine space. It describes this connection on tangent vectors; its definition does not require a metric.

For smooth functions $f,g$ and vector fields $X,Z,Y,W$, the defining rules are

$$
\nabla_{fX+gZ}Y=f\nabla_XY+g\nabla_ZY,
$$

$$
\nabla_X(Y+W)=\nabla_XY+\nabla_XW,
\qquad
\nabla_X(fY)=X(f)Y+f\nabla_XY.
$$

The operator $X(f)$ is the directional derivative of the scalar function $f$. The last rule includes linearity over real constants, since their directional derivatives vanish. It is a Leibniz rule over functions, rather than function-linearity in the second argument.

## Why the two slots behave differently

The derivative direction at a point depends only on the vector $X_p$. To see this locally, write $X=X^i\partial_i$. The first axiom gives $\nabla_XY=X^i\nabla_{\partial_i}Y$, so only the numbers $X^i(p)$ occur at $p$. Here $\partial_i=\partial/\partial x^i$ in a coordinate chart, and repeated indices are summed.

In contrast, knowing only $Y_p$ cannot determine how $Y$ changes nearby. For example, in the usual connection on $\mathbb R$, the fields $Y=x\partial_x$ and $W=0$ agree at $x=0$, but

$$
(\nabla_{\partial_x}Y)_0=(\partial_x)_0,
\qquad
(\nabla_{\partial_x}W)_0=0.
$$

This is why $\nabla$ is not itself a tensor in both input fields. A tensor would be linear over smooth functions in each slot. The covariant derivative of one fixed field $Y$, viewed as $X\mapsto\nabla_XY$, is tensorial in its direction slot.

## The ordinary Euclidean connection

On $\mathbb R^n$ with Cartesian coordinates, define

$$
\nabla_XY=X^i(\partial_iY^j)\partial_j.
$$

The product rule for ordinary derivatives verifies all three axioms. This formula uses the fixed Cartesian basis. In a changing coordinate basis the same connection has extra coefficient terms, as derived in [[21.03 Connection coefficients]]. A formula with zero coefficients in one chart need not have zero coefficients in another.

## Worked example: a different connection on a line

On $\mathbb R$, prescribe $\nabla_{\partial_x}\partial_x=x\partial_x$. The axioms then force, for $X=f\partial_x$ and $Y=g\partial_x$,

$$
\nabla_XY=f\nabla_{\partial_x}(g\partial_x)
=f(g'+xg)\partial_x.
$$

Take $f=x^2$ and $g=x$. First compute $g'=1$, then add the basis contribution $xg=x^2$. Thus

$$
\nabla_{x^2\partial_x}(x\partial_x)=(x^2+x^4)\partial_x.
$$

At $x=2$ this is $20\partial_x$. A connection can therefore differ from the usual Cartesian derivative even on a line. Coordinates do not specify a connection by themselves.

## The difference of connections

If $\nabla$ and $\widehat\nabla$ are connections, define $A(X,Y)=\widehat\nabla_XY-\nabla_XY$. In the second slot,

$$
A(X,fY)=X(f)Y+f\widehat\nabla_XY-X(f)Y-f\nabla_XY=fA(X,Y).
$$

The derivative terms cancel. The first slot is already function-linear, so $A$ is a tensor of type $(1,2)$. This useful fact does not make either connection individually a tensor.

## Practice

1. For the line connection above, compute $\nabla_{2\partial_x}(x^2\partial_x)$.
2. Does $D_XY=f\nabla_XY$, for one fixed nonconstant function $f$, generally define another connection?

## Worked answers

1. Substitute $g=x^2$ into $g'+xg=2x+x^3$, then multiply by $2$. The result is $(4x+2x^3)\partial_x$. The derivative direction contributes no derivative of its coefficient.
2. In general it does not. For a scalar $h$, the proposed rule gives $D_X(hY)=fX(h)Y+hD_XY$. The required rule has $X(h)Y$ as its first term. These agree for all choices only if $f=1$ wherever nonzero directional derivatives can be chosen. Adding a tensor to a connection works; multiplying the entire connection by an arbitrary function does not.
