> [!TLDR]
> A derivative of a vector field must include changes in both its components and its basis.

## What changes when a vector changes?

A vector field assigns a tangent vector to each point of a smooth manifold. In a local frame $e_1,\ldots,e_n$, write the field as $V=V^a e_a$. Here $V^a$ are scalar functions, and a repeated upper and lower index means a sum from $1$ to $n$. A frame is a basis at every point of the region. Its basis vectors can change with position even when its component labels do not.

In Euclidean space, where vectors at different points have a standard identification, the product rule gives

$$
\frac{dV}{dt}=\frac{dV^a}{dt}e_a+V^a\frac{de_a}{dt}.
$$

The first term measures a change in the component list. The second term measures a change in the frame. Omitting that term can make a constant vector appear to change, or a changing vector appear to be constant.

On a general manifold, an ordinary derivative of $e_a$ is not yet defined: its values lie in different tangent spaces. A **connection** supplies the missing comparison rule. The Euclidean calculation below shows why such a rule needs basis terms. The formal definition is in [[21.02 Affine connections]].

## A rotating basis in the plane

Use polar coordinates $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$, with $r>0$ and an angular interval that gives a valid chart. Let $e_x,e_y$ be the fixed Cartesian unit vectors. The polar unit vectors are

$$
e_r=\cos\theta\,e_x+\sin\theta\,e_y,
\qquad
e_\theta=-\sin\theta\,e_x+\cos\theta\,e_y.
$$

Differentiate each coefficient with respect to $\theta$. This gives

$$
\partial_\theta e_r=e_\theta,
\qquad
\partial_\theta e_\theta=-e_r.
$$

For a curve $t\mapsto(r(t),\theta(t))$ and a field along it, $V=a(t)e_r+b(t)e_\theta$, apply these two identities:

$$
\frac{dV}{dt}
=(\dot a-b\dot\theta)e_r+(\dot b+a\dot\theta)e_\theta.
$$

A dot denotes a derivative with respect to $t$. Both basis terms disappear on a radial curve with constant $\theta$, but they are essential on a circle.

## Worked example: a constant Cartesian vector

Consider the unit circle $r=1$, $\theta=t$, and the constant vector $V=e_x$. Resolving it into the rotating frame gives $a=\cos t$ and $b=-\sin t$. Thus

$$
\dot a-b\dot\theta=-\sin t-(-\sin t)=0,
$$

$$
\dot b+a\dot\theta=-\cos t+\cos t=0.
$$

The derivative is zero, as required. Differentiating only the components would instead give $-\sin t\,e_r-\cos t\,e_\theta$, which is nonzero. The missing basis derivatives cancel that expression exactly.

## Coordinate basis versus unit frame

The polar coordinate vectors are $\partial_r=e_r$ and $\partial_\theta=r e_\theta$. The second vector has length $r$, rather than length one. Consequently,

$$
\partial_\theta\partial_r=\frac1r\partial_\theta,
\qquad
\partial_r\partial_\theta=\frac1r\partial_\theta,
\qquad
\partial_\theta\partial_\theta=-r\partial_r,
$$

where these Euclidean derivatives use the standard Cartesian comparison. The notation represents differentiation of vector fields, not multiplication of differential operators. An intrinsic formulation writes $\nabla$ in these expressions. Confusing $e_\theta$ with $\partial_\theta$ loses factors of $r$.

## Practice

1. On the unit circle with $\theta=t$, let $V=e_r$. Find its derivative and explain why its constant component list does not imply a zero derivative.
2. Along the radial curve $r=t>0$, $\theta=0$, let $W=\partial_\theta$. Find its Cartesian expression and its derivative.

## Worked answers

1. Here $a=1$, $b=0$, and $\dot\theta=1$. Therefore $dV/dt=e_\theta$. The local list $(1,0)$ is constant, but the first basis vector turns as $t$ increases. In Cartesian components the same result follows from $V=(\cos t,\sin t)$.
2. The coordinate vector is $\partial_\theta=r e_\theta=t e_y$ at $\theta=0$. Its derivative is $e_y$. Equivalently, $\nabla_{\partial_r}\partial_\theta=(1/r)\partial_\theta=e_y$. The unit vector $e_\theta$ is constant on this curve, but the coordinate vector has changing length.
