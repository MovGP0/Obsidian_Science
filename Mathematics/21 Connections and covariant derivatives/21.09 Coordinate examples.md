> [!TLDR]
> Flat polar coordinates have nonzero connection coefficients, but the curvature terms cancel exactly.

## The Euclidean plane in a curved coordinate grid

Consider a polar chart $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$ with $r>0$ and a suitable open angular interval. Differentiating gives

$$
dx=\cos\theta\,dr-r\sin\theta\,d\theta,
\qquad
dy=\sin\theta\,dr+r\cos\theta\,d\theta.
$$

Square these expressions and add them. The mixed terms cancel, while $\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1$. Hence the Euclidean metric is

$$
g=dx^2+dy^2=dr^2+r^2d\theta^2.
$$

The metric components are $g_{rr}=1$, $g_{r\theta}=0$, $g_{\theta\theta}=r^2$. This changing matrix does not indicate intrinsic curvature. It records the lengths of coordinate basis vectors: $\partial_r$ has length one, while $\partial_\theta$ has length $r$.

## Calculate the connection

Use the Levi-Civita formula from [[21.06 Levi-Civita connection]], with the derivative direction first in $\Gamma^k{}_{ij}$. The inverse metric has diagonal entries $1,r^{-2}$. Only $\partial_rg_{\theta\theta}=2r$ is nonzero, so

$$
\Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta}=-r,
\qquad
\Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta}=\Gamma^\theta{}_{\theta r}=\frac1r,
$$

and the other coefficients vanish. For example,

$$
\nabla_{\partial_\theta}\partial_\theta=-r\partial_r.
$$

As the angular coordinate changes, the tangent to its circular coordinate line changes direction. The nonzero derivative measures that change of the coordinate basis. Fig. 21.1 in [[21.01 The differentiation problem]] displays this rotation. Fig. 21.2 in [[21.08 Geodesics]] shows a straight path described in the same changing grid.

## Curvature is a different calculation

Fix the convention

$$
R(X,Y)Z=\nabla_X\nabla_YZ-\nabla_Y\nabla_XZ-\nabla_{[X,Y]}Z.
$$

Define $R^\ell{}_{kij}$ by $R(\partial_i,\partial_j)\partial_k=R^\ell{}_{kij}\partial_\ell$. Expanding both derivatives with the product rule gives

$$
R^\ell{}_{kij}
=\partial_i\Gamma^\ell{}_{jk}-\partial_j\Gamma^\ell{}_{ik}
+\Gamma^m{}_{jk}\Gamma^\ell{}_{im}
-\Gamma^m{}_{ik}\Gamma^\ell{}_{jm}.
$$

Here repeated $m$ is summed. Coordinate fields commute, so the bracket term is zero. Both derivatives of coefficients and products of coefficients are necessary. Keeping only the derivative terms would give a wrong conclusion even in the flat plane.

## Worked example: an explicit zero curvature component

Choose $\ell=r$, $k=\theta$, $i=r$, $j=\theta$. Substitute the coefficients carefully:

$$
\begin{aligned}
R^r{}_{\theta r\theta}
&=\partial_r\Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta}
-\partial_\theta\Gamma^r{}_{r\theta}
+\Gamma^m{}_{\theta\theta}\Gamma^r{}_{rm}
-\Gamma^m{}_{r\theta}\Gamma^r{}_{\theta m}\\
&=-1-0+0-\left(\frac1r\right)(-r)=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

The derivative contribution $-1$ cancels the product contribution $+1$. For the other potentially nonzero output on the input $\partial_r$, calculate

$$
R^\theta{}_{r r\theta}
=\partial_r(1/r)+\frac1{r^2}
=-\frac1{r^2}+\frac1{r^2}=0.
$$

The remaining output entries for these inputs vanish directly because each summand contains a zero coefficient or an angular derivative of an $r$-only function. Antisymmetry in $i,j$ handles the reversed order; equal $i,j$ gives zero. Thus every curvature component vanishes. Independently, in Cartesian coordinates all coefficients vanish everywhere, so the curvature is zero there. Since curvature is a tensor, it must also be zero in the polar chart.

## Singular charts and actual geometry

The factor $1/r$ is undefined at $r=0$, where the polar chart is not valid. The Euclidean metric and connection are smooth at the origin in Cartesian coordinates. A coordinate singularity is not a geometric singularity. Also, choosing zero coefficients at one point is weaker than finding a chart where they vanish throughout an open set: curvature uses their derivatives nearby.

## Practice

1. Compute $\nabla_\theta g_{r\theta}$ in the polar chart. Explain why it vanishes although two correction terms are nonzero.
2. Verify flatness directly by calculating $R(\partial_r,\partial_\theta)\partial_r$ from successive vector derivatives, without the component curvature formula.

## Worked answers

1. The partial derivative of $g_{r\theta}=0$ is zero. The two correction terms are $-\Gamma^\theta{}_{\theta r}g_{\theta\theta}=-r$ and $-\Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta}g_{rr}=+r$. Their sum is zero, as metric compatibility requires.
2. Since $\nabla_\theta\partial_r=(1/r)\partial_\theta$, differentiate radially to get $\nabla_r[(1/r)\partial_\theta]=-(1/r^2)\partial_\theta+(1/r)(1/r)\partial_\theta=0$. The other ordered derivative is $\nabla_\theta(\nabla_r\partial_r)=0$. The bracket vanishes. Their difference is therefore zero.
