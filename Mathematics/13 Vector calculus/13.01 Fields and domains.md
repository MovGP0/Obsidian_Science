title: "Fields and domains"

> [!TLDR]
> A field assigns a scalar or vector to each point of a domain; the domain determines where derivatives and integral theorems apply.

## Values attached to points

A **scalar field** on a set $U\subseteq\mathbb R^n$ is a function $f:U\to\mathbb R$. A **vector field** is a function $\mathbf F:U\to\mathbb R^n$. The input is a point, while the output is a number or a vector. For example,

$$
f(x,y)=x^2+y^2,\qquad \mathbf F(x,y)=(-y,x)
$$

are fields on all of $\mathbb R^2$. The scalar field has circular level sets. The vector field is tangent to those circles. An arrow drawing samples the values of a vector field at selected points; it does not specify all values unless a formula or another rule accompanies it.

Write $\mathbf F=(F_1,\ldots,F_n)$ for the component functions in Cartesian coordinates. A field is continuous or differentiable when its components have the stated property. The notation $C^k(U)$ means that all partial derivatives through order $k$ exist and are continuous on the open set $U$. Smooth means $C^k$ for every finite $k$.

Differentiability is a local condition. The derivative $D\mathbf F(p)$ is a linear map that gives

$$
\mathbf F(p+h)=\mathbf F(p)+D\mathbf F(p)h+o(\|h\|).
$$

The remainder divided by $\|h\|$ tends to zero as $h\to0$. This definition controls changes in all directions together. The existence of separate partial derivatives alone does not give that control. Continuous first partial derivatives are a useful sufficient condition.

## Why state the domain?

An open domain gives a small ball around each point in which all sufficiently short directional changes are allowed. On a boundary, a derivative may require an extension or a one-sided definition. Integral theorems often require a field to be $C^1$ on an open neighborhood of the whole integration region, including its boundary.

A domain is **path connected** if any two of its points can be joined by a continuous path in it. A path-connected domain is **simply connected** if every closed loop can be continuously contracted to a point while staying in the domain. A hole in a drawing can signal a failure of simple connectivity, but the dimension matters. Removing the origin from $\mathbb R^2$ leaves a noncontractible circular loop; removing the origin from $\mathbb R^3$ does not have the same effect on loops.

These distinctions matter in [[13.08 Potentials and path independence]]. Local derivative information can fail to determine a global integral.

## Worked example: a smooth field with an excluded point

Consider

$$
\mathbf G(x,y)=\left(-\frac{y}{x^2+y^2},\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\right),
\qquad U=\mathbb R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}.
$$

Every denominator is nonzero on $U$, so both components are smooth there. At a point of radius $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$,

$$
\|\mathbf G(x,y)\|^2
=\frac{y^2+x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}=\frac1{r^2}.
$$

Thus $\|\mathbf G\|=1/r$. It is unbounded near the excluded origin and has no continuous extension there. On the unit circle, let $\gamma(t)=(\cos t,\sin t)$ for $0\le t\le2\pi$. Then

$$
\mathbf G(\gamma(t))=(-\sin t,\cos t)=\gamma'(t),
$$

so its circulation is $\int_0^{2\pi}1\,dt=2\pi$. This calculation is valid even though the field has no value at the center. A theorem applied to the filled disk would need that missing value and suitable regularity there.

## Common errors

Do not cancel a denominator and silently restore an excluded point: an extension is a new field with a new domain. Do not infer a potential from a vanishing curl without a domain condition. Also distinguish a singular formula from a coordinate singularity: polar coordinates fail at the origin even for many fields that are smooth in Cartesian coordinates.

## Practice

1. Find the largest open subset of $\mathbb R^2$ on which $f(x,y)=\log(4-x^2-y^2)$ is real and smooth. Can its value extend continuously to the boundary circle?
2. Let $\mathbf H(x,y)=(x,y)/(x^2+y^2)$. Find its magnitude and determine whether it is bounded on $1<\sqrt{x^2+y^2}<2$.

## Worked answers

1. The logarithm requires $4-x^2-y^2>0$, so the domain is the open disk $x^2+y^2<4$. Composition of smooth functions proves smoothness there. As $x^2+y^2\to4$ from inside, the logarithm tends to $-\infty$. Thus no finite continuous extension exists on the boundary circle.
2. Squaring the magnitude gives $(x^2+y^2)/(x^2+y^2)^2=1/r^2$, so $\|\mathbf H\|=1/r$. On the annulus, $1/2<\|\mathbf H\|<1$. It is bounded, although it does not attain either bound on this open set.
