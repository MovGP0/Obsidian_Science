> [!TLDR]
> Curl records local oriented circulation; its component in a chosen normal direction is circulation per unit area in the limit.

## Definition in oriented Euclidean space

Fix a right-handed Cartesian coordinate system $(x,y,z)$ in $\mathbb R^3$. For a $C^1$ vector field $\mathbf F=(P,Q,R)$, define

$$
\nabla\times\mathbf F
=\left(\partial_yR-\partial_zQ,
\partial_zP-\partial_xR,
\partial_xQ-\partial_yP\right).
$$

This vector is the **curl**. The cross product notation records both the component pattern and the orientation. For vectors $a,b$, $a\times b$ is perpendicular to their plane, has magnitude equal to their parallelogram area, and points according to the right-hand rule. Reversing the order changes its sign. The familiar vector-valued curl relies on three dimensions and an orientation; it should not be transferred unchanged to arbitrary dimensions.

For a unit normal $n$, orient a small plane loop by the right-hand rule: it is counterclockwise when viewed from the tip of $n$ toward the plane. Then

$$
(\nabla\times\mathbf F)(p)\cdot n
=\lim_{\varepsilon\to0}
\frac{\displaystyle\oint_{C_\varepsilon}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r}
{\operatorname{Area}(D_\varepsilon)},
$$

where $D_\varepsilon$ is a shrinking disk centered at $p$ in that plane and $C_\varepsilon=\partial D_\varepsilon$. Stokes' theorem and continuity justify this local limit. The sign of the loop is essential.

## A component from a small rectangle

Take a rectangle in a plane parallel to the $xy$-plane. Traverse its boundary counterclockwise as viewed from $+z$. On the horizontal edges, subtract the top $P$ contribution from the bottom one. Their first-order difference is $-\partial_yP\,\Delta x\Delta y$. On the vertical edges, right minus left gives $\partial_xQ\,\Delta x\Delta y$. The total divided by area tends to

$$
\partial_xQ-\partial_yP.
$$

This is the $z$ component of curl. Rectangles in the other coordinate planes give the remaining two components. This derivation also explains why a difference of cross derivatives appears, while [[13.03 Divergence]] uses a sum of same-coordinate derivatives.

## Worked example: circulation and orientation

Let $\mathbf F(x,y,z)=(-y,x,0)$. Then

$$
\nabla\times\mathbf F=(0,0,1-(-1))=(0,0,2).
$$

On a circle of radius $a>0$ in $z=0$, use $\gamma(t)=(a\cos t,a\sin t,0)$ for $0\le t\le2\pi$. This orientation corresponds to normal $+e_z$. Direct substitution gives

$$
\mathbf F(\gamma(t))=(-a\sin t,a\cos t,0),
\qquad \gamma'(t)=(-a\sin t,a\cos t,0).
$$

Their dot product is $a^2$, so the circulation is $2\pi a^2$. Dividing by disk area $\pi a^2$ gives $2$, as predicted by curl. Reversing the traversal makes the integral negative. Consistently replacing the normal by $-e_z$ also changes the curl component to $-2$.

## Differential identities and their limits

If $f$ is $C^2$, then

$$
\nabla\times\nabla f=0.
$$

For example, its last component is $\partial_x\partial_y f-\partial_y\partial_x f=0$ by equality of continuous mixed partial derivatives. If $\mathbf F$ is $C^2$, the corresponding cancellation gives $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F)=0$.

The reverse of the first identity needs more than a component calculation. A curl-free field can have nonzero circulation around a hole in its domain. [[13.08 Potentials and path independence]] computes this obstruction. Also, a vector field can be curl-free without being constant: $(2x,2y,2z)$ is a simple example.

## Practice

1. Find the curl of $\mathbf F=(yz,xz,xy)$. Identify a scalar potential.
2. For $\mathbf F=(0,x,0)$, calculate circulation around the unit square in $z=0$ with normal $+e_z$.

## Worked answers

1. The components are $(x-x,y-y,z-z)=(0,0,0)$. The function $f(x,y,z)=xyz$ has partial derivatives $(yz,xz,xy)$, so $\mathbf F=\nabla f$ on all of $\mathbb R^3$.
2. Along horizontal edges $dy=0$, so $\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=x\,dy=0$. Along the right edge, $x=1$ and $y$ increases from $0$ to $1$, giving $1$. Along the left edge, $x=0$, giving zero. Total circulation is $1$. Curl is $(0,0,1)$ and the square area is $1$, in agreement with Stokes' theorem.
