title: "Potentials and path independence"
---

> [!TLDR]
> A vector field has a scalar potential exactly when its line integrals are path independent; a hole can prevent this even when curl vanishes.

## Three equivalent properties

Let $U\subseteq\mathbb R^n$ be open and path connected, and let $\mathbf F$ be continuous on $U$. Consider piecewise $C^1$ paths in $U$. The following properties are equivalent:

1. There is a $C^1$ scalar function $f$ on $U$ with $\mathbf F=\nabla f$.
2. Every integral $\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r$ depends only on the two endpoints.
3. Every closed-path integral is zero.

A field with the first property is a **gradient field**, or conservative field, and $f$ is a scalar potential. The associated differential form $F_1\,dx_1+\cdots+F_n\,dx_n$ is then **exact**, meaning it equals $df$.

The gradient theorem proves the first implication. For path independence, compare a path from $A$ to $B$ with the constant path when $A=B$; closed integrals vanish. Conversely, concatenate one path from $A$ to $B$ with the reverse of another. Zero integral on this closed path forces the two integrals to agree.

To construct a potential from path independence, fix $p_0\in U$ and set

$$
f(p)=\int_{p_0}^{p}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r.
$$

The value is well-defined. For small $h$, a coordinate segment from $p$ to $p+he_i$ stays in $U$, so

$$
f(p+he_i)-f(p)=\int_0^h F_i(p+te_i)\,dt.
$$

Divide by $h$ and use continuity to obtain $\partial_i f(p)=F_i(p)$. These continuous partial derivatives make $f$ a $C^1$ potential. Two potentials differ by a constant on a connected domain because their difference has zero derivative along every path.

## Worked example: recover a potential

On all of $\mathbb R^2$, let $\mathbf F=(2xy+3,x^2+4y)$. The cross derivatives agree: $\partial_yF_1=2x=\partial_xF_2$. To find a potential, integrate its first required partial derivative with respect to $x$:

$$
f(x,y)=x^2y+3x+g(y).
$$

Differentiate this expression with respect to $y$ and compare it with $F_2$:

$$
f_y=x^2+g'(y)=x^2+4y.
$$

Hence $g'(y)=4y$ and $g(y)=2y^2+C$. Thus $f=x^2y+3x+2y^2+C$. The integral from $(0,0)$ to $(1,2)$ is $f(1,2)-f(0,0)=2+3+8=13$. The function of the remaining variable was necessary; an arbitrary numerical constant after the first integration would have lost the $2y^2$ term.

## Closed does not always mean exact

For a $C^1$ field in three dimensions, vanishing curl is necessary for a potential, since mixed partial derivatives of the potential agree. In a plane, the corresponding condition is $\partial_xF_2-\partial_yF_1=0$. Such a field or associated one-form is called closed. On a simply connected open domain, closed $C^1$ one-forms are exact. A star-shaped domain is a useful stronger sufficient condition: all line segments from one fixed center stay in the domain.

The domain condition cannot be discarded. On $U=\mathbb R^2\setminus\{0\}$, take

$$
\mathbf G=\left(-\frac{y}{r^2},\frac{x}{r^2}\right),\qquad r^2=x^2+y^2.
$$

Direct differentiation gives

$$
\partial_xG_2=\frac{y^2-x^2}{r^4}
=\partial_yG_1,
$$

so the plane curl is zero. Yet on $\gamma(t)=(a\cos t,a\sin t)$, $a>0$,

$$
\mathbf G(\gamma(t))=\frac1a(-\sin t,\cos t),
\qquad \mathbf G(\gamma(t))\cdot\gamma'(t)=1.
$$

The closed integral is $2\pi$. Thus no single-valued potential exists on $U$. Locally an angle function is a potential, but an angle increases by $2\pi$ after one circuit, so it cannot supply a global real-valued function there.

![[math-13-hole-circulation.svg|**Fig. 13.2** The field (−y, x)/(x² + y²) has zero curl away from the excluded origin, but a counterclockwise circle has circulation 2π. A slit permits one continuous angle branch.]]

The slit domain obtained by removing the nonpositive $x$-axis admits the branch $-\pi<\theta<\pi$. Its gradient is $\mathbf G$. The circle used above is no longer a closed path entirely inside this slit domain.

## Practice

1. Find a potential for $(2x\cos y,-x^2\sin y)$ and evaluate its integral from $(0,0)$ to $(2,\pi)$.
2. Does $\mathbf H=(x,y)/(x^2+y^2)$ have a potential on the punctured plane? Explain why the domain's hole does not decide this alone.

## Worked answers

1. Integrating the first component gives $f=x^2\cos y+g(y)$. Its $y$ derivative is $-x^2\sin y+g'(y)$, so $g'=0$. The integral is $f(2,\pi)-f(0,0)=-4-0=-4$.
2. Set $f=\tfrac12\log(x^2+y^2)=\log r$, which is single-valued and smooth for $r>0$. Its partial derivatives are $x/r^2$ and $y/r^2$. Hence $\mathbf H=\nabla f$ even on this nonsimply connected domain. Simple connectivity is sufficient to make every closed field exact; it is not necessary for a particular field to be exact.
