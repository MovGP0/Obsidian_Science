> [!TLDR]
> The gradient represents a scalar differential by an inner product and points in the direction of greatest local increase.

## From a differential to a vector

Let $f:U\to\mathbb R$ be differentiable on an open set $U\subseteq\mathbb R^n$. Its differential at $p$, written $df_p$, is the linear function that satisfies

$$
f(p+h)=f(p)+df_p(h)+o(\|h\|).
$$

With the Euclidean inner product $v\cdot w=\sum_i v_iw_i$, there is a unique vector $\nabla f(p)$ such that

$$
df_p(v)=\nabla f(p)\cdot v\quad\text{for every }v\in\mathbb R^n.
$$

To see uniqueness, subtract two representing vectors and take $v$ equal to their difference. Its squared norm must be zero. Existence follows by writing $v=\sum_i v_ie_i$: linearity gives $df_p(v)=\sum_i v_i df_p(e_i)$. Since $df_p(e_i)=\partial_i f(p)$,

$$
\nabla f=(\partial_1 f,\ldots,\partial_n f).
$$

The differential is naturally a linear function on displacement vectors. The gradient is the vector chosen to represent that function by a specified inner product. This distinction becomes essential when the inner product or coordinates change.

## Directional change and level sets

For a unit vector $u$, the directional derivative is

$$
D_u f(p)=\lim_{t\to0}\frac{f(p+tu)-f(p)}t
=\nabla f(p)\cdot u.
$$

The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives $D_u f(p)\le\|\nabla f(p)\|$. If the gradient is nonzero, equality occurs at $u=\nabla f(p)/\|\nabla f(p)\|$. The opposite unit vector gives the greatest decrease. A nonunit direction introduces its length into the derivative, so comparisons of rates require unit vectors.

Suppose $\gamma(t)$ stays in the level set $f=c$. The chain rule gives

$$
0=\frac{d}{dt}f(\gamma(t))
=\nabla f(\gamma(t))\cdot\gamma'(t).
$$

Thus the gradient is normal to every tangent vector of a regular level set. At a point where $\nabla f=0$, this calculation supplies no nonzero normal and no preferred ascent direction.

## Worked example

Take $f(x,y)=x^2+3y^2$ and $p=(1,1)$. First differentiate:

$$
\nabla f(x,y)=(2x,6y),\qquad \nabla f(p)=(2,6).
$$

For $u=(3/5,4/5)$, whose norm is one,

$$
D_u f(p)=2\frac35+6\frac45=6.
$$

The largest directional derivative is $\sqrt{2^2+6^2}=2\sqrt{10}$, attained at $(1,3)/\sqrt{10}$. The level curve through $p$ is $x^2+3y^2=4$. Its tangent line follows from orthogonality:

$$
(2,6)\cdot(x-1,y-1)=0,
\qquad x+3y=4.
$$

The linear approximation is $f(1+h,1+k)\approx4+2h+6k$. Direct expansion adds the remainder $h^2+3k^2$, which is second order. This confirms the derivative calculation and identifies its approximation error.

## Dependence on the inner product

If the inner product is $\langle v,w\rangle_G=v^TGw$ for a symmetric positive definite matrix $G$, let $a$ be the column of Cartesian partial derivatives. The gradient $g$ must satisfy $g^TGv=a^Tv$ for all $v$. Therefore $Gg=a$, so

$$
\operatorname{grad}_G f=G^{-1}a.
$$

The scalar differential stays fixed while its representing vector changes. In orthogonal curvilinear coordinates, scale factors express this same issue; see [[13.09 Coordinate expressions]].

## Practice

1. For $f(x,y,z)=xy+z^2$, find the greatest rate of increase at $(1,2,-1)$ and a unit direction that attains it.
2. For $f(x,y)=x+y$ with $G=\operatorname{diag}(4,1)$, reconstruct the gradient and verify its defining equality.

## Worked answers

1. Differentiation gives $\nabla f=(y,x,2z)$, so the vector at the point is $(2,1,-2)$. Its norm is $\sqrt{4+1+4}=3$. The unit direction $(2,1,-2)/3$ attains rate $3$ because its dot product with the gradient equals $9/3$.
2. The partial-derivative column is $a=(1,1)^T$. Multiplication by $G^{-1}$ gives $g=(1/4,1)^T$. For $v=(v_1,v_2)$, $g^TGv=(1/4)4v_1+v_2=v_1+v_2=df(v)$, as required.
