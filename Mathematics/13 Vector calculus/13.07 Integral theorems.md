> [!TLDR]
> Integral theorems convert derivatives inside a region into oriented integrals on its boundary, under stated smoothness and domain conditions.

## The gradient theorem

Let $f$ be $C^1$ on an open set containing a piecewise $C^1$ curve $\gamma:[a,b]\to\mathbb R^n$. The chain rule gives $(f\circ\gamma)'=\nabla f(\gamma)\cdot\gamma'$. Integrating each smooth piece and canceling the shared endpoint values proves

$$
\int_C\nabla f\cdot d\mathbf r=f(\gamma(b))-f(\gamma(a)).
$$

This is the fundamental theorem for line integrals. Closed curves give zero because their endpoints agree. No simple-connectivity assumption is needed once a single-valued potential $f$ is already given on a neighborhood of the curve.

For $f=x^2y+z$, a curve from $A=(0,1,2)$ to $B=(2,3,1)$ has gradient integral $f(B)-f(A)=13-2=11$, regardless of its shape within the domain.

## Green's theorem in the plane

Let $D$ be a bounded plane region with piecewise smooth boundary, with finitely many boundary components and no crossings. Let $P,Q$ be $C^1$ on an open neighborhood of $\overline D$. Give $\partial D$ the positive orientation: the region stays to the left as the boundary is traversed. The outer boundary is counterclockwise; inner hole boundaries are clockwise. Then

$$
\oint_{\partial D}P\,dx+Q\,dy
=\iint_D(\partial_xQ-\partial_yP)\,dA.
$$

For a rectangle, the fundamental theorem of calculus on opposite edges produces the two derivatives. Subdivision extends this cancellation idea to suitable regions: interior edges occur twice with opposite orientations. This explains the boundary structure, while the theorem's full proof also controls approximation at curved boundaries.

The flux version follows by applying the same theorem to $P=-F_2$, $Q=F_1$:

$$
\oint_{\partial D}\mathbf F\cdot n\,ds
=\iint_D(\partial_xF_1+\partial_yF_2)\,dA,
$$

where $n$ is the outward plane unit normal. On the unit disk, $P=-y$, $Q=x$ gives derivative difference $2$, so circulation is $2\pi$.

## The divergence theorem

Let $V\subset\mathbb R^3$ be a bounded region with piecewise smooth boundary, and let $\mathbf F$ be $C^1$ on an open neighborhood of $\overline V$. Use the outward unit normal on every boundary component, including inward-facing normals on cavity surfaces. Then

$$
\iint_{\partial V}\mathbf F\cdot n\,dS
=\iiint_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV.
$$

For $\mathbf F=(x,y,z)$ and a ball of radius $a$, divergence is $3$ and volume is $4\pi a^3/3$. The theorem gives flux $4\pi a^3$. A direct check uses $n=(x,y,z)/a$ on the sphere: $\mathbf F\cdot n=a$, so flux is $a(4\pi a^2)$, the same result.

The essential condition is regularity throughout the filled region. Smoothness only on the boundary is insufficient. For $\mathbf F=(x,y,z)/(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}$, divergence is zero away from the origin, but flux through a centered sphere is $4\pi$. The origin prevents application to the full ball. On a shell, the two boundary contributions cancel with the correct outward orientations.

## Stokes' theorem

Let $S$ be a compact oriented, piecewise smooth surface with piecewise smooth boundary. Let $\mathbf F$ be $C^1$ on an open neighborhood of $S$. Choose the boundary orientation induced by the surface normal using the right-hand rule. Then

$$
\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
=\iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot n\,dS.
$$

The surface must have a consistent orientation. A surface with no global normal, such as a Möbius band, does not satisfy this oriented-surface formulation. On surfaces with holes, every boundary component must be included.

For $\mathbf F=(-y/2,x/2,0)$, curl is $(0,0,1)$. The upward paraboloid patch $z=1-x^2-y^2$ over the unit disk has vector area $n\,dS=(2x,2y,1)\,dx\,dy$. Its curl integral is therefore $\pi$. Its boundary is the unit circle in $z=0$, counterclockwise from above. Direct substitution gives $\mathbf F(\gamma)\cdot\gamma'=1/2$, and integration gives $\pi$. The curved surface and the flat disk give the same value because they share this oriented boundary and the field is smooth near both.

## Choose the theorem carefully

Identify the integral type before selecting a theorem. Gradient theorem needs an actual scalar potential. Green's theorem needs a plane region. Divergence theorem needs a closed boundary of a volume. Stokes' theorem needs a surface with the given curve as its full boundary. Check excluded points before changing the integration set; [[13.08 Potentials and path independence]] gives a plane counterexample.

## Practice

1. Use Green's theorem to find $\oint_C(-y^3\,dx+x^3\,dy)$ around the unit circle counterclockwise.
2. Use the divergence theorem for $(x^3,y^3,z^3)$ on the unit cube $[0,1]^3$.

## Worked answers

1. The derivative difference is $3x^2+3y^2$. In polar coordinates the area integral becomes $3\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1 r^3\,dr\,d\theta=3(2\pi)(1/4)=3\pi/2$. The circle bounds a disk on which the polynomial field is smooth, so the hypotheses hold.
2. Divergence is $3x^2+3y^2+3z^2$. Each term integrates to $1$ on the cube: for example $\int_0^1 3x^2\,dx=1$, with unit factors from the other variables. Total outward flux is $3$. Directly, only the three faces at coordinate $1$ contribute; each contributes $1$.
