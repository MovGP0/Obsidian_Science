title: "Divergence"

> [!TLDR]
> Divergence measures the local outward signed flux of a vector field per unit volume.

## Definition and sign

Let $\mathbf F=(P,Q,R)$ be a $C^1$ vector field on an open subset of $\mathbb R^3$. In Cartesian coordinates its **divergence** is the scalar field

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F
=\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}
+\frac{\partial Q}{\partial y}
+\frac{\partial R}{\partial z}.
$$

In $n$ dimensions the definition is $\operatorname{div}\mathbf F=\sum_{i=1}^n\partial_iF_i$. It is the trace of the derivative matrix $D\mathbf F$. The off-diagonal entries do not enter the trace, but they can still change the field's direction and shape.

For an oriented surface, the signed flux is $\iint\mathbf F\cdot n\,dS$, where $n$ is a unit normal. Positive contributions occur where the field has a component in the normal direction. For the boundary of a region, take $n$ outward. A negative divergence means that the total inward contribution locally exceeds the total outward contribution. This language refers to vector geometry and integration; no physical interpretation is required.

## Derive the local expression

Take a small box of side lengths $h_x,h_y,h_z$ around $p$. On the two faces perpendicular to the $x$-axis, the outward normals are $e_x$ and $-e_x$. The first-order difference between their contributions is

$$
\left[P\left(p+\frac{h_x}2e_x\right)
-P\left(p-\frac{h_x}2e_x\right)\right]h_yh_z
\approx \partial_xP(p)h_xh_yh_z.
$$

The minus sign comes from the normal on the left face. The other face pairs contribute $\partial_yQ(p)$ and $\partial_zR(p)$ times the volume. Divide the total by $h_xh_yh_z$ and shrink the box. Continuity of the first derivatives controls the approximation errors and gives the stated divergence.

For balls $B_\varepsilon(p)$ contained in the domain, the same quantity satisfies

$$
\operatorname{div}\mathbf F(p)
=\lim_{\varepsilon\to0}
\frac{1}{\operatorname{Vol}(B_\varepsilon(p))}
\iint_{\partial B_\varepsilon(p)}\mathbf F\cdot n\,dS.
$$

The divergence theorem and continuity of divergence justify this form: the quotient is the average of divergence over the shrinking ball. The face calculation explains why the Cartesian sum has this local meaning.

## Worked example: exact flux on a box

Let $\mathbf F(x,y,z)=(x^2,y,z)$ and let $B=[0,a]\times[0,b]\times[0,c]$, with $a,b,c>0$. Differentiation gives

$$
\operatorname{div}\mathbf F=2x+2.
$$

Compute the outward flux directly. The face $x=a$ contributes $a^2bc$; the face $x=0$ contributes zero. The face $y=b$ contributes $abc$; the face $y=0$ contributes zero. The $z$ faces similarly give $abc$. Hence total flux is $a^2bc+2abc$.

Integrating the divergence over the box gives the same value:

$$
\iiint_B(2x+2)\,dV
=bc\int_0^a(2x+2)\,dx
=bc(a^2+2a).
$$

This verifies both the component calculation and the outward normal signs for this example. It does not prove the theorem for all regions; the general result appears in [[13.07 Integral theorems]].

## Rules and limitations

For a scalar $f$ and vector field $\mathbf F$, the product rule gives

$$
\operatorname{div}(f\mathbf F)
=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf F+f\operatorname{div}\mathbf F.
$$

Zero divergence does not mean that the field is zero, constant, or curl-free. For example $(-y,x,0)$ has zero divergence and nonzero curl. Also, the Cartesian formula cannot be applied to polar or spherical components without scale factors. A field with an excluded singularity requires special care before a theorem is applied across that point.

## Practice

1. Find the divergence of $(xy,yz,zx)$ and its value at $(1,2,3)$.
2. Let $\mathbf F=(x,y,z)$ and $f=x^2+y^2+z^2$. Find $\operatorname{div}(f\mathbf F)$ by the product rule.

## Worked answers

1. The required derivatives are $\partial_x(xy)=y$, $\partial_y(yz)=z$, and $\partial_z(zx)=x$. Thus divergence is $x+y+z$, which equals $6$ at the stated point.
2. Here $\nabla f=2(x,y,z)$, so $\nabla f\cdot\mathbf F=2f$. Also $\operatorname{div}\mathbf F=3$. Therefore the product rule gives $2f+3f=5(x^2+y^2+z^2)$. The result varies with position; divergence is itself a scalar field.
