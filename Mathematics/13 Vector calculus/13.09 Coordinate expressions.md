> [!TLDR]
> Curvilinear coordinates require length, area, and volume factors because coordinate increments do not usually have unit length.

## Orthogonal coordinates and scale factors

Let $X(q_1,q_2,q_3)$ be a smooth, locally one-to-one coordinate map into Euclidean three-space. Suppose its coordinate tangent vectors are mutually perpendicular and nonzero. Define the scale factors and orthonormal basis by

$$
h_i=\left\|\frac{\partial X}{\partial q_i}\right\|,
\qquad e_i=\frac1{h_i}\frac{\partial X}{\partial q_i}.
$$

Assume $(e_1,e_2,e_3)$ is right-handed. A coordinate displacement is

$$
d\mathbf r=\sum_{i=1}^3 h_ie_i\,dq_i,
\qquad ds^2=\sum_{i=1}^3h_i^2\,dq_i^2.
$$

The volume element is $dV=h_1h_2h_3\,dq_1dq_2dq_3$, and the area of a coordinate face with constant $q_i$ has factor $h_jh_k$. These factors describe geometry, not a change to the scalar function itself.

Write $\mathbf F=F_1e_1+F_2e_2+F_3e_3$. Here $F_i$ are components in the orthonormal basis. They are not the coefficients of $\partial X/\partial q_i$; those coordinate-basis coefficients would be $F_i/h_i$. Always state which convention is used.

## Gradient, divergence, and curl

Since $df=\sum_i(\partial f/\partial q_i)dq_i$ must equal $\nabla f\cdot d\mathbf r$, coefficient comparison gives

$$
\nabla f=\sum_{i=1}^3\frac1{h_i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}e_i.
$$

For divergence, opposite faces of a small coordinate box carry the quantities $h_jh_kF_i$. Subtract their values, divide by volume, and shrink the box. The result is

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F
=\frac1{h_1h_2h_3}\left[
\partial_{q_1}(h_2h_3F_1)
+\partial_{q_2}(h_3h_1F_2)
+\partial_{q_3}(h_1h_2F_3)\right].
$$

For curl, compare the circulation around a small coordinate rectangle with its area. For example, the $e_1$ component is

$$
(\nabla\times\mathbf F)_1
=\frac{\partial_{q_2}(h_3F_3)-\partial_{q_3}(h_2F_2)}{h_2h_3}.
$$

The other components follow by cyclically replacing $(1,2,3)$ with $(2,3,1)$ and $(3,1,2)$. The line factors belong inside the derivatives because they vary with position. These formulas require orthogonal coordinates; for a general coordinate map, metric or Jacobian formulas are needed instead.

## Cylindrical coordinates

Use $X(r,\theta,z)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,z)$ with $r>0$. Differentiation gives $h_r=1$, $h_\theta=r$, $h_z=1$. Thus

$$
dV=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz,\qquad
\nabla f=e_r f_r+e_\theta\frac{f_\theta}{r}+e_z f_z,
$$

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F
=\frac1r\partial_r(rF_r)
+\frac1r\partial_\theta F_\theta+\partial_zF_z.
$$

The orthonormal vectors are $e_r=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0)$ and $e_\theta=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta,0)$. They change with $\theta$. This basis change is one reason that simply adding component derivatives would fail.

For example, let $f=r^2\sin\theta$. Then

$$
\nabla f=2r\sin\theta\,e_r+r\cos\theta\,e_\theta.
$$

At $r=2$, $\theta=\pi/6$, its components are $2$ and $\sqrt3$, and its norm is $\sqrt7$. A change $d\theta$ has length $r\,d\theta$, so $f_\theta$ alone is not the derivative per unit distance.

## Worked example: radial fields and spherical volume

Use spherical coordinates $(r,\theta,\phi)$ with $\theta$ measured down from the positive $z$-axis and $\phi$ the azimuth:

$$
X=(r\sin\theta\cos\phi,r\sin\theta\sin\phi,r\cos\theta).
$$

On $r>0$, $0<\theta<\pi$, the scale factors are $1,r,r\sin\theta$. Therefore $dV=r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi$. For a radial field $\mathbf F=A(r)e_r$, the divergence formula reduces to

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=\frac1{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}(r^2A(r)).
$$

If $A(r)=r$, divergence is $r^{-2}(3r^2)=3$, as for the Cartesian field $(x,y,z)$. If $A(r)=r^{-2}$, it is zero for $r>0$. Nevertheless the outward flux through a sphere is $A(r)4\pi r^2=4\pi$. No contradiction occurs: this second field has no smooth extension to the origin, so the divergence theorem cannot fill the sphere with a ball in its domain.

Coordinate failures need separate interpretation. The spherical basis fails at the poles, while the field $(x,y,z)$ is smooth there. The radial field $e_r/r^2$, by contrast, has a genuine singularity at the origin. A coordinate formula with a denominator does not decide this without examining the geometric field.

## Practice

1. In cylindrical coordinates, find the divergence of $\mathbf F=r^2e_r+rz\,e_z$ for $r>0$.
2. Find the cylindrical $e_z$ component of curl for $\mathbf F=r e_\theta$. Check it in Cartesian coordinates.

## Worked answers

1. The azimuthal component is zero. Thus divergence is $r^{-1}\partial_r(r^3)+\partial_z(rz)=3r+r=4r$. Differentiating $F_r$ without the factor $r$ would miss one $r$ term.
2. The cyclic curl formula gives $(\nabla\times\mathbf F)_z=r^{-1}[\partial_r(rF_\theta)-\partial_\theta F_r]=r^{-1}\partial_r(r^2)=2$. Since $r e_\theta=(-y,x,0)$, the Cartesian curl is $(0,0,2)$. The apparent coordinate restriction at $r=0$ is removable for this particular field.
