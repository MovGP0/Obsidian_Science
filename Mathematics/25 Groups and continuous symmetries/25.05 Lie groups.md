> [!TLDR]
> A Lie group is both a smooth manifold and a group whose multiplication and inverse vary smoothly.

## Algebra and smooth geometry together

A **Lie group** $G$ is a finite-dimensional smooth manifold with a group structure such that multiplication $m:G\times G\to G$, $m(g,h)=gh$, and inversion $j:G\to G$, $j(g)=g^{-1}$, are smooth maps. Use the usual convention that manifolds are Hausdorff and second countable. Smooth means that coordinate expressions have derivatives of every order.

A coordinate chart assigns real coordinates to an open part of the manifold. If $G$ has dimension $d$, a chart near its identity assigns $d$ real parameters to nearby group elements. The group law then becomes a smooth function of these local parameters. The definition requires compatibility between geometry and algebra; a set with a group law and an unrelated smooth structure is not enough.

Translation by a fixed element, $L_g(h)=gh$, is a smooth bijection with smooth inverse $L_{g^{-1}}$. Thus every point has the same local geometry as the identity. This is why derivatives at the identity contain useful information about the entire group. They form its Lie algebra; see [[25.06 Generators and Lie algebras]].

## Worked example: the general linear group

View $M_n(\mathbb R)$ as $\mathbb R^{n^2}$ by listing matrix entries. Since determinant is a continuous polynomial, the condition $\det A\ne0$ defines an open subset. Therefore $GL_n(\mathbb R)$ is a smooth manifold of dimension $n^2$.

Every entry of $AB$ is a polynomial in the entries of $A$ and $B$, so multiplication is smooth. The formula

$$
A^{-1}=\frac{\operatorname{adj}(A)}{\det A}
$$

expresses each inverse entry as a polynomial divided by a nonzero polynomial on this open set. It is therefore smooth. This verifies both required maps directly. The dimension is $n^2$, not the number of free eigenvalues: eigenvalues do not specify a matrix or supply coordinates near every matrix.

The group has more than one connected component. A continuous path of invertible real matrices cannot change the sign of determinant, since a sign change would require determinant zero. A Lie group need not be connected.

## Local angles are not a global coordinate

Consider the circle group

$$
U(1)=\{z=x+iy:x^2+y^2=1\}.
$$

It is a one-dimensional smooth manifold. Near $1$, the map $\theta\mapsto e^{i\theta}$ gives a chart parameter, for example for $-\pi/2<\theta<\pi/2$. In these coordinates, multiplication near the identity is angle addition whenever the product remains in the chosen arc. The inverse corresponds to $\theta\mapsto-\theta$.

The same formula gives every circle point if all real angles are allowed, but it is not a global coordinate chart:

$$
e^{i(\theta+2\pi k)}=e^{i\theta}\quad(k\in\mathbb Z).
$$

It is not one-to-one. Restricting to $[-\pi,\pi)$ gives one angle for each point, but introduces a jump at $-1$. This interval is not an open coordinate domain, and the inverse angle function is not continuous there. A surjective parameterization and a global coordinate system are different claims.

For example, multiplying the points with principal angles $3\pi/4$ and $3\pi/4$ gives angle $3\pi/2$. In the principal range $[-\pi,\pi)$, the result is $-\pi/2$. The jump is in the selected angle value. The product point on the circle changes smoothly.

## Smooth matrix subgroups

The orthogonal and unitary groups in [[25.04 Matrix groups]] are Lie groups with their usual embedded smooth structures. One general theorem states that a subgroup closed in a finite-dimensional Lie group is an embedded Lie subgroup. This theorem needs a proof beyond a few defining equations; it is stated here to identify its scope, not proved by parameter counting.

The closed-subgroup hypothesis is a sufficient condition for this embedded structure. An arbitrary subgroup can have a complicated inherited topology. For example, rotations by integer multiples of an irrational angle form a dense subgroup of the circle. One must specify the topology and smooth structure before calling a subgroup a Lie group in a particular sense.

## Practice

1. Verify that the positive real numbers under multiplication form a one-dimensional Lie group.
2. Why does the determinant sign prevent a path in $GL_2(\mathbb R)$ from joining $I_2$ to $\operatorname{diag}(-1,1)$?

### Worked answers

1. The set $(0,\infty)$ is an open one-dimensional manifold. The maps $(a,b)\mapsto ab$ and $a\mapsto1/a$ are smooth there. The identity is $1$ and all inverses remain positive. The global coordinate $u=\log a$ converts multiplication to addition, since $\log(ab)=\log a+\log b$.
2. Along such a path $A(t)$, the real function $\det A(t)$ would be continuous, with endpoint values $1$ and $-1$. The intermediate value theorem would give a point with determinant zero. This contradicts the requirement that every matrix on the path be invertible.
