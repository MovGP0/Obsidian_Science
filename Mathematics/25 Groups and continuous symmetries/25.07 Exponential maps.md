title: "Exponential maps"

> [!TLDR]
> The exponential map converts a Lie algebra element into a one-parameter subgroup and gives coordinates near the identity, with possible global failures of uniqueness and coverage.

## The matrix exponential

For a real or complex square matrix $X$, define

$$
e^X=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{X^k}{k!}
=I+X+\frac{X^2}{2!}+\cdots.
$$

The series converges absolutely in a submultiplicative matrix norm, since $\|X^k\|\le\|X\|^k$ and the scalar exponential series converges. It therefore defines every finite square matrix, including singular ones. The result $e^X$ is always invertible: $e^Xe^{-X}=I$.

For a matrix Lie group $G$ with Lie algebra $\mathfrak g$, the **exponential map** is $\exp:\mathfrak g\to G$, $X\mapsto e^X$. For fixed $X$, its scaled curve satisfies

$$
e^{(s+t)X}=e^{sX}e^{tX},\qquad
\frac{d}{dt}e^{tX}=Xe^{tX},\qquad e^{0X}=I.
$$

Termwise differentiation proves the derivative formula. The addition law follows from multiplying absolutely convergent series; the binomial identity applies because all powers of the same $X$ commute. These properties show that $t\mapsto e^{tX}$ is a one-parameter subgroup with initial derivative $X$.

For a general Lie group, the exponential is defined through the one-parameter subgroup with specified initial tangent vector, rather than through matrix powers. The matrix series is its concrete realization for matrix Lie groups.

## Worked example: the rotation generator

Set $J=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. Then $J^2=-I$, $J^3=-J$, and $J^4=I$. Separate the even and odd powers:

$$
e^{tJ}
=\left(1-\frac{t^2}{2!}+\frac{t^4}{4!}-\cdots\right)I
+\left(t-\frac{t^3}{3!}+\frac{t^5}{5!}-\cdots\right)J.
$$

These scalar series are cosine and sine, so

$$
e^{tJ}=\cos t\,I+\sin t\,J
=\begin{pmatrix}\cos t&-\sin t\\\sin t&\cos t\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Thus a linear tangent direction produces a curved family of rotations. The parameter is not unique globally: $e^{(t+2\pi)J}=e^{tJ}$. A small interval around $0$, however, gives unique nearby rotation angles.

## Local coordinates and global limits

The derivative of $\exp$ at zero is the identity map under the natural tangent-space identification, because $e^{tX}=I+tX+O(t^2)$. The inverse function theorem then gives neighborhoods of $0\in\mathfrak g$ and $e\in G$ on which $\exp$ is a smooth bijection with a smooth inverse. This is the local logarithm. It does not assert a global inverse.

There are two separate global questions: is every group element an exponential, and can two generators have the same exponential? Rotations already demonstrate nonuniqueness. Noncoverage occurs for $GL_n(\mathbb R)$: for real $X$,

$$
\det(e^X)=e^{\operatorname{tr}X}>0.
$$

One way to justify this identity is to triangularize $X$ over $\mathbb C$. The diagonal entries of its exponential are the exponentials of its eigenvalues, whose product is the exponential of their sum, the trace. Hence a matrix with negative determinant cannot be an exponential of a real matrix. More generally, $t\mapsto e^{tX}$ is a path from the identity, so exponentials lie in the identity component. Membership in that component alone does not guarantee a single exponential in every Lie group.

## Products of different generators

The rule $e^{X+Y}=e^Xe^Y$ holds when $XY=YX$. It can fail otherwise. To see the first discrepancy, compare

$$
e^{tX}e^{tY}=I+t(X+Y)+t^2\left(\frac{X^2}{2}+XY+\frac{Y^2}{2}\right)+O(t^3)
$$

with the series for $e^{t(X+Y)}$. Their difference at order $t^2$ is $\tfrac12t^2[X,Y]$. The local Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff expansion starts with $X+Y+\tfrac12[X,Y]$ in the logarithm of the product. It is a local expansion, not a license to add arbitrary noncommuting generators exactly. See [[25.06 Generators and Lie algebras]].

## Practice

1. Calculate $e^{tN}$ for $N=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.
2. Find all real $t$ for which $e^{tJ}=I$ and decide whether $\operatorname{diag}(-1,1)$ is a real matrix exponential.

### Worked answers

1. Since $N^2=0$, every term after the linear term vanishes. Thus $e^{tN}=I+tN=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&t\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. Multiplying two such matrices adds their parameters, which checks the subgroup law directly.
2. The rotation formula requires $\cos t=1$ and $\sin t=0$, so $t=2\pi k$, $k\in\mathbb Z$. The diagonal matrix has determinant $-1$. Since a real exponential has positive determinant, it has no real matrix logarithm.
