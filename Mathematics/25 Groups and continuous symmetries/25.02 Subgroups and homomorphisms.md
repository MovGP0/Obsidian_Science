title: "Subgroups and homomorphisms"

> [!TLDR]
> Subgroups retain a group operation, and homomorphisms preserve that operation between groups.

## Subgroups inherit the rule

Let $G$ be a group with identity $e$. A subset $H\subseteq G$ is a **subgroup** if the operation restricted to $H$ makes $H$ a group. The operation is inherited; one cannot change the rule and still call the result a subgroup of the specified group.

A useful test is: $H$ is nonempty and $ab^{-1}\in H$ for all $a,b\in H$. To prove the test, choose $h\in H$. Then $hh^{-1}=e\in H$. Taking $a=e$ gives $b^{-1}\in H$. Applying the test to $a$ and $b^{-1}$ gives $ab\in H$. Associativity comes from $G$. Thus identity, inverses, and closure all hold.

For example, $2\mathbb Z=\{2k:k\in\mathbb Z\}$ is a subgroup of the additive group $\mathbb Z$: $2j-2k=2(j-k)$ is even. The positive even integers fail because they omit $0$ and negative inverses. See [[25.01 Group axioms]] for the underlying axioms.

## Maps that preserve composition

A **homomorphism** $\varphi:G\to K$ satisfies

$$
\varphi(ab)=\varphi(a)\varphi(b)
$$

for all $a,b\in G$. The operations on the two sides belong to different groups. In additive notation, the same rule is $\varphi(a+b)=\varphi(a)+\varphi(b)$. Homomorphisms send identity to identity: $\varphi(e)=\varphi(e)^2$ implies $\varphi(e)=e_K$ after cancellation. They also send inverses to inverses, because $\varphi(a)\varphi(a^{-1})=\varphi(e)=e_K$.

The **kernel** and **image** are

$$
\ker\varphi=\{g\in G:\varphi(g)=e_K\},\qquad
\operatorname{im}\varphi=\{\varphi(g):g\in G\}.
$$

Both are subgroups. For the kernel, $\varphi(ab^{-1})=e_Ke_K^{-1}=e_K$. For the image, $\varphi(a)\varphi(b)^{-1}=\varphi(ab^{-1})$ is still an image element. A kernel is also **normal**: if $h\in\ker\varphi$, then $\varphi(ghg^{-1})=\varphi(g)e_K\varphi(g)^{-1}=e_K$.

The kernel measures loss of information. If $\varphi(a)=\varphi(b)$, then $\varphi(b^{-1}a)=e_K$. Thus $\varphi$ is injective exactly when $\ker\varphi=\{e\}$. An **isomorphism** is a bijective homomorphism. It gives the same abstract group structure in two forms, and its inverse also preserves the operation.

## Worked example: a remainder map

Define $\varphi:\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$ by $\varphi(n)=[n]_4$, the remainder class of $n$ modulo $4$. Then

$$
\varphi(m+n)=[m+n]_4=[m]_4+[n]_4.
$$

Thus this is a homomorphism. It is surjective because the classes $[0]_4,[1]_4,[2]_4,[3]_4$ all occur. Its kernel is $4\mathbb Z$, because the zero class consists of multiples of $4$. It is not an isomorphism: $0$ and $4$ have the same image.

Each fiber is a coset: $\varphi(n)=\varphi(r)$ exactly when $n-r\in4\mathbb Z$. The quotient group $\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$ therefore records precisely the distinctions left after the kernel is identified with zero. More generally, the first isomorphism theorem states $G/\ker\varphi\cong\operatorname{im}\varphi$.

## Representation is an additional choice

A matrix representation is a homomorphism $\rho:G\to GL(V)$, where $GL(V)$ consists of invertible linear maps of a vector space $V$. A representation need not be injective. Even an injective representation specifies extra data: the vector space and the action on it. An isomorphism states that two groups have the same structure; it does not select a preferred vector space or basis.

For example, $[k]_4\mapsto i^k$ is an isomorphism onto $\{1,i,-1,-i\}$ under multiplication. The map $[k]_4\mapsto(-1)^k$ is also a one-dimensional complex representation, but its kernel is $\{[0]_4,[2]_4\}$. See [[25.09 Representations]] for this distinction in more detail.

## Practice

1. Find the kernel and image of $f:\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z$, $f(n)=3n$, using addition.
2. For $n\ge1$, find the kernel of $\det:GL_n(\mathbb R)\to\mathbb R^\times$.

### Worked answers

1. Since $3(m+n)=3m+3n$, the map is a homomorphism. The equation $3n=0$ gives $n=0$, so its kernel is trivial and the map is injective. Its image is $3\mathbb Z$, so it is not surjective onto $\mathbb Z$. It is an isomorphism onto its image.
2. The determinant law $\det(AB)=\det(A)\det(B)$ makes this a homomorphism. The target identity is $1$, so the kernel is $SL_n(\mathbb R)=\{A:\det A=1\}$. The kernel is not the set of singular matrices: singular matrices do not belong to the domain.
