> [!TLDR]
> A representation realizes a group by invertible linear maps, and different vector spaces or actions can represent the same group.

## The definition and its information

Let $G$ be a group and let $V$ be a real or complex vector space. A **representation** is a homomorphism $\rho:G\to GL(V)$, where $GL(V)$ is the group of invertible linear maps $V\to V$. Thus

$$
\rho(e)=I_V,\qquad \rho(gh)=\rho(g)\rho(h).
$$

Writing $g\cdot v=\rho(g)v$ gives a group action in which each acting map is linear. A basis turns these linear maps into matrices. The representation exists before a basis is selected; a matrix array is its coordinate description. In a topological or Lie group setting, one normally also requires the representation to be continuous or smooth, respectively. The examples below satisfy those conditions where relevant.

The **kernel** consists of group elements acting as the identity on every vector. A representation is **faithful** when this kernel is $\{e\}$. Faithfulness makes $G$ isomorphic to its image, but it does not say the image is all of $GL(V)$. It also does not select a unique representation of $G$.

## Worked example: different circle representations

For each integer $n$, take $V=\mathbb C$ and define

$$
\rho_n(e^{i\theta})v=e^{in\theta}v.
$$

The integer condition makes the value independent of replacing $\theta$ by $\theta+2\pi k$. Also

$$
\rho_n(e^{i\alpha}e^{i\beta})v
=e^{in(\alpha+\beta)}v
=\rho_n(e^{i\alpha})\rho_n(e^{i\beta})v.
$$

For $n=1$, only $z=1$ acts trivially, so the representation is faithful. For $n=2$, both $1$ and $-1$ act as $1$, so its kernel is $\{1,-1\}$. For $n=0$, every element acts as the identity; this is the trivial representation. The abstract input group is $U(1)$ in each case. Its choice of action changes the information retained.

The same group also acts on $\mathbb R^2$ by the rotation matrix $R(\theta)$. Identifying $(x,y)$ with $x+iy$ shows that this is $\rho_1$ viewed as a real representation. Its real dimension is two, while its complex dimension is one when the complex structure is retained. Always state the scalar field before comparing dimensions.

## Equivalent representations and invariant subspaces

Representations $\rho:G\to GL(V)$ and $\sigma:G\to GL(W)$ over the same field are **equivalent** if there is a linear isomorphism $T:V\to W$ with

$$
T\rho(g)=\sigma(g)T\quad\text{for all }g\in G.
$$

In coordinates, $\sigma(g)=T\rho(g)T^{-1}$. This changes the basis or identifies two linear realizations. It does not mean that every two representations of the same abstract group are equivalent. For example, the one-dimensional complex representations $\rho_1$ and $\rho_2$ are inequivalent. A nonzero scalar $T$ would commute with both scalar actions, forcing $z=z^2$ for every $z\in U(1)$, which fails at $z=-1$.

A subspace $W\subseteq V$ is **invariant** if $\rho(g)W\subseteq W$ for every $g$. Because inverses also act, inclusion is in fact equality. A nonzero representation is **irreducible** if its only invariant subspaces are $\{0\}$ and $V$. This property depends on the scalar field. A real plane rotation representation has no invariant real line under all angles: the angle $\pi/2$ takes each real line to a perpendicular line. Its complexification has the eigenvectors $(1,-i)^T$ and $(1,i)^T$ for every $R(\theta)$. Direct multiplication gives eigenvalues $e^{i\theta}$ and $e^{-i\theta}$, respectively. Their complex spans give two invariant complex lines with different weights.

## Representations on functions

If $G$ acts on a set $X$, let $V$ be all complex-valued functions on $X$. Define the **pullback representation**

$$
(\rho(g)f)(x)=f(g^{-1}\cdot x).
$$

This is linear in $f$. The inverse is essential for the left representation convention:

$$
(\rho(g)\rho(h)f)(x)
=f(h^{-1}\cdot(g^{-1}\cdot x))
=f((gh)^{-1}\cdot x)
=(\rho(gh)f)(x).
$$

For a circle acting on itself by multiplication, put $f_m(z)=z^m$ for integer $m$. Then

$$
(\rho(g)f_m)(z)=(g^{-1}z)^m=g^{-m}f_m(z).
$$

Each span of $f_m$ is invariant and carries weight $-m$ with this pullback convention. The minus sign follows from the inverse; it is not a different group law. For smooth functions on the circle, these same transformations preserve smoothness, so one can use that function space as well.

## Practice

1. Find the kernel of $\rho_3:U(1)\to GL_1(\mathbb C)$.
2. For $G=\{e,s\}$ with $s^2=e$, let $\rho(s)(a,b)=(b,a)$ on $\mathbb R^2$. Find two invariant lines and the action on each.

### Worked answers

1. Acting trivially requires $z^3=1$. Therefore the kernel is $\{1,e^{2\pi i/3},e^{4\pi i/3}\}$. The representation is not faithful, although the domain remains the full circle group.
2. The vectors $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$ are eigenvectors of the swap map with eigenvalues $1$ and $-1$. Their spans are invariant. Every $(a,b)$ equals $\tfrac{a+b}{2}(1,1)+\tfrac{a-b}{2}(1,-1)$, so the representation is the direct sum of the trivial one-dimensional action and the one-dimensional sign action.
