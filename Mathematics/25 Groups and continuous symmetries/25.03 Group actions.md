> [!TLDR]
> A group action assigns a compatible transformation of a set to each group element and makes orbits, stabilizers, and invariants precise.

## From a multiplication rule to transformations

A **left action** of a group $G$ on a set $X$ is a map $G\times X\to X$, written $(g,x)\mapsto g\cdot x$, such that

$$
e\cdot x=x,\qquad (gh)\cdot x=g\cdot(h\cdot x).
$$

Here $e$ is the group identity. The second condition fixes the order of composition: $h$ acts first. For each $g$, the map $x\mapsto g\cdot x$ is bijective, because the action of $g^{-1}$ reverses it. Thus an action is equivalent to a homomorphism from $G$ to $\operatorname{Sym}(X)$, the group of bijections of $X$.

The group and the set are distinct objects. The same group can act on many sets. For example, a square symmetry acts on the four vertices, on the four edges, and on every point of the plane. These actions have different elements in their target sets, even though the group composition rule is the same.

## Orbits and stabilizers

The **orbit** of $x\in X$ is $G\cdot x=\{g\cdot x:g\in G\}$. It contains every point reachable from $x$. Define $x\sim y$ when $y=g\cdot x$ for some $g$. The identity gives reflexivity; inverses give symmetry; products give transitivity. Therefore the orbits partition $X$ into disjoint equivalence classes.

The **stabilizer** of $x$ is

$$
G_x=\{g\in G:g\cdot x=x\}.
$$

It is a subgroup. The identity fixes $x$, and if $g,h$ fix $x$, then $(gh^{-1})\cdot x=g\cdot x=x$. For finite $G$, the orbit–stabilizer formula is

$$
|G\cdot x|=\frac{|G|}{|G_x|}.
$$

To see why, the map $gG_x\mapsto g\cdot x$ identifies left cosets with orbit points. Two elements $g,h$ give the same point exactly when $h^{-1}g\in G_x$, which is exactly the condition that $gG_x=hG_x$. Each coset contains $|G_x|$ elements.

## Worked example: the square

Let $D_4$ be the eight symmetries of the square with vertices $(1,1)$, $(-1,1)$, $(-1,-1)$, and $(1,-1)$. There are four rotations and four reflections. Let $v=(1,1)$. All four vertices are in its orbit. Exactly two symmetries fix it: the identity and reflection across the line $y=x$. Thus

$$
|D_4\cdot v|=8/2=4.
$$

The center $(0,0)$ has a different stabilizer: every element fixes it. Its orbit has one point, and $8/8=1$. A group action need not move every point.

![[math-25-square-action.svg|**Fig. 25.1** The orbit of a square vertex has four points. Its stabilizer contains the identity and reflection in the marked diagonal.]]

The diagonal in the figure passes through the chosen vertex. Reflection in the other diagonal exchanges it with the opposite vertex and is not in its stabilizer. This gives a direct way to check the stabilizer count instead of counting every symmetry as a possible fixer.

## Invariant points, subsets, and functions

A point is **fixed by the group** if every element fixes it. A subset $A\subseteq X$ is **invariant** if $gA=A$ for every $g$. It need not be fixed point by point: the vertex set is invariant under $D_4$, but a quarter turn changes every vertex.

A function $F:X\to\mathbb R$ is invariant if $F(g\cdot x)=F(x)$. For the standard orthogonal action on $\mathbb R^2$, $F(x,y)=x^2+y^2$ is invariant. Each orbit of the full orthogonal group is a circle centered at zero, except for the singleton orbit of zero. An invariant function is constant on every orbit; this does not automatically mean its value distinguishes different orbits for an arbitrary action.

For a vector space $V$, require each acting map to be linear to obtain a representation. An invariant subspace $W$ satisfies $gW=W$ for every $g$. See [[25.09 Representations]]. Do not assume an arbitrary action on the underlying set of $V$ is linear.

## Practice

1. The group $\mathbb Z$ acts on $\mathbb R$ by $n\cdot x=x+n$. Find the orbit and stabilizer of $1/3$.
2. The group $\{1,-1\}$ acts on $\mathbb R$ by multiplication. Find all orbits and all points fixed by the whole group.

### Worked answers

1. The orbit is $\{1/3+n:n\in\mathbb Z\}$. The equality $1/3+n=1/3$ forces $n=0$, so the stabilizer is $\{0\}$, where $0$ is the additive identity. The finite counting formula is not needed for this infinite group.
2. Zero has orbit $\{0\}$. Every nonzero $x$ has orbit $\{x,-x\}$, so the remaining orbits can be indexed by positive real numbers. A fixed point must satisfy $-x=x$, hence $x=0$. Each two-point orbit is an invariant subset, although its individual points are exchanged.
