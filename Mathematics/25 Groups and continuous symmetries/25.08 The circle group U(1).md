title: "The circle group U(1)"

> [!TLDR]
> Unit complex numbers form an Abelian Lie group whose real angles are identified modulo 2π and whose Lie algebra is the imaginary axis.

## Unit complex numbers and multiplication

For $z=x+iy\in\mathbb C$, its complex conjugate is $\overline z=x-iy$, and $|z|^2=z\overline z=x^2+y^2$. The **circle group** is

$$
U(1)=\{z\in\mathbb C:|z|=1\}.
$$

The notation comes from the unitary group of one-by-one matrices: $\overline z z=1$ is its unitary condition. The operation is complex multiplication. It is closed because $|zw|=|z||w|=1$. Its identity is $1$, and $z^{-1}=\overline z$ on the unit circle. Associativity and commutativity come from complex multiplication, so this is an Abelian group.

Every element has the form $e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$ with a real angle $\theta$. The angle addition formulas give

$$
e^{i\alpha}e^{i\beta}=e^{i(\alpha+\beta)},\qquad
(e^{i\theta})^{-1}=e^{-i\theta}.
$$

Here angles are mathematical real parameters. No physical interpretation or units are needed.

## A periodic parameter

Define $p:\mathbb R\to U(1)$ by $p(\theta)=e^{i\theta}$. Regard $\mathbb R$ as an additive group. The addition law makes $p$ a surjective homomorphism. Its kernel is

$$
\ker p=2\pi\mathbb Z=\{2\pi k:k\in\mathbb Z\}.
$$

Indeed, $e^{i\theta}=1$ requires both $\sin\theta=0$ and $\cos\theta=1$. Consequently $p(\alpha)=p(\beta)$ exactly when $\alpha-\beta\in2\pi\mathbb Z$. This yields the group identification $U(1)\cong\mathbb R/(2\pi\mathbb Z)$. Angles are added before their equivalence class is taken.

![[math-25-circle-cover.svg|**Fig. 25.2** Angles that differ by 2π map to the same circle point. The tangent at the identity is the imaginary axis.]]

The three marked points on the real line in the figure all map to $i$. They illustrate repeated preimages, rather than three different elements of $U(1)$. The tangent arrow at $1$ shows the derivative of the counterclockwise curve at angle zero. See [[25.05 Lie groups]] for why a periodic parameter is not a global real coordinate chart.

## Worked example: multiply two phases

Take $z=e^{i5\pi/6}$ and $w=e^{i2\pi/3}$. Add the angles:

$$
zw=e^{i(5\pi/6+4\pi/6)}=e^{i3\pi/2}=-i.
$$

If the chosen principal range is $[-\pi,\pi)$, subtract $2\pi$ to obtain $-\pi/2$. Both angles give the same element. Also $z^{-1}=e^{-i5\pi/6}$, and $zz^{-1}=e^0=1$. The principal angle of a product is thus the sum reduced to the selected range, not always the ordinary sum of principal angles.

## Its one-dimensional Lie algebra

Let $z(t)\in U(1)$ be a smooth curve with $z(0)=1$, and write $X=z'(0)$. Differentiate $\overline{z(t)}z(t)=1$ at zero:

$$
\overline X+X=0.
$$

This says that $X$ is purely imaginary. Conversely, $t\mapsto e^{iat}$ lies on the circle and has derivative $ia$ for every $a\in\mathbb R$. Thus

$$
\mathfrak u(1)=i\mathbb R,\qquad [ia,ib]=(ia)(ib)-(ib)(ia)=0.
$$

It is one-dimensional over $\mathbb R$, with basis $i$, and is Abelian. It is not a one-dimensional complex subspace of $\mathbb C$: multiplying $i$ by the complex scalar $i$ gives $-1$, outside $i\mathbb R$. The exponential map sends $ia$ to $e^{ia}$, so its kernel is $2\pi i\mathbb Z$ in the algebra, while $p$ has kernel $2\pi\mathbb Z$ in the real angle line. Keep these domains distinct.

## Integer powers

For any integer $n$, the map $\chi_n(z)=z^n$ is a homomorphism $U(1)\to U(1)$. In angle notation it sends $e^{i\theta}$ to $e^{in\theta}$. Integer $n$ makes this independent of angle choice: replacing $\theta$ by $\theta+2\pi k$ changes the result by $e^{2\pi ink}=1$. A noninteger coefficient in this same formula generally fails to define a single-valued circle map. These powers give representations in [[25.09 Representations]].

## Practice

1. Find all $z\in U(1)$ with $z^3=1$.
2. Does $e^{i\theta}\mapsto e^{i\theta/2}$ define a function on $U(1)$ without any extra choice?

### Worked answers

1. Write $z=e^{i\theta}$. Then $3\theta=2\pi k$, so representatives are $1$, $e^{i2\pi/3}$, and $e^{i4\pi/3}$. Values of $k$ differing by $3$ repeat the same point. These are the kernel of $z\mapsto z^3$.
2. No. The same input $1$ has angles $0$ and $2\pi$, but the proposed outputs are $1$ and $-1$. Selecting a branch gives a set function on a chosen range, but it cannot make this formula a global continuous group homomorphism.
