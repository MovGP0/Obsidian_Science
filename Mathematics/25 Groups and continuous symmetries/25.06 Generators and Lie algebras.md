> [!TLDR]
> A Lie algebra describes tangent directions at a group identity, with a bracket that records the first local failure of commutativity.

## Differentiate a matrix curve

Let $G$ be a real matrix Lie group with identity $I$. A smooth curve $g(t)\in G$ with $g(0)=I$ has derivative

$$
X=g'(0),\qquad g(t)=I+tX+O(t^2).
$$

The derivative $X$ is a tangent vector at $I$. The set of these derivatives is the tangent space $T_IG$, called the **Lie algebra** $\mathfrak g$. The symbol $O(t^2)$ means that the remainder norm is bounded by a constant times $t^2$ near zero. This expansion uses a twice differentiable curve.

A generator belongs to the Lie algebra, not usually to the group. For instance, the zero matrix is always an algebra element but is not invertible. The Lie algebra is a real vector space for the real Lie groups discussed here, even when their matrices have complex entries.

A **one-parameter subgroup** $g:\mathbb R\to G$ also satisfies $g(s+t)=g(s)g(t)$ and $g(0)=I$. Differentiate with respect to $s$ at $s=0$:

$$
g'(t)=Xg(t),\qquad X=g'(0).
$$

Differentiating in the other factor gives $g'(t)=g(t)X$. Its solution is $g(t)=e^{tX}$, explained in [[25.07 Exponential maps]]. A general curve through $I$ need not be a one-parameter subgroup, although its initial derivative is still a tangent vector.

## Differentiate the defining equations

For $G=O(n)$, write $g(t)^Tg(t)=I$ and differentiate at zero. The product rule gives

$$
X^T+X=0.
$$

Thus tangent matrices are skew-symmetric. Conversely, if $X^T=-X$, then $(e^{tX})^Te^{tX}=e^{-tX}e^{tX}=I$, so each such matrix occurs as a tangent direction. Therefore

$$
\mathfrak{so}(n)=\mathfrak{o}(n)=\{X\in M_n(\mathbb R):X^T=-X\}.
$$

The algebras coincide because $O(n)$ and $SO(n)$ agree locally at $I$. The determinant of $e^{tX}$ is continuously $+1$ along this curve. The algebra does not record the reflection component of $O(n)$.

For $U(n)$, the same calculation gives $X^*+X=0$, so $\mathfrak u(n)$ consists of skew-Hermitian matrices. Adding determinant $1$ gives the additional tangent condition $\operatorname{tr}X=0$ for $\mathfrak{su}(n)$. Here trace means the sum of diagonal entries; the derivative of determinant at $I$ is trace.

## The bracket

For matrix Lie algebras, the bracket is the commutator

$$
[X,Y]=XY-YX.
$$

It is bilinear, antisymmetric, and satisfies the Jacobi identity

$$
[X,[Y,Z]]+[Y,[Z,X]]+[Z,[X,Y]]=0.
$$

These properties follow by expansion and cancellation using associative matrix multiplication. The bracket remains in $\mathfrak g$. One explanation is that conjugation $g e^{sY}g^{-1}$ stays in the group, so its derivative $gYg^{-1}$ stays in its tangent space; differentiating this expression at $g=e^{tX}$ gives $XY-YX$.

The small group commutator exposes its meaning:

$$
e^{tX}e^{tY}e^{-tX}e^{-tY}=I+t^2[X,Y]+O(t^3).
$$

The linear terms cancel. Thus the bracket measures a second-order difference between two orders of infinitesimal transformations. This formula fixes the commutator and sign convention used here.

## Worked example: two elementary generators

In $\mathfrak{gl}_2(\mathbb R)=M_2(\mathbb R)$, take

$$
X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Direct multiplication gives $XY=\operatorname{diag}(1,0)$ and $YX=\operatorname{diag}(0,1)$. Hence $[X,Y]=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)\ne0$. Neither the fact that $X^2=Y^2=0$ nor their similar appearance makes them commute. Their exponentials are the elementary shear matrices $I+tX$ and $I+tY$.

## Practice

1. Find the generator of $R(t)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\cos t&-\sin t\\\sin t&\cos t\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ and check its tangent constraint.
2. Compute $[D,X]$ for $D=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$ and the matrix $X$ in the example.

### Worked answers

1. Entrywise differentiation at zero gives $J=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. Its transpose is $-J$, so $J\in\mathfrak{so}(2)$. Every real skew-symmetric $2\times2$ matrix is $aJ$, so this algebra is one-dimensional.
2. Multiplication gives $DX=X$ and $XD=-X$. Therefore $[D,X]=X-(-X)=2X$. Reversing the bracket gives $[X,D]=-2X$, as required by antisymmetry.
