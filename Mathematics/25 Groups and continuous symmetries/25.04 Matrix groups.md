> [!TLDR]
> Invertible, orthogonal, and unitary matrices form groups that describe reversible linear maps and the forms they preserve.

## Invertible matrices

Fix a positive integer $n$ and a field $\mathbb F$, here $\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$. The **general linear group** is

$$
GL_n(\mathbb F)=\{A\in M_n(\mathbb F):\det A\ne0\},
$$

where $M_n(\mathbb F)$ denotes the square $n\times n$ matrices. The operation is matrix multiplication. A matrix acts on a column vector $v$ by $v\mapsto Av$, and $ABv=A(Bv)$ means that $B$ acts first.

The determinant identity $\det(AB)=\det A\det B$ proves closure. Associativity is a property of matrix multiplication. The identity is $I_n$, and the inverse of an invertible matrix is again invertible. Thus the group axioms hold. All square matrices under multiplication do not form a group, because a singular matrix has no inverse.

The **special linear group** $SL_n(\mathbb F)$ consists of matrices with determinant $1$. It is a subgroup because determinants multiply and $\det(A^{-1})=(\det A)^{-1}$. It preserves oriented volume in the real case; it need not preserve lengths or angles.

## Orthogonal matrices preserve real inner products

For real column vectors, use the inner product $\langle v,w\rangle=v^Tw$. Define

$$
O(n)=\{Q\in M_n(\mathbb R):Q^TQ=I_n\}.
$$

The defining condition gives $Q^{-1}=Q^T$. If $Q,R\in O(n)$, then

$$
(QR)^T(QR)=R^TQ^TQR=R^TR=I_n.
$$

This proves closure without assuming $Q$ and $R$ commute. Further,

$$
\langle Qv,Qw\rangle=v^TQ^TQw=v^Tw.
$$

Thus orthogonal matrices preserve inner products and lengths. Conversely, inner-product preservation for all $v,w$ forces $Q^TQ=I_n$. Their columns are an orthonormal basis. Taking determinants gives $(\det Q)^2=1$, so $\det Q=\pm1$. The subgroup $SO(n)$ imposes $\det Q=1$ as well.

## Unitary matrices preserve complex inner products

For a complex matrix $U$, write $U^*=\overline U^T$ for its conjugate transpose. With inner product $\langle v,w\rangle=v^*w$, define

$$
U(n)=\{U:U^*U=I_n\},\qquad
SU(n)=\{U\in U(n):\det U=1\}.
$$

Then $U^{-1}=U^*$ and $\langle Uv,Uw\rangle=\langle v,w\rangle$. The same product calculation as above, with stars in place of transposes, proves closure. The determinant condition gives $|\det U|^2=1$. Thus a unitary determinant lies on the complex unit circle; it need not equal $\pm1$.

## Worked example: rotation and reflection

Let

$$
R=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
S=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

For each, the columns have length $1$ and dot product $0$, so $R^TR=S^TS=I_2$. Also $\det R=1$ and $\det S=-1$. Hence $R\in SO(2)$, while $S\in O(2)\setminus SO(2)$. Applied to $(2,3)^T$, they give $(-3,2)^T$ and $(2,-3)^T$. The squared lengths are $13$ before and after.

Their products are

$$
RS=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
SR=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

These differ, so $O(2)$ is non-Abelian. In contrast, every matrix of $SO(2)$ has the form $R(\theta)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, and $R(\alpha)R(\beta)=R(\alpha+\beta)$. This makes $SO(2)$ Abelian. The case $SO(3)$ is different: its rotations need not commute.

These matrix groups also have smooth structures, developed in [[25.05 Lie groups]]. The equations that define a subgroup must be checked before its name or geometric interpretation is used.

## Practice

1. Classify $A=\operatorname{diag}(2,1/2)$ among $GL_2(\mathbb R)$, $SL_2(\mathbb R)$, and $O(2)$.
2. Show that $U=\operatorname{diag}(i,e^{i\pi/3})$ is unitary. Is it in $SU(2)$?

### Worked answers

1. Its determinant is $2(1/2)=1$, so it lies in both $GL_2(\mathbb R)$ and $SL_2(\mathbb R)$. But $A^TA=\operatorname{diag}(4,1/4)\ne I_2$, so it is not orthogonal. Determinant $1$ alone does not imply length preservation.
2. Its conjugate transpose is $\operatorname{diag}(-i,e^{-i\pi/3})$. Multiplication gives $U^*U=\operatorname{diag}(1,1)=I_2$. Its determinant is $ie^{i\pi/3}=e^{i5\pi/6}\ne1$, so $U\in U(2)$ but $U\notin SU(2)$.
