> [!TLDR]
> A group is a set with a composition rule that has an identity, inverses, and associative composition.

## A rule for reversible operations

A **binary operation** on a set $G$ assigns one element $ab\in G$ to each ordered pair $(a,b)\in G\times G$. The notation $ab$ need not mean ordinary multiplication. It can mean addition, composition of functions, or multiplication of matrices. The specified set and the specified rule together form the algebraic structure.

A **group** is a nonempty set $G$ with a binary operation that satisfies these requirements:

1. **Closure:** if $a,b\in G$, then $ab\in G$.
2. **Associativity:** $(ab)c=a(bc)$ for all $a,b,c\in G$.
3. **Identity:** an element $e\in G$ satisfies $ea=ae=a$ for every $a\in G$.
4. **Inverses:** each $a\in G$ has an element $a^{-1}\in G$ with $aa^{-1}=a^{-1}a=e$.

Closure is already included when the operation is defined as a map $G\times G\to G$. It is still useful to check it explicitly for a proposed example. An **Abelian** group also satisfies $ab=ba$ for all pairs. Associativity changes brackets; commutativity changes order. These are different conditions.

For transformations, use the convention $(fg)(x)=f(g(x))$: the rightmost transformation acts first. Composition is associative because both $((fg)h)(x)$ and $(f(gh))(x)$ equal $f(g(h(x)))$. It is usually not commutative.

## Consequences of the axioms

The identity is unique. If $e$ and $e'$ are both identities, then $e=e e'=e'$. Inverses are also unique: if $b$ and $c$ are two inverses of $a$, then

$$
b=b e=b(ac)=(ba)c=e c=c.
$$

Associativity permits the middle regrouping. This argument explains why $a^{-1}$ has an unambiguous meaning. Cancellation follows by multiplication with an inverse. For example, $ab=ac$ implies $a^{-1}(ab)=a^{-1}(ac)$ and thus $b=c$. Cancellation does not require commutativity.

The inverse of a product reverses the order:

$$
(ab)^{-1}=b^{-1}a^{-1},
$$

because $(ab)(b^{-1}a^{-1})=a(bb^{-1})a^{-1}=e$, and the reversed product also gives $e$. Do not exchange the two factors unless commutativity has been established.

## Worked example: integers modulo four

Let $G=\{0,1,2,3\}$ and let $a\oplus b$ be the remainder of $a+b$ after division by $4$. The result always lies in $G$, so the rule is closed. Integer addition is associative, and taking a remainder preserves equality modulo $4$, so $\oplus$ is associative. The identity is $0$. The inverses are $0,3,2,1$ for $0,1,2,3$, respectively. For example, $1\oplus3=0$ and $2\oplus2=0$. The rule is commutative because integer addition is commutative. This group is denoted $\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$.

Contrast this with the positive integers under addition. They are closed and associative, but they contain no additive identity or additive inverses. The integers under ordinary multiplication also fail: $2$ has no multiplicative inverse in the integers. A familiar operation alone does not guarantee a group.

## Worked example: a non-Abelian group

The group $S_3$ consists of all bijections of $\{1,2,3\}$. Let $p=(12)$ exchange $1$ and $2$, and let $q=(23)$ exchange $2$ and $3$. With rightmost-first composition,

$$
(pq)(1)=p(q(1))=p(1)=2,\qquad
(qp)(1)=q(p(1))=q(2)=3.
$$

Thus $pq\ne qp$. The set remains a group: compositions of bijections are bijections, the identity map is included, and each bijection has an inverse. Noncommutativity does not violate a group axiom. See [[25.03 Group actions]] for the distinction between a group and its action on a set.

## Practice

1. Does $\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}$ form a group under multiplication? Is it Abelian?
2. Solve $axb=c$ in an arbitrary group, where $a,b,c$ are given.

### Worked answers

1. A product of two nonzero real numbers is nonzero. Real multiplication is associative, $1$ is the identity, and $1/a$ is a nonzero real inverse of $a$. Multiplication is commutative, so this is an Abelian group. Including $0$ would destroy the inverse condition.
2. Multiply $axb=c$ on the left by $a^{-1}$ to obtain $xb=a^{-1}c$. Multiply on the right by $b^{-1}$ to obtain $x=a^{-1}cb^{-1}$. Substitution gives $a(a^{-1}cb^{-1})b=c$, which verifies the solution without changing factor order.
