title: "Definitions and assumptions"

> [!TLDR]
> Definitions fix meanings, assumptions restrict a discussion, and theorems require arguments that establish their conclusions.

## Different jobs for mathematical sentences

A **definition** specifies what a term or symbol means. For example, an integer $n$ is **even** if there exists $k\in\mathbb Z$ such that $n=2k$. The definition does not need a proof; it sets a criterion. A later claim that $18$ is even does require checking the criterion: $18=2\cdot9$, and $9$ is an integer.

A definition should be precise and compatible with objects already introduced. If it introduces an object by a property, existence and uniqueness may need proof before the notation is safe. “Let $r$ be the positive real root of $r^2=4$” is safe because $r=2$ exists and no other positive root works. “Let $r$ be the positive real root of $r^2=-1$” fails because no such object exists.

An **assumption** or **hypothesis** is a condition accepted for the current argument. In “let $x>0$,” positivity is given; it is not asserted for every real number. A **theorem** states a conclusion proved from definitions, assumptions, and previously established results. A **lemma** is a theorem used to support another result. A **corollary** follows readily from a result already proved. These names describe roles, not separate standards of truth.

## Identities, equations, and conventions

An **identity** is an equality true for every allowed assignment in its domain. The distributive law gives

$$
(a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2\qquad(a,b\in\mathbb R).
$$

To justify it, multiply $(a+b)(a+b)$ to obtain $a^2+ab+ba+b^2$, then use $ab=ba$. The domain matters: for matrices, multiplication can fail to commute, and the middle terms must remain $AB+BA$ unless extra assumptions are made.

An equation can instead impose a condition, such as $x^2=9$. It does not claim that all real $x$ satisfy it. A **convention** selects a standard notation or interpretation where several choices are possible. We use $\mathbb N=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ here, but some authors omit $0$. We also use $\sqrt a$ for the nonnegative real square root when $a\ge0$. Under that convention, $\sqrt9=3$, while the equation $x^2=9$ has the two solutions $\pm3$.

The notation $f(x):=x^2+1$ often emphasizes a definition. The symbol $=$ still means equality, but the surrounding sentence can make clear that the equality introduces a name. Distinguish the role of a sentence from the glyph alone.

## Track hypotheses through a calculation

Suppose $a,b\in\mathbb R$ and $a\ne b$. Then

$$
\frac{a^2-b^2}{a-b}=a+b.
$$

The numerator factors as $(a-b)(a+b)$. Cancellation is valid because the hypothesis makes the denominator nonzero. Removing the hypothesis would make the displayed fraction undefined at $a=b$. The polynomial identity $a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b)$ has no such restriction; the fraction does. A useful habit is to write domain conditions before simplifying, then keep them after cancellation.

## Worked example: from definition to theorem

Prove that the sum of two odd integers is even. The definition says that an odd integer has the form $2k+1$ for an integer $k$. Let $m$ and $n$ be arbitrary odd integers. There exist integers $r,s$ with

$$
m=2r+1,\qquad n=2s+1.
$$

Then

$$
m+n=(2r+1)+(2s+1)=2(r+s+1).
$$

The integer closure of addition ensures that $r+s+1\in\mathbb Z$. The sum therefore meets the definition of an even integer. The oddness of $m,n$ is the hypothesis, their representations come from a definition, and the evenness of their sum is the proved conclusion. The argument does not assume the conclusion or merely inspect a few examples.

## Common errors

A theorem cannot become true just by calling it a definition, unless one changes the meaning of a term and checks consistency. Likewise, a statement in an example applies only to the chosen objects until a general argument is supplied. When reading a proof, identify which line is a definition, which fact is assumed, and which inference is justified. If a proof divides by $x$, look for the hypothesis $x\ne0$ or a separate zero case.

## Practice

1. Classify the roles in: “Define $g(x):=x^2$ for real $x$. Assume $x\ge0$. Then $\sqrt{g(x)}=x$.” Explain where the assumption is used.
2. A proposed proof starts with $a=b$, writes $a^2-b^2=ab-b^2$, cancels $a-b$, and obtains $a+b=b$. Find the invalid step.

> [!example]- Worked answers
> 1. The first sentence defines a function. The second is a hypothesis. The last is a conclusion justified by the square-root convention. For arbitrary real $x$, $\sqrt{x^2}=|x|$. The hypothesis $x\ge0$ lets us replace $|x|$ by $x$.
>
> 2. Factoring gives $(a+b)(a-b)=b(a-b)$. The assumption $a=b$ implies $a-b=0$, so the cancellation divides by zero. The equality before cancellation is just $0=0$ and does not justify $a+b=b$.
