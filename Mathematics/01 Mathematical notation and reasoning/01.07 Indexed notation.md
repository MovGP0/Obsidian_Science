title: "Indexed notation"
---

> [!TLDR]
> An index labels members of a family, and a summation or product symbol combines the specified terms.

## Indices are labels

An indexed family $(a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n)$ assigns an object $a_i$ to each index $i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$. The subscript selects a member. For example, if $(a_1,a_2,a_3)=(5,-2,7)$, then $a_2=-2$. It does not mean $a$ multiplied by $2$. Two indices can label a table entry: $a_{ij}$ can mean the entry in row $i$ and column $j$.

A superscript often means a power, so $a_i^2=(a_i)^2$. But superscripts can also be labels when the notation explicitly says so. For example, $x^{(k)}$ can label the $k$th object in a sequence. Do not infer the meaning from position alone; read the definition. In ordinary algebra, $a_2^3$ is the cube of the second entry.

## Finite sums and products

The sigma symbol abbreviates repeated addition:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i=a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n.
$$

The lower bound gives the first index, the upper bound gives the last, and both are included. Thus a sum from $i=2$ to $i=5$ has four terms. The pi symbol abbreviates repeated multiplication:

$$
\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_i=a_1a_2\cdots a_n.
$$

For real or complex entries, addition and multiplication are associative and commutative, so we can regroup and reorder finite terms. For other objects, such as matrices, product order may matter. An empty sum is defined as $0$, and an empty product as $1$. These conventions preserve the usual rules when a range has no entries.

The index in a sum is a **bound** or **dummy** variable. Its name has no effect if all its occurrences and limits are changed consistently:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n}i^2=\sum_{k=1}^{n}k^2.
$$

The letter $n$ here is free: its chosen value determines how many terms occur. The dummy letter has no value outside its own sum. The two indices in $\sum_i a_i+\sum_i b_i$ belong to separate scopes.

## Rules and reindexing

For real entries and a fixed real number $c$, distributivity gives

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n}(a_i+b_i)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i+\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_i,
\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^{n}ca_i=c\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i.
$$

The constant can leave the sum because it does not depend on $i$. By contrast, $i$ cannot be moved outside $\sum_i ia_i$. A product does not distribute over a sum of factors in the same way: generally $\prod_i(a_i+b_i)\ne\prod_i a_i+\prod_i b_i$.

Reindexing changes the description of the same terms. If $j=i+1$, then $i=1$ corresponds to $j=2$ and $i=4$ to $j=5$. Therefore

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{4}(i+1)^2=\sum_{j=2}^{5}j^2.
$$

Both the summand and the bounds must change. Expanding a short version is an effective check for a missing first or last term.

## Worked example: a weighted finite sum

Let $a_i=2i-1$ for $i=1,2,3,4$. These entries are $1,3,5,7$. Compute $S=\sum_{i=1}^{4}ia_i$ by showing each contribution:

$$
S=1(1)+2(3)+3(5)+4(7)=1+6+15+28=50.
$$

We can verify the result by substituting the entry formula first:

$$
\begin{aligned}
S&=\sum_{i=1}^{4}i(2i-1)
=2\sum_{i=1}^{4}i^2-\sum_{i=1}^{4}i\\
&=2(1+4+9+16)-(1+2+3+4)\\
&=2(30)-10=50.
\end{aligned}
$$

The two calculations combine exactly the same terms. For comparison, $\prod_{i=1}^{4}a_i=1\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7=105$, which uses multiplication rather than addition.

## Practice

1. If $b_0=2$, $b_1=-1$, and $b_2=3$, compute $\sum_{k=0}^{2}b_k^2$ and $(\sum_{k=0}^{2}b_k)^2$. Explain why they differ.
2. Reindex $\sum_{i=3}^{6}(i-2)^2$ with $j=i-2$, then calculate it. Also calculate $\prod_{j=1}^{4}j$.

> [!example]- Worked answers
> 1. The sum of squares is $2^2+(-1)^2+3^2=4+1+9=14$. The square of the sum is $(2-1+3)^2=4^2=16$. Squaring a sum includes cross terms between distinct entries; the sum of squares does not.
>
> 2. The bounds become $j=1$ and $j=4$, and the summand becomes $j^2$. Thus the sum is $\sum_{j=1}^{4}j^2=1+4+9+16=30$. The product is $1\cdot2\cdot3\cdot4=24$, also written $4!$.

General formulas for finite sums can be established by induction; see [[01.08 Proof methods]].
