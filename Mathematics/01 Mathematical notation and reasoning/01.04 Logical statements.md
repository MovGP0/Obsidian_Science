title: "Logical statements"

> [!TLDR]
> Logical connectives build precise statements, and an implication has a different meaning from its converse.

## Statements and truth values

A **statement** is a sentence with a definite truth value, true or false, in its stated context. “$7$ is prime” is a true statement. “$8$ is prime” is a false statement. A question is not a statement. The condition “$x>3$” has no fixed truth value until we assign or quantify $x$. Such a condition is called a predicate or open statement.

Let $P$ and $Q$ stand for statements. The conjunction $P\land Q$ means “$P$ and $Q$” and is true only if both are true. The disjunction $P\lor Q$ means inclusive “or”: at least one is true, and both may be true. The negation $\neg P$ reverses the truth value of $P$. These are statements about truth, not about the order in which someone checks conditions.

For example, an integer can be even or divisible by $3$ in this inclusive sense. The integer $6$ satisfies both conditions, and therefore satisfies their disjunction. If exactly one alternative is intended, say so explicitly.

## Implication and equivalence

The implication $P\Rightarrow Q$ says that whenever $P$ is true, $Q$ is true. Here $P$ is the hypothesis and $Q$ the conclusion. The implication is false only when $P$ is true and $Q$ is false:

| $P$ | $Q$ | $P\Rightarrow Q$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| True | True | True |
| True | False | False |
| False | True | True |
| False | False | True |

When $P$ is false, the implication makes no failed promise. This explains the last two rows; they do not claim that $Q$ follows from some unrelated fact. In mathematics, a universally quantified implication must hold for all allowed inputs, including those for which the hypothesis is true.

The **converse** is $Q\Rightarrow P$. It is a separate claim. The **contrapositive** is $\neg Q\Rightarrow\neg P$, which is equivalent to the original implication. Both fail in exactly the case $P$ true and $Q$ false. The **inverse**, $\neg P\Rightarrow\neg Q$, is equivalent to the converse, not generally to the original.

The equivalence $P\Leftrightarrow Q$, read “if and only if,” asserts both $P\Rightarrow Q$ and $Q\Rightarrow P$. It says that $P$ and $Q$ have the same truth value. In this language, $P$ is a sufficient condition for $Q$ when $P\Rightarrow Q$, and $Q$ is a necessary condition for $P$.

## Worked example: divisibility and a converse

An integer $n$ is divisible by an integer $d\ne0$ if $n=dk$ for some integer $k$. Consider:

$$
4\mid n\ \Rightarrow\ 2\mid n.
$$

If $n=4k$, then $n=2(2k)$. Since $2k$ is an integer, $n$ is divisible by $2$. This proves the implication for every integer $n$. The converse would state that every even integer is divisible by $4$. At $n=6$, the hypothesis $2\mid6$ is true but $4\mid6$ is false. Thus the converse is false.

The contrapositive says $2\nmid n\Rightarrow4\nmid n$. This is true: if $4$ divided $n$, the proved implication would force $2$ to divide it. The integer $6$ does not refute this contrapositive, because its hypothesis is false there.

## Negating combined statements

De Morgan's laws describe the failure of “and” and “or”:

$$
\neg(P\land Q)\Leftrightarrow(\neg P\lor\neg Q),\qquad
\neg(P\lor Q)\Leftrightarrow(\neg P\land\neg Q).
$$

To deny that both conditions hold, at least one must fail. To deny that either holds, both must fail. Also $\neg(P\Rightarrow Q)$ is equivalent to $P\land\neg Q$. A counterexample to an implication must satisfy its hypothesis. Showing only that its conclusion can be false is insufficient.

## Practice

1. For real $x$, let $P$ mean $x>2$ and $Q$ mean $x^2>4$. Is $P\Rightarrow Q$ true for all real $x$? Is its converse true?
2. Negate “$n$ is even and $n>10$.” Then state what is required to refute “if $n$ is even, then $n>10$.”

> [!example]- Worked answers
> 1. If $x>2$, then $x+2>0$ and $x-2>0$. Their product $x^2-4=(x-2)(x+2)$ is positive, so the implication is true. The converse fails at $x=-3$: $x^2=9>4$ but $x>2$ is false.
>
> 2. The negation is “$n$ is not even or $n\le10$.” For an integer, “not even” means odd. To refute the implication, choose an even integer with $n\le10$, such as $n=4$. Its hypothesis is true and its conclusion false.
