> [!TLDR]
> The exterior derivative increases form degree by one, obeys a signed product rule, and gives zero when applied twice to a smooth form.

## Definition in coordinates

Let $U\subseteq\mathbb R^n$ be open, with coordinates $x^1,\ldots,x^n$. For a smooth function $f$, its exterior derivative is the one-form

$$
df=\sum_{j=1}^n\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^j}dx^j.
$$

The covector $df_p$ maps a tangent vector $v$ to the directional derivative $v(f)$ at $p$. Thus $df$ needs no metric: it is a covector rather than a vector. A metric can later turn it into a gradient vector, as in [[23.09 Classical operations]].

Write a $k$-form using increasing multi-indices $I=(i_1<\cdots<i_k)$ as $\omega=\sum_I f_I dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_k}$. Define

$$
d\omega=\sum_I\sum_j
\frac{\partial f_I}{\partial x^j}
dx^j\wedge dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_k}.
$$

Only the coefficients are differentiated; $d(dx^j)=0$. Repeated wedge factors vanish. The derivative therefore increases the degree by one, and the derivative of every top-degree form on an $n$-dimensional manifold is zero. Compatible coordinate changes make this a well-defined operator on manifolds; [[23.05 Pullbacks]] explains the compatibility.

## The graded product rule

For a $p$-form $\alpha$ and any form $\beta$,

$$
d(\alpha\wedge\beta)=d\alpha\wedge\beta+(-1)^p\alpha\wedge d\beta.
$$

To see the sign, first write $\alpha=f\,dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_p}$ and $\beta=g\,dx^{j_1}\wedge\cdots$. Differentiating $fg$ gives $g\,df+f\,dg$. In the second term, moving $dg$ past the $p$ coordinate covectors in $\alpha$ introduces $p$ sign changes. Linearity gives the result for sums. For a function $f$, $p=0$, so the familiar unsigned product rule returns.

## Why the derivative squared is zero

For a $C^2$ function, equality of mixed partial derivatives gives

$$
d(df)=\sum_{i,j}\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x^j\partial x^i}
dx^j\wedge dx^i=0.
$$

Terms with $i=j$ vanish. For $i\ne j$, the coefficient is symmetric under exchanging $i,j$, but the wedge is antisymmetric. The two corresponding terms cancel. For a general form, the same cancellation applies to each coefficient $f_I$, with the fixed trailing wedge $dx^I$. Hence $d^2\omega=0$. This proof assumes enough regularity for mixed partials to agree; smooth coefficients satisfy that condition.

## Worked example: differentiate a one-form

On $\mathbb R^3$, let

$$
\alpha=xy\,dx+x^2\,dy+yz\,dz.
$$

Differentiate each coefficient before collecting the ordered wedges:

$$
\begin{aligned}
d\alpha
&=(y\,dx+x\,dy)\wedge dx
  +2x\,dx\wedge dy
  +(z\,dy+y\,dz)\wedge dz\\
&=-x\,dx\wedge dy+2x\,dx\wedge dy+z\,dy\wedge dz\\
&=x\,dx\wedge dy+z\,dy\wedge dz.
\end{aligned}
$$

Apply $d$ again. The result is $dx\wedge dx\wedge dy+dz\wedge dy\wedge dz=0$, since each term repeats a covector. The first derivative is nonzero, so $\alpha$ is not closed; its derivative is closed automatically. These terms are defined in [[23.08 Closed and exact forms]].

A common error is to write $d(f\,dx)=df\,dx$ without the wedge and then treat the multiplication as commutative. Keep the wedge visible until the order and repeated factors have been checked. Another error is to differentiate only coefficients with respect to coordinates already in the wedge; those are exactly the derivatives that disappear.

## Practice

1. Calculate $d(x^2y\,dz)$ on $\mathbb R^3$.
2. Let $\alpha=x\,dy$ and $\beta=z\,dx$. Verify the signed product rule directly.

### Worked answers

1. Since $d(x^2y)=2xy\,dx+x^2\,dy$, the result is $2xy\,dx\wedge dz+x^2\,dy\wedge dz$. There is no derivative in $z$ because the coefficient does not depend on $z$.
2. First $\alpha\wedge\beta=-xz\,dx\wedge dy$, so its derivative is $-x\,dz\wedge dx\wedge dy=-x\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz$. On the right, $d\alpha=dx\wedge dy$ and $d\beta=dz\wedge dx$. The term $d\alpha\wedge\beta$ has a repeated $dx$ and vanishes. The other term is $-x\,dy\wedge dz\wedge dx=-x\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz$, as required.
