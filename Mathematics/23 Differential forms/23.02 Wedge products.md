> [!TLDR]
> The wedge product combines alternating forms, adds their degrees, and introduces a sign when arguments or factors pass each other.

## Definition and normalization

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional real vector space. A $p$-form $\alpha\in\Lambda^pV^*$ and a $q$-form $\beta\in\Lambda^qV^*$ have a wedge product $\alpha\wedge\beta\in\Lambda^{p+q}V^*$. For covectors $a$ and $b$, the convention is

$$
(a\wedge b)(u,v)=a(u)b(v)-a(v)b(u).
$$

For general degrees, one precise definition is

$$
(\alpha\wedge\beta)(v_1,\ldots,v_{p+q})
=\frac1{p!q!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{p+q}}
\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)
\alpha(v_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,v_{\sigma(p)})
\beta(v_{\sigma(p+1)},\ldots,v_{\sigma(p+q)}).
$$

Here $S_m$ denotes all permutations of $m$ objects, and $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)$ is $+1$ for even permutations and $-1$ for odd ones. The factorials remove repeated contributions from the already alternating factors. Some texts define an alternation operator with a different normalization; the two-covector formula above fixes the convention used here.

On a manifold the same definition applies at each point. Coefficients are smooth functions. A zero-form is a function $f$, and $f\wedge\alpha=f\alpha$ is ordinary scalar multiplication.

## Rules and their reasons

The wedge product is bilinear and associative. Thus sums expand as in polynomial multiplication, while the order of basis covectors determines the sign. For degree-one factors,

$$
dx^i\wedge dx^j=-dx^j\wedge dx^i,
\qquad dx^i\wedge dx^i=0.
$$

The general exchange rule is **graded commutativity**:

$$
\alpha\wedge\beta=(-1)^{pq}\beta\wedge\alpha.
$$

To move a block of $p$ covectors through a block of $q$ covectors, each of the first $p$ factors must pass each of the last $q$ factors. There are $pq$ exchanges. Linearity extends this argument from basis wedges to all forms. A two-form therefore commutes with a one-form, since $(-1)^{2\cdot1}=1$, although its individual covectors still anticommute.

If $p$ is odd, $\alpha\wedge\alpha=-\alpha\wedge\alpha$, so $\alpha\wedge\alpha=0$. For even $p$, that conclusion does not follow. For example, on $\mathbb R^4$,

$$
\gamma=dx^1\wedge dx^2+dx^3\wedge dx^4,
\qquad
\gamma\wedge\gamma=2\,dx^1\wedge dx^2\wedge dx^3\wedge dx^4.
$$

The two mixed terms have the same sign, and the two repeated terms vanish. This is a useful check against the false rule that every form has zero square.

## Worked example: expand and collect

On $\mathbb R^3$, take $a=dx+2dy$ and $b=3dx-dy+dz$. Distributivity gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
a\wedge b
&=3dx\wedge dx-dx\wedge dy+dx\wedge dz\\
&\quad+6dy\wedge dx-2dy\wedge dy+2dy\wedge dz.
\end{aligned}
$$

Delete the repeated factors and change $dy\wedge dx$ to $-dx\wedge dy$:

$$
a\wedge b=-7dx\wedge dy+dx\wedge dz+2dy\wedge dz.
$$

Evaluated on $u=(1,0,0)$ and $v=(0,1,0)$, this is $-7$. Direct evaluation gives the same result: $a(u)b(v)-a(v)b(u)=1(-1)-2(3)=-7$. This checks both the normalization and the collected coefficient. The determinant interpretation in [[23.01 Alternating tensors]] explains this sign geometrically.

A wedge of $k$ covectors is called decomposable. General forms can be sums of decomposable forms. Do not assume every two-form is a single wedge: the four-dimensional $\gamma$ above cannot be one, because a single wedge $a\wedge b$ has square zero, while $\gamma$ does not.

## Practice

1. Simplify $(dx+dy)\wedge(dx-dy)$.
2. Let $\eta=dx\wedge dz$ and $\theta=dy$. Express $\theta\wedge\eta$ in the ordered basis $dx\wedge dy\wedge dz$, and compare it with $\eta\wedge\theta$.

### Worked answers

1. The expansion is $dx\wedge dx-dx\wedge dy+dy\wedge dx-dy\wedge dy$. The repeated factors vanish and the middle terms add, giving $-2dx\wedge dy$.
2. Starting with $dy\wedge dx\wedge dz$, one exchange gives $-dx\wedge dy\wedge dz$. Starting with $dx\wedge dz\wedge dy$, one exchange gives the same result. The two forms commute because their degrees are one and two, so the block exchange sign is $(-1)^2=1$.
