---
title: "Closed and exact forms"
---

> [!TLDR]
> A closed form has zero exterior derivative; an exact form has a global potential, which can fail to exist even when local potentials exist.

## Two distinct conditions

A smooth $k$-form $\omega$ is **closed** when $d\omega=0$. For $k\ge1$, it is **exact** when there is a globally defined smooth $(k-1)$-form $\eta$ with $\omega=d\eta$. The form $\eta$ is called a potential or primitive. Because $d^2=0$, every exact form is closed. The converse requires conditions on the domain.

For a one-form $\alpha=Pdx+Qdy$ on an open subset of $\mathbb R^2$, closedness is the pointwise derivative test

$$
\partial_xQ=\partial_yP.
$$

Exactness asks for a single smooth function $f$ throughout the domain with $\partial_xf=P$ and $\partial_yf=Q$. This is a global existence question. On a connected domain, two such functions differ by a constant, because their difference has zero differential.

If $\alpha=df$, then for a piecewise smooth path $c$ from $a$ to $b$,

$$
\int_c\alpha=f(b)-f(a).
$$

Indeed, $c^*df=d(f\circ c)$, so the ordinary fundamental theorem applies. In particular an exact one-form integrates to zero on every closed path. Conversely, on a path-connected open domain, if all closed-path integrals of a smooth one-form vanish, define $f(p)$ by integrating from a fixed base point to $p$. The vanishing condition makes the answer independent of path. Integration along a short coordinate segment then gives $\partial_if=\alpha_i$, so $df=\alpha$.

## Worked example: closed but not exact

On $U=\mathbb R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}$, let

$$
\alpha=\frac{-y\,dx+x\,dy}{x^2+y^2}.
$$

Set $P=-y/(x^2+y^2)$ and $Q=x/(x^2+y^2)$. The quotient rule gives

$$
\partial_xQ=\frac{y^2-x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2},
\qquad
\partial_yP=\frac{y^2-x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}.
$$

Hence $d\alpha=0$ on $U$. However, on $c(t)=(\cos t,\sin t)$, $0\le t\le2\pi$, the pullback is

$$
c^*\alpha=
\frac{(-\sin t)(-\sin t)+\cos t\cos t}{1}dt=dt.
$$

The integral is $2\pi$, so $\alpha$ is not exact on $U$. There is no globally defined real angle function with differential $\alpha$. A local angle branch does give a primitive on a suitable small neighborhood. Applying Stokes’ theorem to the full disk would be invalid because $\alpha$ is undefined at its center.

## The local Poincaré lemma

The **Poincaré lemma** states that a smooth closed form of positive degree on a star-shaped open subset of $\mathbb R^n$ is exact. Star-shaped about the origin means that $tx$ remains in the set whenever $x$ is in the set and $0\le t\le1$. Consequently a closed form on a manifold is exact in a sufficiently small coordinate neighborhood. Degree zero is different: closed zero-forms are locally constant functions, rather than derivatives of negative-degree forms.

Here is an explicit proof for one-forms. Write $\alpha=\sum_i a_i(x)dx^i$ with $\partial_ja_i=\partial_ia_j$, and define

$$
f(x)=\int_0^1\sum_i a_i(tx)x^i\,dt.
$$

Differentiate with respect to $x^j$ and use the closedness condition:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_jf
&=\int_0^1\left[a_j(tx)+t\sum_i x^i\partial_ja_i(tx)\right]dt\\
&=\int_0^1\frac{d}{dt}\bigl[t\,a_j(tx)\bigr]dt=a_j(x).
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus $df=\alpha$. For higher positive degrees the theorem uses the corresponding radial integration operator; the proof just given establishes its one-form case fully. The local theorem does not say that every domain is star-shaped, or that local primitives join into one global primitive. The distinction is recorded by de Rham cohomology: closed $k$-forms are considered equivalent when their difference is exact.

## Practice

1. Show that $\beta=2xy\,dx+x^2dy$ is exact on $\mathbb R^2$ and find its primitive.
2. Is $\gamma=y\,dx$ closed on $\mathbb R^2$? Could any restriction to an open disk be exact?

### Worked answers

1. Integrating $\partial_xf=2xy$ in $x$ gives $f=x^2y+h(y)$. Its $y$ derivative is $x^2+h'(y)$, which must equal $x^2$. Thus $h$ is constant, and $\beta=d(x^2y)$. The equality of cross derivatives also confirms closedness.
2. The derivative is $d\gamma=dy\wedge dx=-dx\wedge dy$, nonzero at every point. Every exact form is closed, so $\gamma$ is not exact on any nonempty open disk. A smaller open domain cannot remove a derivative obstruction that is present everywhere.
