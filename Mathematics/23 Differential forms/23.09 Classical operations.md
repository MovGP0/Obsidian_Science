> [!TLDR]
> In oriented Euclidean three-space, the metric and Hodge star translate exterior differentiation into gradient, curl, and divergence.

## The additional geometric choices

The exterior derivative $d$ is defined without a metric. The familiar vector operators require a metric, and curl also requires an orientation. Work here in $\mathbb R^3$ with Euclidean metric $g=dx^2+dy^2+dz^2$ and positive volume form $\mathrm{vol}=dx\wedge dy\wedge dz$. Coordinates $(x,y,z)$ are orthonormal and positively oriented.

For a vector field $X=X^x\partial_x+X^y\partial_y+X^z\partial_z$, its metric dual is the one-form

$$
X^\flat=g(X,\,\cdot\,)=X^x dx+X^y dy+X^z dz.
$$

The inverse map is written $\sharp$. These maps identify vectors and covectors using the chosen metric; they are not part of the definition of a differential form.

The **Hodge star** maps $k$-forms to $(3-k)$-forms. It is defined by $\alpha\wedge*\beta=\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\mathrm{vol}$ for forms of the same degree, where the inner product is induced by the metric. In these coordinates,

$$
\begin{aligned}
*dx&=dy\wedge dz,& *dy&=dz\wedge dx,& *dz&=dx\wedge dy,\\
*(dy\wedge dz)&=dx,& *(dz\wedge dx)&=dy,& *(dx\wedge dy)&=dz,\\
*1&=\mathrm{vol},& *\mathrm{vol}&=1.
\end{aligned}
$$

The cyclic order in the middle pair matters. For example $*(dx\wedge dz)=-dy$. In Euclidean dimension three, $*^2=1$ on all degrees because the general sign $(-1)^{k(3-k)}$ is always positive.

## Gradient, curl, and divergence

For a scalar function $f$, the gradient is characterized by $g(\operatorname{grad}f,Y)=df(Y)$ for every vector field $Y$. Therefore

$$
(\operatorname{grad}f)^\flat=df,
\qquad \operatorname{grad}f=(df)^\sharp.
$$

For $X=P\partial_x+Q\partial_y+R\partial_z$, differentiation gives

$$
dX^\flat=(\partial_xQ-\partial_yP)dx\wedge dy
+(\partial_yR-\partial_zQ)dy\wedge dz
+(\partial_zP-\partial_xR)dz\wedge dx.
$$

Taking the star converts this two-form to the one-form of the curl:

$$
(\operatorname{curl}X)^\flat=*dX^\flat.
$$

The two-form $*X^\flat=Pdy\wedge dz+Qdz\wedge dx+Rdx\wedge dy$ instead leads to divergence. Differentiate it and reorder the wedges:

$$
d(*X^\flat)=(\partial_xP+\partial_yQ+\partial_zR)\mathrm{vol},
\qquad \operatorname{div}X=*d*X^\flat.
$$

These equalities also specify which degrees occur: functions go to one-forms, one-forms to two-forms, and two-forms to three-forms. Ordinary coordinate formulas hide that degree sequence. In nonorthonormal coordinates the metric and volume coefficients must be included; the Cartesian formulas cannot be copied without change.

## Worked example and identities

Take $X=-y\partial_x+x\partial_y+z^2\partial_z$. Its one-form is $X^\flat=-y\,dx+x\,dy+z^2dz$. Then

$$
dX^\flat=-dy\wedge dx+dx\wedge dy+2z\,dz\wedge dz
=2dx\wedge dy.
$$

Thus $*dX^\flat=2dz$ and $\operatorname{curl}X=2\partial_z$. For divergence,

$$
*X^\flat=-y\,dy\wedge dz+x\,dz\wedge dx+z^2dx\wedge dy.
$$

The first two differentiated terms have repeated factors and vanish. The last gives $2z\,dz\wedge dx\wedge dy=2z\,\mathrm{vol}$. Hence $\operatorname{div}X=2z$.

The identity $d^2=0$ explains two classical relations. First, $(\operatorname{curl}\operatorname{grad}f)^\flat=*d(df)=0$. Second, since $*^2=1$, one obtains

$$
\operatorname{div}(\operatorname{curl}X)=*d*(*dX^\flat)=*d(dX^\flat)=0.
$$

Smoothness, or sufficient regularity for equality of mixed derivatives, is assumed. These are local differential identities. They do not prove that every curl-free field is a global gradient; the closed-versus-exact distinction in [[23.08 Closed and exact forms]] still applies.

Reversing orientation changes the Hodge star sign and hence changes the curl vector sign. Gradient and divergence remain the same; divergence uses the star twice. This is a useful check on which constructions depend on orientation.

## Practice

1. For $f=x^2y+z$, find $df$, $\operatorname{grad}f$, and its curl.
2. For $Y=x\partial_x+y\partial_y+z\partial_z$, use forms to find its divergence and curl.

### Worked answers

1. Differentiate the scalar coefficients: $df=2xy\,dx+x^2dy+dz$. Raising the index gives $\operatorname{grad}f=2xy\partial_x+x^2\partial_y+\partial_z$. Its exterior derivative is $2x\,dy\wedge dx+2x\,dx\wedge dy=0$, so its curl is zero.
2. The one-form is $Y^\flat=x\,dx+y\,dy+z\,dz$ and its derivative is zero, since each summand produces a repeated covector. Thus curl is zero. Also $*Y^\flat=xdy\wedge dz+ydz\wedge dx+zdx\wedge dy$. Its derivative is $3\,\mathrm{vol}$, so divergence is three.
