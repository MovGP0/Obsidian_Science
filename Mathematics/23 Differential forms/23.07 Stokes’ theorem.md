title: "Stokes’ theorem"

> [!TLDR]
> Stokes’ theorem equates the integral of an exterior derivative over an oriented region with the integral of the original form over its induced boundary.

## Statement and orientation

Let $M$ be a compact oriented smooth manifold of dimension $m$, with boundary $\partial M$. Let $\omega$ be a smooth $(m-1)$-form, smooth up to the boundary. The general Stokes theorem states

$$
\int_M d\omega=\int_{\partial M}\omega.
$$

On the right, the form means its pullback by the boundary inclusion. More generally, on a noncompact manifold the theorem holds for compactly supported forms under the same smoothness conditions. Piecewise smooth regions with corners, such as rectangles, obey the corresponding version; edges of higher codimension do not add separate terms to this formula.

The boundary orientation is fixed by the **outward-first** convention. At a boundary point choose an outward transverse vector $n$. A tangent basis $(v_1,\ldots,v_{m-1})$ is positive on $\partial M$ when $(n,v_1,\ldots,v_{m-1})$ is positive on $M$. For the usual orientation $dx\wedge dy$ on the plane, this gives counterclockwise traversal around an outer boundary. For a hole, the positive boundary direction is clockwise. The boundary direction is induced by the region; it is not an independent choice.

## The local calculation behind the theorem

Take a rectangle $R=[a,b]\times[c,d]$ oriented by $dx\wedge dy$, and a one-form $\omega=P(x,y)dx+Q(x,y)dy$, with $P,Q$ continuously differentiable near $R$. By the exterior derivative rules,

$$
d\omega=(\partial_xQ-\partial_yP)dx\wedge dy.
$$

Integrating $\partial_xQ$ in $x$ gives $Q(b,y)-Q(a,y)$ by the fundamental theorem of calculus. Integrating $-\partial_yP$ in $y$ gives $P(x,c)-P(x,d)$. Thus

$$
\int_Rd\omega=
\int_a^bP(x,c)dx+\int_c^dQ(b,y)dy
-\int_a^bP(x,d)dx-\int_c^dQ(a,y)dy.
$$

These are exactly the bottom, right, top, and left boundary integrals in their induced directions. This proves the planar rectangular case. The full theorem is obtained by local coordinate versions and a partition of unity; contributions on internal faces cancel because neighboring oriented pieces induce opposite face orientations. That description explains the general proof structure, while the complete calculation here proves the stated local case.

## Worked example: both sides on a rectangle

Let $R=[0,2]\times[0,1]$ and $\omega=x^2dy$. Then $d\omega=2x\,dx\wedge dy$, so

$$
\int_Rd\omega=\int_0^2\int_0^1 2x\,dy\,dx
=\int_0^2 2x\,dx=4.
$$

On the bottom and top edges, $dy=0$, so both contributions vanish. On the right edge, $x=2$ and $y$ increases from zero to one; its contribution is $\int_0^1 4dy=4$. On the left edge $x=0$, so its contribution vanishes even though $y$ decreases. The boundary total is four, as required.

For $m=1$, $\omega$ is a function and the theorem becomes $\int_a^bdf=f(b)-f(a)$. The endpoint $b$ has positive boundary orientation and $a$ has negative orientation. For $m=2$, the coordinate formula above is Green’s theorem. In higher dimensions it includes the usual surface and volume forms of vector calculus after a metric identifies vectors and forms; see [[23.09 Classical operations]].

## Limits and checks

An exact top-degree form integrates to zero on a compact manifold with no boundary. The same conclusion applies when a closed integration cycle is the boundary of a region on which the original form is smooth. A singularity inside the region invalidates that application: deleting the singularity introduces a new boundary component. The punctured-plane example in [[23.08 Closed and exact forms]] illustrates why this qualification matters.

## Practice

1. Use Stokes’ theorem to integrate $-y\,dx+x\,dy$ around the counterclockwise unit circle.
2. On the annulus $1\le x^2+y^2\le4$, what directions are induced on its two circular boundary components? Calculate the boundary integral of $x\,dy$.

### Worked answers

1. The derivative is $2dx\wedge dy$. Its integral over the oriented unit disk is twice the disk area, or $2\pi$. Smoothness holds on the full disk, so Stokes’ theorem applies.
2. The outer circle is counterclockwise and the inner circle is clockwise. Since $d(x\,dy)=dx\wedge dy$, the total is the annulus area $4\pi-\pi=3\pi$. Separately, the outer circle contributes $4\pi$ and the inner circle contributes $-\pi$, with the negative sign fixed by its induced orientation.
