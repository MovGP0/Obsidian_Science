---
title: "Pullbacks of differential forms"
---

> [!TLDR]
> A pullback transfers forms from the target of a smooth map to its source by applying the derivative to their vector arguments.

## Why the direction reverses

Let $F:M\to N$ be a smooth map between smooth manifolds. At $p\in M$, its derivative is the linear map $dF_p:T_pM\to T_{F(p)}N$. If $\omega$ is a $k$-form on $N$, its **pullback** is the $k$-form on $M$ defined by

$$
(F^*\omega)_p(v_1,\ldots,v_k)
=\omega_{F(p)}(dF_pv_1,\ldots,dF_pv_k).
$$

The vectors move forward through $dF_p$, so the form can be evaluated at the target point. The resulting rule takes vectors at the source and is therefore a form on the source. This explains why the arrow for forms reverses. No inverse of $F$ is required. The map can fail to be one-to-one or have different source and target dimensions.

For functions, $F^*f=f\circ F$. For a target coordinate $x^i$, one has $F^*(dx^i)=d(x^i\circ F)$. If source coordinates are $u^1,\ldots,u^m$ and $F^i=x^i\circ F$, then

$$
F^*(dx^i)=\sum_a\frac{\partial F^i}{\partial u^a}du^a.
$$

Pullback is linear, preserves degree, and preserves wedge products:

$$
F^*(\alpha\wedge\beta)=F^*\alpha\wedge F^*\beta.
$$

Thus one can pull back a coordinate expression by composing every coefficient with $F$ and replacing every $dx^i$ with $dF^i$. Both steps are necessary. For composable maps $F:M\to N$ and $G:N\to P$, the chain rule gives $(G\circ F)^*=F^*G^*$. This is another instance of reversed direction.

## Worked example: a polar coordinate map

Let $F(r,\theta)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)$, defined for $r>0$ and $\theta\in\mathbb R$. Its target coordinates are $(x,y)$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
F^*dx&=\cos\theta\,dr-r\sin\theta\,d\theta,\\
F^*dy&=\sin\theta\,dr+r\cos\theta\,d\theta.
\end{aligned}
$$

Wedge these expressions. Terms with $dr\wedge dr$ or $d\theta\wedge d\theta$ vanish, leaving

$$
F^*(dx\wedge dy)=r(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta)dr\wedge d\theta
=r\,dr\wedge d\theta.
$$

For $\alpha=-y\,dx+x\,dy$, compose the coefficients as well:

$$
\begin{aligned}
F^*\alpha
&=-r\sin\theta(\cos\theta\,dr-r\sin\theta\,d\theta)\\
&\quad+r\cos\theta(\sin\theta\,dr+r\cos\theta\,d\theta)\\
&=r^2d\theta.
\end{aligned}
$$

This calculation uses the map on its whole source; a globally single-valued angle function on the punctured plane has not been assumed.

## Why pullback commutes with differentiation

For every smooth form,

$$
d(F^*\omega)=F^*(d\omega).
$$

For a function this is exactly the chain rule. For a coordinate generator $dx^i$, the left side is $d(dF^i)=0$ and the right side is $F^*(d(dx^i))=0$. Every coordinate form is a sum of functions times wedges of these generators. The product rule for $d$, together with preservation of wedges by $F^*$, extends the equality to every term. This argument uses the same degree signs on both sides.

The worked example checks the identity: $d\alpha=2dx\wedge dy$, so $F^*(d\alpha)=2r\,dr\wedge d\theta$. Meanwhile $d(F^*\alpha)=d(r^2d\theta)=2r\,dr\wedge d\theta$.

If $k>\dim M$, every pulled-back $k$-form is zero. More generally, if $dF_p$ has rank below $k$, the images of any $k$ vectors are dependent, so $(F^*\omega)_p=0$. This is a consequence of alternation, not a failure of the definition. Pullbacks are the basic calculation step in [[23.06 Integration]].

## Practice

1. For the curve $c(t)=(t,t^2)$, calculate $c^*(x\,dy-y\,dx)$.
2. Let $F(u,v)=(u+v,u-v)$. Calculate $F^*(dx\wedge dy)$ and interpret its sign.

### Worked answers

1. Substitute $x=t$, $y=t^2$, $dx=dt$, and $dy=2t\,dt$. The result is $t(2t\,dt)-t^2dt=t^2dt$.
2. The replacement gives $(du+dv)\wedge(du-dv)=-2du\wedge dv$. The determinant of the derivative is $-2$, so the map reverses the usual orientation and multiplies oriented coordinate area by negative two.
