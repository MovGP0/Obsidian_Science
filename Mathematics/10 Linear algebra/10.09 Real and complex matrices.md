title: "Real and complex matrices"

> [!TLDR]
> The conjugate transpose is the correct adjoint for complex coordinates; Hermitian matrices have real eigenvalues and unitary maps preserve inner products.

## Transpose and conjugate transpose

For an $m\times n$ matrix $A=(a_{ij})$, the **transpose** $A^T$ has entries $(A^T)_{ij}=a_{ji}$ and size $n\times m$. For complex entries, write $\overline z$ for complex conjugation and define the **conjugate transpose**, or adjoint, by

$$
A^*=\overline A^{\,T},\qquad (A^*)_{ij}=\overline{a_{ji}}.
$$

For example, if $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\2-i&3\end{pmatrix}$, then $A^T=\begin{pmatrix}1&2-i\\i&3\end{pmatrix}$ but $A^*=\begin{pmatrix}1&2+i\\-i&3\end{pmatrix}$. For real matrices, conjugation does nothing and $A^*=A^T$.

Both operations reverse product order: $(AB)^T=B^TA^T$ and $(AB)^*=B^*A^*$. They also satisfy $(A^*)^*=A$. For scalars, $(cA)^*=\overline c A^*$; the conjugation of $c$ is essential.

## Inner products explain the adjoint

Use the complex inner product $\langle x,y\rangle=x^*y=\sum_j\overline{x_j}y_j$, conjugate linear in its first argument and linear in its second. Its norm is $\|x\|=\sqrt{x^*x}$. Then

$$
\langle Ax,y\rangle=(Ax)^*y=x^*A^*y=\langle x,A^*y\rangle.
$$

This equality explains why conjugation accompanies transposition. Without it, $x^Tx$ need not be positive: for $x=(1,i)^T$ it equals zero, while $x^*x=2$.

A real square matrix is **symmetric** if $A^T=A$. A complex square matrix is **Hermitian** if $A^*=A$. Hermitian diagonal entries are real, and off-diagonal entries on opposite sides of the diagonal are conjugate pairs. Complex symmetry $A^T=A$ alone is a different property and does not imply real eigenvalues.

## Worked example: a Hermitian matrix

Consider

$$
H=\begin{pmatrix}2&i\\-i&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Taking its conjugate transpose returns $H$, so it is Hermitian. Its characteristic polynomial is

$$
\det(tI-H)=(t-2)^2-(-i)(i)=(t-2)^2-1,
$$

with roots $3$ and $1$. For $3$, $-x+iy=0$, so choose $v_3=(i,1)^T$. For $1$, $x+iy=0$, so choose $v_1=(-i,1)^T$. Their inner product is

$$
v_3^*v_1=(-i)(-i)+1=-1+1=0.
$$

Each vector has squared norm two. Thus $U=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}i&-i\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ has orthonormal columns and $U^*HU=\operatorname{diag}(3,1)$.

For any Hermitian matrix, an eigenvalue is real: if $Hv=\lambda v$ with $v\ne0$, then $v^*Hv=\lambda v^*v$. The left side is real because its conjugate equals $v^*H^*v=v^*Hv$, and $v^*v>0$. Eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are orthogonal: from $Hu=\lambda u$ and $Hv=\mu v$, the adjoint identity gives $\lambda\langle u,v\rangle=\mu\langle u,v\rangle$, using the reality of $\lambda$.

The spectral theorem states that every finite Hermitian matrix has a complete orthonormal eigenbasis. A proof can proceed by choosing an eigenvector over $\mathbb C$, showing that its orthogonal complement is invariant, and applying induction to that complement. The real symmetric case gives an orthonormal basis of real eigenvectors.

## Orthogonal and unitary maps

A real square matrix $Q$ is **orthogonal** if $Q^TQ=I$. A complex square matrix $U$ is **unitary** if $U^*U=I$. Their columns are orthonormal, their inverses are $Q^T$ and $U^*$, and

$$
\langle Ux,Uy\rangle=x^*U^*Uy=x^*y.
$$

Thus these maps preserve inner products, lengths, and orthogonality. A unitary matrix need not be Hermitian: $\operatorname{diag}(i,1)$ is unitary but has $U^*\ne U$. Its eigenvalues have modulus one. To see the modulus condition in general, $Uv=\lambda v$ gives $\|v\|=\|Uv\|=|\lambda|\|v\|$.

## Practice

1. Find the adjoint of $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\0&2-i\end{pmatrix}$. Is $A$ Hermitian?
2. Show that $Q=\frac15\begin{pmatrix}3&-4\\4&3\end{pmatrix}$ is orthogonal and find its inverse.

## Worked answers

1. Transpose and conjugate every entry: $A^*=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\-i&2+i\end{pmatrix}$. It differs from $A$, so $A$ is not Hermitian. In particular, the diagonal entry $2-i$ is not real.
2. Each column has squared length $(9+16)/25=1$, and their dot product is $(-12+12)/25=0$. Hence $Q^TQ=I$. Its inverse is $Q^T=\frac15\begin{pmatrix}3&4\\-4&3\end{pmatrix}$. The column calculation checks the full matrix identity without any assumption about the diagram of a rotation.
