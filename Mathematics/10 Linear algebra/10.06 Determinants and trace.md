title: "Determinants and trace"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Determinant and trace are scalar invariants of a linear endomorphism; they stay the same when its matrix changes by similarity.

## Two numbers from a square matrix

For an $n\times n$ matrix $A=(a_{ij})$ over $\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$, its **trace** is the sum of its diagonal entries:

$$
\operatorname{tr}A=\sum_{i=1}^n a_{ii}.
$$

Its **determinant** can be defined by a signed sum over all permutations $\sigma$ of $1,\ldots,n$:

$$
\det A=\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)
\prod_{i=1}^n a_{i,\sigma(i)}.
$$

Here $S_n$ is the set of permutations and $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)$ is $+1$ for an even permutation and $-1$ for an odd permutation. Each product selects one entry in every row and every column. In two dimensions this gives $\det\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}=ad-bc$.

Over $\mathbb R$, the determinant is the signed volume factor of the map. Its absolute value scales volume, and its sign records whether orientation is preserved. A zero determinant means that the columns are dependent, so the map collapses dimension and cannot have an inverse. The determinant is defined only for square matrices; rank remains meaningful for rectangular ones.

## Calculation and row operations

The determinant is linear in each row separately and changes sign when two rows are exchanged. These properties give practical rules:

- Swapping two rows multiplies the determinant by $-1$.
- Multiplying one row by $c$ multiplies the determinant by $c$.
- Adding a multiple of another row leaves the determinant unchanged.

The last rule follows from row linearity: the extra term has two proportional rows and therefore determinant zero. For a triangular matrix, only the product of diagonal entries survives in the permutation formula. Elimination therefore gives a short determinant calculation if all row factors and swaps are tracked.

The trace has no matching invariance under arbitrary row operations. It is linear as a function of the entire matrix, but replacing one row by a combination can change its diagonal entry.

## Worked example: elimination with a sign

Let

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&2&1\\1&3&0\\2&1&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Swap the first two rows to form $B$. Then subtract twice its first row from its third row:

$$
B=\begin{pmatrix}1&3&0\\0&2&1\\2&1&1\end{pmatrix}
\longrightarrow
\begin{pmatrix}1&3&0\\0&2&1\\0&-5&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Next add $5/2$ times row two to row three. The triangular result has diagonal $1,2,7/2$, so its determinant is $7$. The row additions preserve $\det B$, but the initial swap gives $\det B=-\det A$. Consequently $\det A=-7$. Independently, expansion along the first row gives $-2(1)+1(1-6)=-7$.

The original trace is $0+3+1=4$. The triangular matrix has trace $1+2+7/2=13/2$. This difference shows why elimination preserves the determinant only with tracked rules and does not preserve trace in general.

## Why basis change preserves both

One endomorphism in two bases has matrices $A$ and $P^{-1}AP$, with $P$ invertible; see [[10.03 Matrices]]. The multiplicative determinant rule $\det(XY)=\det X\det Y$ gives

$$
\det(P^{-1}AP)=\det(P)^{-1}\det(A)\det(P)=\det A.
$$

For compatible rectangular matrices $X$ and $Y$, direct summation proves the cyclic trace rule:

$$
\operatorname{tr}(XY)
=\sum_i\sum_j x_{ij}y_{ji}
=\sum_j\sum_i y_{ji}x_{ij}
=\operatorname{tr}(YX).
$$

Apply it with $X=P^{-1}A$ and $Y=P$ to obtain $\operatorname{tr}(P^{-1}AP)=\operatorname{tr}(APP^{-1})=\operatorname{tr}A$. These are invariants of the map, even though individual diagonal entries depend on the basis. Over $\mathbb C$, trace equals the sum of eigenvalues and determinant their product, with algebraic multiplicity. This remains true without an eigenbasis, as the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial show.

## Practice

1. Calculate determinant and trace of $M=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\4&3\end{pmatrix}$. What happens to them when its first row is doubled?
2. For $n\times n$ matrices, compare $\det(2A)$ and $\operatorname{tr}(2A)$ with the corresponding values for $A$.

## Worked answers

1. $\det M=2\cdot3-1\cdot4=2$ and $\operatorname{tr}M=5$. Doubling row one gives $\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\4&3\end{pmatrix}$, with determinant $4$ and trace $7$. The determinant doubles; the trace does not.
2. Each of the $n$ rows is doubled, so $\det(2A)=2^n\det A$. Each diagonal entry is doubled, so $\operatorname{tr}(2A)=2\operatorname{tr}A$. Confusing a factor on one row with a factor on the whole matrix loses the exponent $n$.
