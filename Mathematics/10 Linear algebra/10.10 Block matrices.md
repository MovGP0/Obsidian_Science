title: "Block matrices"

> [!TLDR]
> Block matrices group coordinates into subspaces; elimination with an invertible diagonal block produces a Schur complement that controls the remaining system.

## Partition a linear map

Suppose the input space is a direct sum $X\oplus Y$ and the output space is $U\oplus V$. A linear map between them can be written

$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\C&D\end{pmatrix},\qquad
M\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}Ax+By\\Cx+Dy\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Here $A:X\to U$, $B:Y\to U$, $C:X\to V$, and $D:Y\to V$. The blocks can be rectangular, but sizes must agree along each row and column. A block is a whole map between coordinate groups, not a new kind of scalar.

If a second block matrix has compatible partitions, ordinary multiplication gives

$$
\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\C&D\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}E&F\\G&H\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}AE+BG&AF+BH\\CE+DG&CF+DH\end{pmatrix}.
$$

For example, the first output group receives $A(Ex+Fy)+B(Gx+Hy)$, which expands to $(AE+BG)x+(AF+BH)y$. This proves the first block row formula and fixes the multiplication order. Even square blocks generally do not commute, so moving a factor from one side to the other is not allowed.

## Elimination and the Schur complement

Now suppose $M$ is square, $A$ is $p\times p$, $D$ is $q\times q$, and $A$ is invertible. To solve

$$
Ax+By=f,\qquad Cx+Dy=g,
$$

first solve the top equation for $x=A^{-1}(f-By)$. Substitute this expression into the bottom equation:

$$
(D-CA^{-1}B)y=g-CA^{-1}f.
$$

The matrix $S=D-CA^{-1}B$ is the **Schur complement of $A$**. The order $CA^{-1}B$ follows from substitution and must be preserved. The same elimination is a factorization:

$$
M=
\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\CA^{-1}&I\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\0&S\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The first factor has determinant one. The second is block triangular, so

$$
\det M=\det A\det S.
$$

This block triangular determinant rule follows by grouping terms in the ordinary determinant expansion; the zero lower-left block prevents terms that mix unmatched row and column groups. Under the assumption that $A$ is invertible, $M$ is invertible exactly when $S$ is invertible.

## Derive the block inverse

If both $A$ and $S$ are invertible, the eliminated system gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
y&=S^{-1}g-S^{-1}CA^{-1}f,\\
x&=(A^{-1}+A^{-1}BS^{-1}CA^{-1})f-A^{-1}BS^{-1}g.
\end{aligned}
$$

Reading off the coefficients of $f$ and $g$ produces

$$
M^{-1}=
\begin{pmatrix}
A^{-1}+A^{-1}BS^{-1}CA^{-1}&-A^{-1}BS^{-1}\\
-S^{-1}CA^{-1}&S^{-1}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The added upper-left term accounts for the coupling through the second coordinate group. Inverting only $A$ and $D$ would miss this effect. If $A$ is singular, the displayed formula does not apply, although $M$ itself can still be invertible. When $D$ is invertible instead, eliminate the bottom equation first and use the other Schur complement $A-BD^{-1}C$.

## Worked example with a two-dimensional first block

Let

$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}2&0&1\\0&2&1\\1&1&3\end{pmatrix},\quad
A=2I_2,\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},\quad
C=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\end{pmatrix},\quad D=(3).
$$

Here $A^{-1}=\tfrac12I_2$, so $CA^{-1}B=\tfrac12+\tfrac12=1$. Thus $S=(2)$ and $\det M=(2\cdot2)\cdot2=8$. The block inverse formula gives

$$
M^{-1}=\frac18\begin{pmatrix}5&1&-2\\1&5&-2\\-2&-2&4\end{pmatrix}.
$$

For example, the upper-left correction is $\tfrac18\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$, added to $\tfrac12I_2$. To check one whole product row, the first row of $MM^{-1}$ is $\tfrac18(2(5,1,-2)+(-2,-2,4))=(1,0,0)$. The second row similarly gives $(0,1,0)$; the third gives $\tfrac18((5,1,-2)+(1,5,-2)+3(-2,-2,4))=(0,0,1)$.

## Practice

1. Use a Schur complement to find the determinant of $N=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\3&4\end{pmatrix}$, treating entries as one-dimensional blocks.
2. Is $K=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ invertible? Explain why the formula using its upper-left block cannot decide this directly.

## Worked answers

1. Take $A=(2)$, so $A^{-1}=(1/2)$ and $S=(4-3(1/2)1)=(5/2)$. Therefore $\det N=2\cdot5/2=5$. This agrees with $2\cdot4-1\cdot3$.
2. Multiplication gives $K^2=I$, so $K^{-1}=K$. Its upper-left block is zero and has no inverse, so the stated Schur formula has an unmet hypothesis. The whole matrix exchanges two coordinate groups and loses no information. A singular diagonal block does not force a singular full matrix.
