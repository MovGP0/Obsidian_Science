> [!TLDR]
> An eigenvector is a nonzero vector whose image stays in its own line; the corresponding eigenvalue gives the scale and sign.

## Invariant directions

Let $T:V\to V$ be a linear endomorphism of a finite-dimensional vector space over $\mathbb F$, either $\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$. A scalar $\lambda\in\mathbb F$ is an **eigenvalue** if some nonzero vector $v$ satisfies $T(v)=\lambda v$. Such a $v$ is an **eigenvector**. The nonzero condition matters: the zero vector satisfies the equation for every scalar and cannot identify an eigenvalue.

All scalar multiples of an eigenvector lie in an invariant line: applying $T$ keeps them on that line. Over $\mathbb R$, a positive eigenvalue preserves direction, a negative one reverses it, and zero sends the entire line to zero. For a matrix $A$, the equation can be rewritten as

$$
(A-\lambda I)v=0.
$$

The **eigenspace** for $\lambda$ is $E_\lambda=\ker(A-\lambda I)$. It includes zero and is a subspace. Its nonzero members are precisely the eigenvectors with that eigenvalue.

## Finding eigenvalues and eigenspaces

The homogeneous equation has a nonzero solution exactly when $A-\lambda I$ is singular. Define the characteristic polynomial by

$$
p_A(t)=\det(tI-A).
$$

The eigenvalues are its roots in the chosen field. Using $\det(A-tI)$ instead changes the polynomial by $(-1)^n$ and leaves its roots unchanged. After finding a root, solve the linear system for $E_\lambda$; the determinant alone does not give the eigenvectors.

The **algebraic multiplicity** of an eigenvalue is its multiplicity as a polynomial root. Its **geometric multiplicity** is $\dim E_\lambda$. For every eigenvalue,

$$
1\le\dim E_\lambda\le\text{algebraic multiplicity of }\lambda.
$$

For the upper bound, choose a basis of $E_\lambda$ and extend it to the whole space. In this basis the matrix is block upper triangular, with $\lambda I$ on the first diagonal block. The characteristic polynomial therefore contains at least as many factors $t-\lambda$ as the dimension of that eigenspace.

## Worked example: two invariant lines

Let $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$. Compute

$$
p_A(t)=\det\begin{pmatrix}t-2&-1\\-1&t-2\end{pmatrix}
=(t-2)^2-1=(t-3)(t-1).
$$

For $\lambda=3$, the equation $(A-3I)v=0$ is $-x+y=0$, so

$$
E_3=\operatorname{span}((1,1)).
$$

For $\lambda=1$, it is $x+y=0$, so $E_1=\operatorname{span}((1,-1))$. Direct checks give $A(1,1)^T=(3,3)^T$ and $A(1,-1)^T=(1,-1)^T$. Each eigenvalue has algebraic and geometric multiplicity one.

![[math-10-eigen-directions.svg|**Fig. 10.2** The symmetric map stretches one diagonal eigenline by three and leaves the other unchanged; the unit circle becomes an ellipse.]]

Any input has a unique decomposition $v=a(1,1)+b(1,-1)$, so its output is $Av=3a(1,1)+b(1,-1)$. The circle and ellipse in the figure show the effect on many vectors at once. Only vectors on the diagonal eigenlines keep their line. This decomposition leads to [[10.08 Diagonalization]].

## Repeated roots and the scalar field

The matrix $J=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$ has characteristic polynomial $(t-2)^2$. But $(J-2I)(x,y)^T=(y,0)^T$, so its eigenspace is only $\operatorname{span}((1,0))$. Algebraic multiplicity two does not supply two independent eigenvectors.

The real matrix $R=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ has characteristic polynomial $t^2+1$. It has no real eigenvalues and no invariant real line. Over $\mathbb C$, the roots are $i$ and $-i$, and complex eigenvectors exist. Always state the field before deciding whether roots or eigenvectors exist.

## Practice

1. Find the eigenvalues and eigenspaces of $D=\operatorname{diag}(2,2,-1)$ over $\mathbb R$.
2. Find the eigenvalues of $B=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$ and one eigenvector for each. Check the trace and determinant.

## Worked answers

1. The polynomial is $(t-2)^2(t+1)$. For $\lambda=2$, the third component must be zero and the first two are free, so $E_2=\operatorname{span}((1,0,0),(0,1,0))$. For $\lambda=-1$, the first two components vanish, so $E_{-1}=\operatorname{span}((0,0,1))$. The dimensions are two and one.
2. Triangular form gives $p_B(t)=(t-1)(t-3)$. At $\lambda=1$, $2y=0$, so use $(1,0)$. At $\lambda=3$, $-2x+2y=0$, so use $(1,1)$. The sum $1+3=4$ matches the trace and the product $1\cdot3=3$ matches the determinant.
