> [!TLDR]
> A vector space permits addition and scalar multiplication; a basis gives each vector a unique set of coordinates.

## What makes a vector space

A **field** is a set of scalars with addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division by nonzero elements. In this article the field $\mathbb F$ is either the real numbers $\mathbb R$ or the complex numbers $\mathbb C$. A vector space $V$ over $\mathbb F$ is a set of objects called vectors, together with vector addition and scalar multiplication. A vector need not be an arrow: polynomials, matrices, and functions can also be vectors.

For vectors $u,v,w\in V$ and scalars $a,b\in\mathbb F$, the operations must satisfy these axioms:

- Addition stays in $V$, is commutative, and is associative: $u+v=v+u$ and $(u+v)+w=u+(v+w)$.
- A zero vector $0$ satisfies $v+0=v$, and each $v$ has an additive inverse $-v$.
- Scalar multiplication stays in $V$, satisfies $a(bv)=(ab)v$, and has $1v=v$.
- Both distributive laws hold: $a(u+v)=au+av$ and $(a+b)v=av+bv$.

These conditions imply $0v=0$ and $a0=0$. For example, $0v=(0+0)v=0v+0v$; adding the inverse of $0v$ to both sides gives $0v=0$. In $\mathbb R^n$, addition and scalar multiplication act component by component, so each axiom follows from the corresponding rule for real numbers.

## Subspaces and span

A **subspace** $W\subseteq V$ uses the same operations and scalar field as $V$ and is itself a vector space. A short test is sufficient: $W$ must contain $0$ and must contain $au+bv$ whenever $u,v\in W$ and $a,b\in\mathbb F$. This test supplies closure; the other axioms are inherited from $V$.

The **span** of $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ is the set of all their linear combinations:

$$
\operatorname{span}(v_1,\ldots,v_k)
=\{a_1v_1+\cdots+a_kv_k:a_i\in\mathbb F\}.
$$

It is a subspace because sums and scalar multiples of such combinations are again combinations. It is also the smallest subspace that contains all the listed vectors. A translated line that misses the origin is not a subspace: its zero vector is missing.

## Independence, basis, and dimension

Vectors $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ are **linearly independent** if

$$
a_1v_1+\cdots+a_kv_k=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
a_1=\cdots=a_k=0.
$$

Otherwise they are dependent, and at least one vector can be written as a combination of the others. A **basis** is an independent spanning list. Spanning gives an expression for every vector. Independence makes that expression unique: subtract two expressions and use the independence condition.

The **dimension** of a finite-dimensional space is the number of vectors in any basis. All bases have the same number of vectors. The key reason is that an independent list cannot have more members than a spanning list: successively replace members of a spanning list by independent vectors without losing the span. Reversing the roles of two bases gives equality. The zero space has the empty basis and dimension zero.

## Worked example: a plane inside ℝ³

Let

$$
W=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb R^3:x+y-z=0\}.
$$

The equation is homogeneous, so $0\in W$. If two vectors satisfy it, every linear combination does too, because $x+y-z$ distributes over that combination. Thus $W$ is a subspace.

Solve the defining equation for $z$: $z=x+y$. Then

$$
(x,y,z)=x(1,0,1)+y(0,1,1).
$$

The two displayed vectors span $W$. If $a(1,0,1)+b(0,1,1)=0$, the first two coordinates give $a=b=0$. They are therefore a basis, and $\dim W=2$. The vector $(3,-2,1)$ has coordinates $(3,-2)$ in this basis. Its three ambient components and its two basis coordinates serve different purposes.

## Practice

1. Does $S=\{(x,y):x+y=1\}$ form a subspace of $\mathbb R^2$?
2. Find a basis and dimension for $\operatorname{span}((1,2),(2,4),(0,1))$.

## Worked answers

1. The zero vector fails the equation because $0+0\ne1$. Thus $S$ is not a subspace. It is an affine line, a translate of the subspace $x+y=0$.
2. The second vector is twice the first, so remove it without changing the span. If $a(1,2)+b(0,1)=0$, the first coordinate gives $a=0$ and the second then gives $b=0$. The remaining two vectors are independent. They span all of $\mathbb R^2$, since $(x,y)=x(1,2)+(y-2x)(0,1)$. They form a basis and the dimension is two.
