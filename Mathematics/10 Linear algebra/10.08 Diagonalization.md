> [!TLDR]
> A matrix can be diagonalized exactly when its vector space has a basis of eigenvectors over the chosen scalar field.

## A basis adapted to a map

Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ matrix over $\mathbb F$, either $\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$. It is **diagonalizable over $\mathbb F$** if there are an invertible matrix $P$ over that field and a diagonal matrix $D$ such that

$$
P^{-1}AP=D,\qquad\text{equivalently }A=PDP^{-1}.
$$

The columns of $P$ are a new ordered basis written in old coordinates. If those columns are $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ and $D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)$, then $AP=PD$ says, column by column,

$$
Av_j=\lambda_jv_j.
$$

Thus diagonalization is exactly the existence of an **eigenbasis**. A diagonal matrix scales each coordinate separately because there is no mixing between the basis directions. The order of the columns of $P$ must match the order of eigenvalues in $D$.

## A complete test

Find the eigenvalues and bases of their eigenspaces as in [[10.07 Eigenvalues and eigenvectors]]. A diagonalization exists if these eigenspace bases together contain $n$ vectors. Eigenvectors from distinct eigenvalues are independent. One proof uses a shortest nontrivial dependence $\sum_j c_jv_j=0$. Apply $A-\lambda_kI$ to eliminate one term. The remaining coefficients are $c_j(\lambda_j-\lambda_k)$, all nonzero for distinct eigenvalues, which contradicts minimality.

Consequently, $n$ distinct eigenvalues guarantee diagonalizability. Distinct roots are sufficient, but not necessary: the identity matrix has only eigenvalue one and every basis is an eigenbasis.

More generally, diagonalizability requires the characteristic polynomial to split into linear factors over $\mathbb F$ and the geometric multiplicity of each eigenvalue to equal its algebraic multiplicity. To justify this criterion, different eigenspaces form a direct sum by the independence argument. When the multiplicities agree, their dimensions sum to the degree $n$ of the characteristic polynomial. Conversely, an eigenbasis supplies the full number of independent eigenvectors for every repeated eigenvalue.

## Worked example: diagonalization and powers

Take $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$. Its eigenvectors $(1,1)^T$ and $(1,-1)^T$ have eigenvalues $3$ and $1$. Set

$$
P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad
D=\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad
P^{-1}=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The determinant of $P$ is $-2$, so its columns are a basis. Check $AP=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\3&-1\end{pmatrix}=PD$; multiplication by $P^{-1}$ gives the required diagonalization.

For a nonnegative integer $k$, adjacent inverse factors cancel in a product of $k$ copies of $PDP^{-1}$. Therefore

$$
A^k=PD^kP^{-1}
=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}3^k+1&3^k-1\\3^k-1&3^k+1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

For $k=2$, this gives $A^2=\begin{pmatrix}5&4\\4&5\end{pmatrix}$. Direct multiplication of $A$ by itself gives the same result. For $k=0$ the formula gives $I$. Negative powers are also allowed here because neither eigenvalue is zero. In general a diagonalization with a zero eigenvalue does not permit negative powers.

## Why diagonalization can fail

Consider $J=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$. The characteristic polynomial splits as $(t-2)^2$, but its eigenvectors all satisfy $y=0$. Only one independent eigenvector exists, so no invertible $P$ can be formed from eigenvectors. This failure cannot be repaired by choosing two different multiples of $(1,0)$; those columns remain dependent.

A different failure occurs for a real quarter-turn $R=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$. Its polynomial $t^2+1$ does not split over $\mathbb R$, so it is not diagonalizable over $\mathbb R$. Over $\mathbb C$ it has distinct eigenvalues $i,-i$ and is diagonalizable. The field is part of the statement, not a minor calculation choice.

Do not confuse diagonalization with row reduction. Row reduction multiplies by elementary matrices on one side and can reduce any invertible matrix to the identity. Diagonalization is a simultaneous input and output basis change, which must have the form $P^{-1}AP$. Real symmetric matrices have an orthonormal eigenbasis, a stronger result discussed in [[10.09 Real and complex matrices]].

## Practice

1. Is $\operatorname{diag}(4,4,0)$ diagonalizable? Is it invertible?
2. Diagonalize $B=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$ and calculate $B^2$ from the diagonalization.

## Worked answers

1. It is already diagonal, so take $P=I$ and $D$ equal to the matrix itself. It is not invertible because its third standard basis vector is sent to zero. Diagonalizable does not mean invertible.
2. Eigenvectors are $(1,0)^T$ for $1$ and $(1,1)^T$ for $3$. Take $P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, $P^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, and $D=\operatorname{diag}(1,3)$. Then $PD^2P^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&8\\0&9\end{pmatrix}$. Direct multiplication gives upper-right entry $1\cdot2+2\cdot3=8$, which checks the result.
