> [!TLDR]
> A Poisson table gives probabilities for a count at a stated mean; distinguish an exact-count probability from a cumulative probability or an upper tail.

## Define the model and the table entry

A random variable assigns a numerical outcome to an uncertain experiment. A count takes values $0,1,2,\ldots$. The Poisson model with parameter $\lambda>0$ has

$$
p_k=P(X=k)=e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!},\qquad k=0,1,2,\ldots.
$$

Here $P(X=k)$ means probability of exactly $k$ occurrences, and $k!$ is the factorial. The mean and variance are both $\lambda$. A Poisson model is appropriate for counts produced by a homogeneous Poisson process, which assumes independent increments and a constant average rate. A count alone does not establish these assumptions. This article uses mathematical counts in abstract trials and introduces no physical units. Definitions and distribution properties follow the [NIST Poisson distribution reference](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda366j.htm).

A **probability mass** is a probability at one integer. The cumulative distribution function (CDF) is $F(k)=P(X\le k)=\sum_{j=0}^kp_j$. A table may contain masses, CDF values, or quantiles. Read the heading before looking for a number. A mass table column does not add to one unless it includes every possible count or records the missing tail.

## Representative mass table

The following entries are $P(X=k)$, rounded to six decimal places. The row marked tail is the total probability at counts 6 and above.

| Count $k$ | $\lambda=1$ | $\lambda=2$ | $\lambda=3$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 0.367879 | 0.135335 | 0.049787 |
| 1 | 0.367879 | 0.270671 | 0.149361 |
| 2 | 0.183940 | 0.270671 | 0.224042 |
| 3 | 0.061313 | 0.180447 | 0.224042 |
| 4 | 0.015328 | 0.090224 | 0.168031 |
| 5 | 0.003066 | 0.036089 | 0.100819 |
| Tail $k\ge6$ | 0.000594 | 0.016564 | 0.083918 |

The table can be generated by $p_0=e^{-\lambda}$ and

$$
p_{k+1}=\frac{\lambda}{k+1}p_k.
$$

Divide the formula for $p_{k+1}$ by that for $p_k$ to derive this recurrence. It avoids calculating large factorials. For very large $\lambda$, $p_0$ can underflow in floating-point arithmetic; a production algorithm starts nearer the mode or works with logarithms. The recurrence remains an exact mathematical identity.

The probability masses sum to one because $e^{-\lambda}\sum\lambda^k/k!=e^{-\lambda}e^\lambda=1$. The exponential-series definition from [[Series and Fourier tables]] thus supplies a direct normalization check.

## Worked example: three different questions

Let $X$ have a Poisson distribution with $\lambda=2$. The probability of exactly three is $p_3=0.180447$ approximately. The probability of at most three is the sum of rows 0 through 3:

$$
F(3)\approx0.135335+0.270671+0.270671+0.180447=0.857124.
$$

The probability of at least three is $P(X\ge3)=1-P(X\le2)\approx1-0.676677=0.323323$. It is not $1-F(3)$, because that would exclude count 3. Rounding can cause a last-digit difference; exact calculations give $F(3)\approx0.85712346$ and $P(X\ge3)\approx0.32332358$.

## Quantiles and discreteness

Define a $p$-quantile as the smallest nonnegative integer $q_p$ with $F(q_p)\ge p$. For $\lambda=2$, $F(3)\approx0.8571$ and $F(4)\approx0.9473$. Thus the 90th percentile is 4. It does not give exactly 90% cumulative probability; a discrete CDF jumps. Do not interpolate to invent a fractional count quantile unless the problem explicitly defines a different interpolation rule.

The mean is not necessarily the most likely integer. From $p_{k+1}/p_k=\lambda/(k+1)$, masses increase while $k+1<\lambda$ and decrease afterward. For integer $\lambda$, the two counts $\lambda-1$ and $\lambda$ share a maximum; the table shows this for $\lambda=2$ and $\lambda=3$.

## Practice

1. For $\lambda=1$, find $P(X\ge2)$ without summing an infinite tail.
2. For $\lambda=3$, use the recurrence to calculate $p_2$ from $p_1$. Find the 50th-percentile count using cumulative masses.

> [!example]- Worked answers
> 1. $P(X\ge2)=1-p_0-p_1=1-2e^{-1}\approx0.26424112$.
> 2. $p_2=(3/2)p_1\approx0.22404181$. The cumulative probability at 2 is about 0.42319008 and at 3 is about 0.64723189, so the smallest count with CDF at least 0.5 is 3.
