> [!TLDR]
> Jacobi elliptic functions invert an elliptic integral and extend sine-like behavior to meromorphic functions with two independent complex periods.

## What an elliptic function means

Read [[Complex branches and elementary functions]], [[Laurent series and residues]], and [[08.04 Substitution]]. A nonconstant **elliptic function** is a meromorphic function $f$ on the complex plane with two periods $\omega_1,\omega_2$ whose ratio is not real:

$$
f(z+\omega_1)=f(z),\qquad f(z+\omega_2)=f(z).
$$

“Not real” means the two shifts point in different plane directions. Their integer combinations form a **period lattice**. One parallelogram generated by the shifts contains a repeating pattern. The word elliptic does not mean that the graph of the function is an ellipse. The name comes from the integrals that originally occurred in ellipse-length calculations.

A nonconstant elliptic function must have poles. Otherwise it would be continuous and bounded on a closed period parallelogram, and periodicity would give the same bound everywhere. Liouville's theorem would then make it constant. On a parallelogram with no pole on the boundary, opposite-edge integrals cancel, so the sum of its enclosed residues is zero. This is a useful link between double periodicity and the residue theorem, not a claim that each individual residue vanishes.

## Relation to elliptic integrals

For a real **modulus** $0\le k<1$, define the incomplete elliptic integral of the first kind

$$
F(\phi,k)=\int_0^\phi\frac{d\theta}{\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2\theta}}.
$$

For real $\phi$, choose the positive real square root. The integrand is positive, so $F$ is strictly increasing and has a real inverse. When $k=0$, this integral is just $\phi$. For general $k$, it is a new function rather than an elementary combination of logarithms and trigonometric functions. Complex extension requires the branch and continuation methods already introduced; we first define the functions on the real line.

The **complete** integral is $K(k)=F(\pi/2,k)$. Put $k'=\sqrt{1-k^2}$ and $K'=K(k')$ when $0<k<1$. The prime on $K'$ means a complementary modulus, not differentiation. Many software systems instead use the **parameter** $m=k^2$. Always identify which convention a formula or numerical routine uses. Here every displayed Jacobi function uses modulus $k$, as in [NIST DLMF §19.2](https://dlmf.nist.gov/19.2).

## Jacobi functions from the inverse

Let $u=F(\phi,k)$ and write $\phi=\operatorname{am}(u,k)$, the **amplitude**. Define

$$
\operatorname{sn}(u,k)=\sin\phi,\qquad
\operatorname{cn}(u,k)=\cos\phi,\qquad
\operatorname{dn}(u,k)=\sqrt{1-k^2\sin^2\phi}.
$$

The last square root is positive for real $u$ and $k<1$. The identities

$$
\operatorname{sn}^2+\operatorname{cn}^2=1,\qquad
k^2\operatorname{sn}^2+\operatorname{dn}^2=1
$$

follow immediately. Differentiating $u=F(\phi,k)$ gives $d\phi/du=\operatorname{dn}(u,k)$. The chain rule then gives

$$
\operatorname{sn}'=\operatorname{cn}\operatorname{dn},\qquad
\operatorname{cn}'=-\operatorname{sn}\operatorname{dn},\qquad
\operatorname{dn}'=-k^2\operatorname{sn}\operatorname{cn}.
$$

These derivations also explain the [derivative formulas in NIST DLMF §22.13](https://dlmf.nist.gov/22.13). The prime here does mean differentiation with respect to the first argument. At $u=0$ the three values are $0,1,1$. At $u=K$ they are $1,0,k'$.

## Worked example: a nonlinear differential equation

Suppose a real function $y(u)$ initially satisfies $y(0)=0$, $y'(0)=1$, and

$$
(y')^2=(1-y^2)(1-k^2y^2).
$$

Near zero, choose the positive derivative, separate variables, and integrate:

$$
u=\int_0^y\frac{dt}{\sqrt{(1-t^2)(1-k^2t^2)}}.
$$

Set $t=\sin\theta$ while $-1<y<1$ on the initial increasing branch. The integral becomes $F(\arcsin y,k)$, so inversion gives $y=\operatorname{sn}(u,k)$. The identity and derivative formulas check the differential equation directly. Beyond the first turning point, the sign of $y'$ changes; keeping the positive square root forever would give the wrong continuation. The squared first-order equation alone does not specify that sign at all later points.

At $k=0$, the equation becomes $(y')^2=1-y^2$, and the corresponding Jacobi function reduces to $\sin u$. The second-order equation, obtained by differentiating and continued through turning points, is $y''=-(1+k^2)y+2k^2y^3$. It displays the added nonlinear cubic term without requiring a prior course in differential equations.

## Complex periods and the twelve functions

For $0<k<1$, analytic continuation makes $\operatorname{sn}$, $\operatorname{cn}$, and $\operatorname{dn}$ meromorphic in complex $u$. For $\operatorname{sn}$, a pair of fundamental periods is $4K$ and $2iK'$. Other Jacobi functions have different period pairs; do not give them all the same pair by assumption. The remaining functions are ratios and reciprocals, such as $\operatorname{ns}=1/\operatorname{sn}$, $\operatorname{sc}=\operatorname{sn}/\operatorname{cn}$, and $\operatorname{cd}=\operatorname{cn}/\operatorname{dn}$. Their poles occur where a denominator vanishes, with cancellations checked when necessary. The full period and pole tables are in [NIST DLMF §22.4](https://dlmf.nist.gov/22.4).

At the limiting values $k=0$ and $k=1$, the two-period structure degenerates. At $k=0$, $\operatorname{sn}=\sin$, $\operatorname{cn}=\cos$, and $\operatorname{dn}=1$. At $k=1$, the corresponding limiting functions are $\tanh u$, $\operatorname{sech}u$, and $\operatorname{sech}u$. These limiting cases are tabulated in [NIST DLMF §22.5](https://dlmf.nist.gov/22.5). They should not be called nondegenerate doubly periodic elliptic functions. [[Theta and Weierstrass functions]] gives a second route to the same class of functions.

## Practice

1. For $k=3/5$, find $\operatorname{sn}(K,k)$, $\operatorname{cn}(K,k)$, and $\operatorname{dn}(K,k)$. Is $K'$ a derivative?
2. Differentiate $\operatorname{sn}^2+\operatorname{cn}^2$ using the Jacobi derivative rules.

### Worked answers

1. The amplitude at $K$ is $\pi/2$. The values are $1,0,\sqrt{1-9/25}=4/5$. Here $K'=K(4/5)$ is the complementary complete integral, not a derivative.
2. The derivative is $2\operatorname{sn}\operatorname{cn}\operatorname{dn}-2\operatorname{cn}\operatorname{sn}\operatorname{dn}=0$. Its value at zero is one, confirming that the combination stays equal to one on its real domain.
