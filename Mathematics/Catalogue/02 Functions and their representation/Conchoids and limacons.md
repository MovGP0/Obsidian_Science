> [!TLDR]
> A conchoid adds a fixed directed distance along rays; a line gives Nicomedes’ curve and a circle gives a limaçon.

## Prerequisite route

Use [[03.05 Trigonometric functions]] and [[03.03 Polynomial and rational functions]]. Polar coordinates describe a point by $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$. Angles are in radians. A negative signed radius is allowed when a curve formula requires it: $(r,\theta)$ and $(-r,\theta+\pi)$ mark the same point. The nonnegative distance to the origin is always $|r|$.

## Fourth-order curves and their equations

A fourth-order algebraic curve, or **quartic**, has a polynomial equation whose largest total term degree is four. For example, $(x^2+y^2)^2$ has degree four. The name refers to the equation, not to the number of visible loops or the number of parameters. The curves below illustrate geometric constructions that lead to quartics. The other important family in this catalogue is taught in [[Cassini ovals and the lemniscate]]. An arbitrary conchoid of an arbitrary base curve is not necessarily quartic or even algebraic.

## General conchoid

Choose a base curve $C$, a point $O$ called the **pole**, and a fixed distance $b>0$. A ray-line through $O$ meets the base at $Q$. Mark two points on that line at signed distances $+b$ and $-b$ from $Q$. As $Q$ moves, these points trace the conchoid. If the base has polar equation $r=f(\theta)$ about $O$, the two branches have signed-radius equations

$$
r=f(\theta)+b,\qquad r=f(\theta)-b.
$$

If the base meets one ray several times, apply the construction to each intersection. If a point moves through the pole, a signed radius can reverse direction. This explains why simply discarding negative radii can delete a loop.

For a parametric base $Q(t)=(u(t),v(t))\ne(0,0)$, the same construction is

$$
P_\pm(t)=\left(1\pm\frac b{\sqrt{u(t)^2+v(t)^2}}\right)(u(t),v(t)).
$$

The vector from $Q$ to $P_\pm$ has length $b$. If the base passes through the pole, this formula is undefined there and limits must be considered separately. This is the standard general meaning of conchoid; [Sendra and Sendra's algebraic study](https://arxiv.org/abs/0705.4590) gives the construction for algebraic base curves.

## Conchoid of Nicomedes

Take the base line $x=a$ with $a>0$. Along a ray with $\cos\theta\ne0$, its signed radius is $a/\cos\theta$. Therefore

$$
r=\frac a{\cos\theta}\pm b,\qquad
x=a\pm b\cos\theta,\quad
y=a\tan\theta\pm b\sin\theta.
$$

Taking $-\pi/2<\theta<\pi/2$ and both signs traces the points obtained from the rightward rays to the base line. For a point $(x,y)$, the relation $r\cos\theta=x$ gives $r(x-a)=\pm bx$. Squaring gives

$$
(x-a)^2(x^2+y^2)=b^2x^2.
$$

This is quartic. The line $x=a$ is a vertical asymptote because $\theta$ near $\pm\pi/2$ makes $|y|$ large while $x$ approaches $a$. When $b>a$, one construction branch reaches across the pole and makes a loop. When $b=a$, the loop contracts into a cusp. When $b<a$, the origin is an isolated solution of the squared polynomial, not a point generated by the ray construction: do not automatically interpret every solution added by squaring as part of the traced curve. See [MacTutor's Nicomedes conchoid](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Curves/Conchoid/) with its parameters relabelled.

## Pascal’s limaçon

Take a circle through the pole with centre $(A/2,0)$ and radius $A/2$, where $A>0$. Its polar equation is $r=A\cos\theta$. Adding a constant signed distance gives the **limaçon of Pascal**:

$$
r=B+A\cos\theta,\quad
x=(B+A\cos\theta)\cos\theta,\quad
y=(B+A\cos\theta)\sin\theta,
$$

where $B>0$ and $0\leq\theta\leq2\pi$. One full angular interval traces the standard closed curve, including any negative-radius part. Its Cartesian polynomial is

$$
(x^2+y^2-Ax)^2=B^2(x^2+y^2).
$$

To obtain it, write $r^2-Ax=Br$ and square. The origin must again be checked against the parametric construction, since the polynomial includes it even if $B>A$ and the radius never vanishes.

If $0<B<A$, the radius is negative near $\theta=\pi$ and the curve has an inner loop. If $B=A$, the inner loop becomes a cusp. If $A<B<2A$, the curve is a dimpled single oval. If $B\geq2A$, the dimple disappears and the oval is convex. The distinction at $2A$ concerns curvature, whereas the loop threshold $B=A$ can be read directly from the sign of the radius. The normalization here matches [MacTutor's limaçon](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Curves/Limacon/) after replacing its $2a$ by $A$ and its $b$ by $B$.

## Cardioid

The **cardioid** is the case $B=A$:

$$
r=A(1+\cos\theta).
$$

It is symmetric about the horizontal axis, has its farthest point $(2A,0)$ at $\theta=0$, and reaches the pole at $\theta=\pi$. At that point the radius is zero and a cusp forms. The equality $r\geq0$ holds for the whole parameter interval, so this case needs no negative-radius segment. A cardioid is also an epicycloid of equal rolling and fixed radii; see [[Epicycloids and hypocycloids]]. A rotation or reflection can put its cusp on another axis without changing its type.

## Worked example: find a loop point

Take $A=2$, $B=1$. At $\theta=\pi$, $r=1-2=-1$. Therefore $x=(-1)(-1)=1$ and $y=0$. The point lies to the right of the origin even though the angle points to the left. This is the inner-loop tip. At $\theta=0$, $r=3$ gives the outer tip $(3,0)$. The two crossings through the pole satisfy $1+2\cos\theta=0$, so $\theta=2\pi/3$ and $4\pi/3$. The negative-radius interval lies between those crossings.

## Practice

1. For a Nicomedes conchoid with base $x=3$ and distance $b=1$, find both construction points on the ray $\theta=\pi/3$.
2. For the cardioid $r=2(1+\cos\theta)$, find the points at $\theta=0,\pi/2,\pi$ and check the middle point in its Cartesian quartic.

## Worked answers

1. The base radius is $3/(1/2)=6$. Thus $r=7$ or $5$. Multiplication by $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)=(1/2,\sqrt3/2)$ gives $(7/2,7\sqrt3/2)$ and $(5/2,5\sqrt3/2)$. Each is one unit from the base point $(3,3\sqrt3)$ along the same line.
2. The radii are $4,2,0$, giving $(4,0),(0,2),(0,0)$. At $(0,2)$ the quartic is $(0+4-2\cdot0)^2=2^2(0+4)$, or $16=16$. The point $(0,0)$ is actually traced here, unlike the extra isolated origin in some non-cardioid parameter choices.
