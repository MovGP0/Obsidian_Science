> [!TLDR]
> A smooth map sends tangent vectors forward by its derivative and sends covectors backward by composition with that derivative.

## Smoothness in charts

Let $F:M\to N$ be a map between smooth manifolds of dimensions $m$ and $n$. It is smooth if it is continuous and, for charts $x$ near $p$ and $y$ near $F(p)$, the coordinate expression

$$
\widehat F=y\circ F\circ x^{-1}
$$

is smooth wherever defined. It is enough to check charts from the specified atlases: compatibility and the chain rule then give the result in every compatible chart. Coordinate expressions are maps between Euclidean open sets, so their derivatives are ordinary Jacobian matrices.

The derivative at $p$ is the linear map

$$
dF_p:T_pM\longrightarrow T_{F(p)}N.
$$

Given a tangent vector represented by a curve $\gamma$, define $dF_p(v)$ using the curve $F\circ\gamma$. Equivalently, for a smooth function $g$ near $F(p)$,

$$
(dF_pv)[g]=v[g\circ F].
$$

This definition makes independence of charts explicit. In coordinates it becomes $(dF_pv)^a=\sum_i(\partial\widehat F^a/\partial x^i)v^i$. The same map is often called the pushforward and written $F_{*p}$.

## Covectors go in the opposite direction

For $\alpha\in T_{F(p)}^*N$, define its pullback by

$$
(dF_p)^*\alpha=\alpha\circ dF_p\in T_p^*M.
$$

Thus $((dF_p)^*\alpha)(v)=\alpha(dF_pv)$. If covector coefficients are stored as columns, the pullback matrix is the transpose Jacobian $J^T$. This does not require $J$ to be square or invertible. In particular,

$$
d(g\circ F)_p=(dF_p)^*(dg_{F(p)}).
$$

This identity is the chain rule stated without coordinates. A general map provides no canonical pushforward of covectors and no canonical pullback of tangent vectors. A diffeomorphism supplies those additional operations by use of its inverse.

## Worked example with a noninvertible derivative

Take $F:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^2$ defined by $F(x,y)=(u,w)=(x^2-y^2,2xy)$. At $p=(1,2)$, its value and Jacobian are

$$
F(p)=(-3,4),\qquad
J_p=\begin{pmatrix}2&-4\\4&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

For $v=3\partial_x-\partial_y$, multiplication gives $dF_p(v)=10\partial_u+10\partial_w$. Let $\alpha=2du-dw$ at $F(p)$. Its pullback is

$$
(dF_p)^*\alpha
=2(2dx-4dy)-(4dx+2dy)=-10dy.
$$

The pairing check gives $\alpha(dF_pv)=2(10)-10=10$, while $(-10dy)(v)=10$. At the origin, however, $J_0=0$. Every vector is sent to zero there. The map remains smooth; smoothness does not require an invertible derivative. Away from the origin, $\det J=4(x^2+y^2)>0$, so the inverse function theorem gives local inverses. It does not give one global inverse, since $F(x,y)=F(-x,-y)$.

## Composition and limits of pushforward fields

For a second smooth map $G:N\to P$, curves or coordinate differentiation give

$$
d(G\circ F)_p=dG_{F(p)}\circ dF_p.
$$

Pullbacks reverse the order of composition because covectors compose on the left. Although each vector pushes forward, a whole vector field on $M$ need not define a field on $N$: two different points mapping to the same target can give different pushed vectors. For a diffeomorphism this ambiguity disappears. See [[19.08 Vector and tensor fields]] for the distinction between a vector at one point and a smooth assignment.

## Practice

1. For $H(t)=(t,t^2)$, compute $dH_2(3\partial_t)$ and pull back $\alpha=4dx+dy$ from $H(2)$.
2. Let $K(x,y)=x+y$ and $H$ be as above. Verify the derivative chain rule at $t=2$.

## Worked answers

1. The derivative column is $(1,2t)^T$, so at $2$, $dH_2(3\partial_t)=3\partial_x+12\partial_y$. The pullback is $(4+2t)dt=8dt$ at $2$. Both pairings equal $24$: $4(3)+12=8(3)$.
2. The composition is $K(H(t))=t+t^2$, with derivative $1+2t=5$ at $2$. Matrix multiplication gives $(1,1)(1,4)^T=5$. Applied to $3\partial_t$, both methods give $15\partial_s$ in the one-dimensional target coordinate $s$.
