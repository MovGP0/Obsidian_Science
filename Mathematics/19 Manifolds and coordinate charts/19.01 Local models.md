title: "Local models"

> [!TLDR]
> A manifold has open neighbourhoods that are homeomorphic to open subsets of a fixed Euclidean space.

## What a local model means

A topological space $M$ is an $n$-dimensional topological manifold without boundary if it is Hausdorff, has a countable basis, and is locally Euclidean of dimension $n$. Here $n$ is a nonnegative integer. Hausdorff means that two distinct points have disjoint open neighbourhoods. A countable basis is a countable collection of open sets from which every open set is a union. These two requirements exclude spaces with poor separation or size properties. They are part of the definition, not consequences of the local coordinate formula.

Locally Euclidean means that for every $p\in M$, there is an open set $U\subset M$ containing $p$ and a homeomorphism

$$
\varphi:U\longrightarrow V,\qquad V\subset\mathbb R^n\text{ open}.
$$

A homeomorphism is a continuous bijection with continuous inverse. Thus a chart preserves the topology, including which sequences converge and which subsets are open. It does not have to preserve distances, angles, or area. A neighbourhood is open in $M$, even if $M$ sits in a larger ambient space. An arc of a circle is open in the circle when it is the intersection of that circle with an open subset of the plane.

The integer $n$ counts independent local coordinates. A circle in $\mathbb R^2$ has dimension one. A sphere in $\mathbb R^3$ has dimension two. The number of ambient coordinates is not the manifold dimension. The theorem called invariance of dimension ensures that nonempty open subsets of $\mathbb R^m$ and $\mathbb R^n$ cannot be homeomorphic when $m\ne n$. We use that theorem here without proving it; it makes the local dimension well defined.

## Worked example: the circle near its top point

Let

$$
S^1=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb R^2:x^2+y^2=1\}.
$$

Take $U=\{(x,y)\in S^1:y>0\}$. This upper semicircle excludes both endpoints and is open in $S^1$. Define $\varphi(x,y)=x$. Its image is $(-1,1)$, an open subset of $\mathbb R$. The inverse is

$$
\varphi^{-1}(t)=(t,\sqrt{1-t^2}),\qquad -1<t<1.
$$

The positive square root is forced by $y>0$. Substitution gives $t^2+(\sqrt{1-t^2})^2=1$, so the inverse lands in $U$. Both directions are continuous. This proves that the circle looks like an interval near $(0,1)$. At $(1,0)$ the same chart does not apply, but the right semicircle has the chart $(x,y)\mapsto y$. A failed formula at one point does not invalidate a different local description.

## Boundaries and failures of local structure

The interval $[0,1]$ is not a one-dimensional manifold *without* boundary. Near $0$, its neighbourhood resembles $[0,\varepsilon)$, not an open interval. Manifolds with boundary use open subsets of the half-space $\{x_n\geq0\}$ as additional models. Always state which convention is in force.

Two crossing lines give a different failure. Let $X=\{(x,y):xy=0\}$. A sufficiently small neighbourhood of the origin has four branches after the origin is removed. Removing a point from an interval leaves two local branches. Therefore the origin has no interval chart. Away from the crossing, each branch is locally a line. Local Euclidean structure must hold at every point, not merely most points. See [[19.09 Local and global statements]] for a comparison with coordinate failures.

## Practice

1. Show that the parabola $P=\{(x,x^2):x\in\mathbb R\}$ is a one-dimensional manifold. Specify a chart and its inverse.
2. Explain why $S^1\setminus\{(1,0)\}$ can be a single chart domain, but the whole circle cannot be homeomorphic to an open interval.

## Worked answers

1. Projection $\varphi(x,x^2)=x$ is a continuous bijection from $P$ to $\mathbb R$. Its inverse $t\mapsto(t,t^2)$ is continuous. Thus one chart covers $P$. The subspace is Hausdorff and has a countable basis because it is a subspace of Euclidean space. Its dimension is one, although its points have two ambient coordinates.
2. The parametrization $\theta\mapsto(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ for $0<\theta<2\pi$ has a continuous inverse on the punctured circle, so it provides the chart. The whole circle is compact. Its homeomorphic image would be compact, but a nonempty open interval is not compact. Compactness is preserved by homeomorphisms, which gives the obstruction.
