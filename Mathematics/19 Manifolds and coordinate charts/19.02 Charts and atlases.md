> [!TLDR]
> A chart gives coordinates on one open region; an atlas gives enough charts to cover the manifold.

## Chart data and coordinate functions

Let $M$ be an $n$-dimensional manifold. A chart is a pair $(U,\varphi)$, where $U$ is open in $M$ and $\varphi:U\to\varphi(U)\subset\mathbb R^n$ is a homeomorphism onto an open image. Write

$$
\varphi(p)=(x^1(p),\ldots,x^n(p)).
$$

The functions $x^i:U\to\mathbb R$ are coordinate functions. Their superscripts are labels, not powers. The point $p$ is an element of $M$; its coordinate tuple is an element of $\mathbb R^n$. Confusing those two objects can hide domain errors.

An atlas is a collection $\{(U_\alpha,\varphi_\alpha)\}$ with $\bigcup_\alpha U_\alpha=M$. If $U_\alpha\cap U_\beta$ is nonempty, a point there has two coordinate tuples. The coordinate descriptions must be related by a transition map, studied in [[19.03 Transition maps]]. A smooth atlas requires those transitions to be smooth. Covering a space by parametrized pieces is insufficient if the maps are not one-to-one or their inverses are not continuous.

## Worked example: two charts for a circle

On $S^1=\{(x,y):x^2+y^2=1\}$, remove the north point $N=(0,1)$ and define

$$
u=\varphi_N(x,y)=\frac{x}{1-y}.
$$

The line through $N$ and $(x,y)$ meets the horizontal axis at $(u,0)$. To find the inverse algebraically, put $x=u(1-y)$ into $x^2+y^2=1$. The non-north solution is

$$
x=\frac{2u}{1+u^2},\qquad y=\frac{u^2-1}{1+u^2}.
$$

Every real $u$ gives a point different from $N$, and substitution verifies the circle equation. Both functions are continuous, with denominator strictly positive. Thus this is a chart onto all of $\mathbb R$.

Remove the south point $S=(0,-1)$ for the second chart:

$$
v=\varphi_S(x,y)=\frac{x}{1+y},\qquad
\varphi_S^{-1}(v)=\left(\frac{2v}{1+v^2},\frac{1-v^2}{1+v^2}\right).
$$

The domains cover $S^1$. Their overlap excludes both poles. On it $u\ne0$ and $v=1/u$. One coordinate line therefore represents two separate overlap intervals, $u<0$ and $u>0$.

The drawing uses $P=(0.8,-0.6)$, so $u=0.8/1.6=0.5$. The straight line is a construction in the plane, not a path that the coordinate map must follow inside the circle.

## Why familiar coordinates fail

An angle on the circle needs a cut. The map $\theta\mapsto(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ is periodic, so it is not one-to-one on $\mathbb R$. Restricting to $(-\pi,\pi)$ removes the point $(-1,0)$ and gives a chart. Assigning one angle at the cut does not fix continuity: angles approaching that point from opposite directions approach opposite endpoints.

On a sphere, longitude fails at both poles because many longitude values name the same point. Latitude and longitude also need a longitude cut. These defects belong to the chosen coordinate system. A graph chart near a pole, such as $(x,y)\mapsto(x,y,\sqrt{1-x^2-y^2})$, gives valid coordinates there. An atlas supplies alternative formulas where one formula fails.

## Practice

1. Find the north and south stereographic coordinates of $P=(3/5,4/5)$. Check their overlap relation.
2. Explain why $\theta\in[0,2\pi)$ is not a chart for the entire circle, although each point is represented once.

## Worked answers

1. The north coordinate is $u=(3/5)/(1/5)=3$. The south coordinate is $v=(3/5)/(9/5)=1/3$. Both are defined because $P$ is neither pole. Their product is one, which agrees with $v=1/u$. The inverse north formula gives $(6/10,8/10)=P$.
2. A chart image must be open in $\mathbb R$, while $[0,2\pi)$ is not open. More directly, points with angle close to $2\pi$ approach $(1,0)$, whose assigned coordinate is $0$. The inverse angle assignment is discontinuous at that point. Uniqueness of a label alone does not give a homeomorphism.
