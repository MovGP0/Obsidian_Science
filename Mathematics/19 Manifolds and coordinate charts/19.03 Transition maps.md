title: "Transition maps"

> [!TLDR]
> Transition maps express the same point in different coordinates, and their differentiability defines a smooth atlas.

## Domains and direction

Suppose $(U,\varphi)$ and $(V,\psi)$ are charts of an $n$-dimensional manifold $M$. On a nonempty overlap the transition from $x=\varphi(p)$ to $y=\psi(p)$ is

$$
T=\psi\circ\varphi^{-1}:\varphi(U\cap V)\longrightarrow\psi(U\cap V).
$$

Both domain and image are open subsets of $\mathbb R^n$. The inverse map first converts coordinates back to a point; the second chart then gives new coordinates. Reversing the composition changes its meaning or makes it undefined. The inverse transition is $\varphi\circ\psi^{-1}$.

A $C^k$ atlas requires all transitions to have continuous partial derivatives through order $k$, for an integer $k\geq1$. A smooth atlas requires $C^\infty$ transitions. Since the requirement includes both chart orders, each transition is a diffeomorphism. Its Jacobian matrix is invertible: differentiating $T^{-1}\circ T=\operatorname{id}$ gives

$$
D(T^{-1})_{T(x)}\,DT_x=I.
$$

Continuous coordinate changes are enough for a topological atlas, but not for differentiation. Analytic atlases require a stronger condition: transitions must be locally represented by convergent power series.

## Worked example: stereographic overlap

Use the circle coordinates from [[19.02 Charts and atlases]],

$$
u=\frac{x}{1-y},\qquad v=\frac{x}{1+y}.
$$

On the overlap, $x\ne0$, and the circle equation gives $x^2=(1-y)(1+y)$. Therefore

$$
uv=\frac{x^2}{(1-y)(1+y)}=1,\qquad v=T(u)=\frac1u.
$$

The domain is $\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}$, not all of $\mathbb R$. Every derivative of $1/u$ exists and is continuous separately on its two components. Its first derivative is $-1/u^2$, which never vanishes there. The inverse is the same function, so these charts form a smooth atlas. The missing value $u=0$ represents the south point, excluded from the other chart. It is not an unexplained hole in the overlap calculation.

If a curve has coordinate velocity $du/dt=2$ at $u=2$, the chain rule gives

$$
\frac{dv}{dt}=T'(2)\frac{du}{dt}=-\frac14\cdot2=-\frac12.
$$

The components differ, but the curve and its tangent vector are the same. The negative derivative reverses coordinate direction on each overlap component.

## Compatibility and a smooth structure

Two smooth atlases define the same smooth structure when their union is also a smooth atlas. Equivalently, every chart of one atlas has smooth transitions with every overlapping chart of the other. The collection of all compatible charts is the maximal smooth atlas. A chosen small atlas is a practical description of this larger structure.

For a useful warning, give the topological line the coordinate $u=x^3$. This is a homeomorphism $\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$. The transition from the standard $x$ chart is $u=x^3$, which is smooth. Its inverse $x=u^{1/3}$ is not differentiable at zero. Thus these two charts are not smoothly compatible. Checking only one direction would produce a wrong conclusion. The single cubic chart still defines a smooth structure of its own; the issue is compatibility with the specified standard chart.

## Practice

1. On $x>0$, compare coordinates $x$ and $y=\log x$. Show smooth compatibility and compute the velocity transformation.
2. Three overlapping coordinates satisfy $y=T(x)$ and $z=S(y)$. Derive the rule for $D(z)/D(x)$ and explain the order of matrix multiplication.

## Worked answers

1. The transition $T:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ is $T(x)=\log x$. Its inverse $e^y$ is smooth, and $T'(x)=1/x\ne0$. For a curve $x(t)>0$, the chain rule yields $\dot y=\dot x/x$. At $x=2$ with $\dot x=3$, the new component is $3/2$.
2. The direct transition is $S\circ T$. Its derivative is $D(S\circ T)_x=DS_{T(x)}DT_x$. A column vector of $x$ components is first multiplied by $DT_x$, then by $DS_{T(x)}$. Matrix order records that sequence. This identity makes changes through a third chart consistent with a direct change.
