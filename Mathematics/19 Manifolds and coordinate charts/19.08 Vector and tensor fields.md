---
title: "Vector and tensor fields"
---

> [!TLDR]
> A field assigns an object to every point smoothly, while coordinate components describe that assignment in a chosen local basis.

## From one tangent vector to a field

A vector field $X$ on a smooth manifold $M$ assigns $X_p\in T_pM$ to each $p$. In a coordinate region $(x^1,\ldots,x^n)$, write

$$
X=\sum_iX^i(x)\partial_i.
$$

The field is smooth when every coefficient function $X^i$ is smooth in each chart. Smooth transition maps make this condition independent of the chart. The tangent bundle is $TM=\bigsqcup_{p\in M}T_pM$, a disjoint union that retains the base point. A vector field is a smooth section of the projection $TM\to M$: its value over $p$ must lie over that same point.

A covector field, or one-form, similarly assigns $\alpha_p\in T_p^*M$ smoothly. Locally $\alpha=\sum_i\alpha_i(x)dx^i$. The pairing $\alpha(X)=\sum_i\alpha_iX^i$ is a smooth scalar function. A smooth function $f$ produces the one-form $df$, whose coefficients are its partial derivatives. It does not produce a preferred vector field without an additional metric.

## Tensor fields and their inputs

A tensor of type $(r,s)$ at $p$ is an element of $(T_pM)^{\otimes r}\otimes(T_p^*M)^{\otimes s}$. Equivalently, it is multilinear in $r$ covector inputs and $s$ vector inputs. A tensor field assigns such a tensor smoothly to each point. For example, a covariant two-tensor has the local form

$$
B=\sum_{i,j}B_{ij}\,dx^i\otimes dx^j,
\qquad B(v,w)=\sum_{i,j}B_{ij}v^iw^j.
$$

The tensor product is defined by $(\alpha\otimes\beta)(v,w)=\alpha(v)\beta(w)$. A general two-tensor need not be symmetric. A Riemannian metric is a special smooth symmetric covariant two-tensor for which $B_p(v,v)>0$ whenever $v\ne0$.

Under coordinates $y=y(x)$, vector coefficients transform with $\partial y/\partial x$, and covector coefficients with $\partial x/\partial y$. A covariant two-tensor therefore satisfies

$$
\widetilde B_{ab}=\sum_{i,j}B_{ij}
\frac{\partial x^i}{\partial y^a}
\frac{\partial x^j}{\partial y^b}.
$$

The two inverse factors preserve its evaluation on two vectors. An arbitrary collection of matrices in unrelated charts is not a tensor field unless these overlap rules hold.

## Worked example: one field, two descriptions

On $\mathbb R^2$, define the smooth field $X=-y\partial_x+x\partial_y$. In polar coordinates $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$, valid on a region with $r>0$ and an angle cut,

$$
\partial_\theta=-r\sin\theta\,\partial_x+r\cos\theta\,\partial_y
=-y\partial_x+x\partial_y.
$$

Thus $X=\partial_\theta$ on each polar chart. The Cartesian formula also defines $X_0=0$. There is no polar chart at zero, so the coefficient $1$ of $\partial_\theta$ there has no meaning. The smooth Cartesian expression establishes that the field extends across the origin.

For a tensor example, consider the Euclidean metric $g=dx\otimes dx+dy\otimes dy$. Since

$$
dx=\cos\theta\,dr-r\sin\theta\,d\theta,
\qquad dy=\sin\theta\,dr+r\cos\theta\,d\theta,
$$

expanding both products cancels the mixed terms. Using $\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1$ gives $g=dr\otimes dr+r^2d\theta\otimes d\theta$. Hence $g(X,X)=r^2=x^2+y^2$. Different coefficient matrices describe the same tensor. Their determinants cannot be compared at a point where one coordinate system is invalid.

## Practice

1. For $X=-y\partial_x+x\partial_y$ and $f=x^2+y^2$, calculate $X[f]$. Interpret the result through circles.
2. Let $B=dx\otimes dy+dy\otimes dx$. Evaluate $B(v,v)$ for $v=\partial_x-2\partial_y$. Is $B$ a Riemannian metric?

## Worked answers

1. The derivation gives $X[f]=(-y)(2x)+x(2y)=0$. Thus the radius squared does not change along integral curves of $X$. Away from zero those curves follow circles, consistent with $X=\partial_\theta$ and a constant radial coordinate. At zero the field vanishes.
2. The two terms give $dx(v)dy(v)+dy(v)dx(v)=1(-2)+(-2)1=-4$. The tensor is symmetric and smooth, but this negative value violates positive definiteness. In fact $B(\partial_x,\partial_x)=0$ also violates the strict positivity required for nonzero vectors. Symmetry alone is insufficient for a metric of Riemannian type.
