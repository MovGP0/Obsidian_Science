> [!TLDR]
> A tangent vector is a curve velocity at a point, or equivalently a directional derivation on smooth functions there.

## Velocities without an ambient space

Let $M$ be a smooth $n$-dimensional manifold and $p\in M$. A smooth curve through $p$ is a smooth map $\gamma:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to M$ with $\gamma(0)=p$. In a chart $x=(x^1,\ldots,x^n)$ near $p$, its coordinate velocity is

$$
v^i=\left.\frac{d}{dt}x^i(\gamma(t))\right|_{t=0}.
$$

Two curves define the same tangent vector if their coordinate velocities agree in one chart. The chain rule ensures agreement in every chart: if $y=T(x)$, then

$$
\widetilde v^a=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\partial y^a}{\partial x^i}(p)v^i.
$$

The Jacobian is evaluated at $x(p)$. Thus a tangent vector is an equivalence class of curves, not a particular path. Curves can bend differently away from $p$ and still have the same first-order behaviour there. A zero tangent vector also has curve representatives, including a constant curve.

## The derivation description

A tangent vector acts on a smooth real-valued function $f$ near $p$ by

$$
v[f]=\left.\frac{d}{dt}f(\gamma(t))\right|_{0}
=\sum_i v^i\frac{\partial(f\circ x^{-1})}{\partial x^i}(x(p)).
$$

This action is linear and satisfies the Leibniz rule

$$
v[fg]=f(p)v[g]+g(p)v[f].
$$

The factors outside the derivatives are numbers evaluated at $p$. A derivation at $p$ means precisely a linear map on germs of smooth functions with this rule. A germ records a function only near $p$; two functions with the same values on some neighbourhood give the same germ. This local language prevents distant behaviour from entering a tangent vector.

Conversely, every such derivation $D$ has the displayed coordinate form. The key local identity, obtained by integrating the first derivative along a line segment in coordinate space, is

$$
f(x)-f(x_0)=\sum_i(x^i-x_0^i)h_i(x),\qquad
h_i(x_0)=\partial_i f(x_0).
$$

The functions $h_i$ are smooth on a sufficiently small ball. The Leibniz rule first gives $D[1]=0$, hence $D$ kills constants. Applying it to this identity gives $D[f]=\sum_iD[x^i]\partial_i f(x_0)$. A coordinate straight line $x(t)=x_0+t(D[x^1],\ldots,D[x^n])$ supplies a curve representative for small $t$. This proves the equivalence of velocities and derivations.

## Worked example on a circle

Let $\gamma(t)=(\cos t,\sin t)$, so $p=(1,0)$. The ambient velocity is $(0,1)$. A local angle chart $\theta$ gives component $d\theta/dt=1$. For the smooth function $f(x,y)=x^2+3y$ restricted to the circle,

$$
f(\gamma(t))=\cos^2t+3\sin t,
\qquad v[f]=-2\cos0\sin0+3\cos0=3.
$$

This agrees with the ambient calculation $\nabla f(p)\cdot(0,1)=(2,3)\cdot(0,1)=3$. The manifold definition did not require this embedding; it merely provides another way to check the result.

## Common errors and practice

The component tuple depends on coordinates. The vector is the action or curve class that those components represent. Also, a tangent vector at $p$ is not a vector field on a whole neighbourhood; see [[19.08 Vector and tensor fields]].

1. For $\gamma(t)=(t,1+t^2)$ in $\mathbb R^2$, find its tangent at $t=0$ and apply it to $f(x,y)=xy^2$.
2. In coordinates $u=x$ and $w=y+x^2$, transform a vector with $(v^x,v^y)=(2,-1)$ at $(x,y)=(1,3)$.

## Worked answers

1. The point is $(0,1)$ and the coordinate velocity is $(1,0)$. Since $\partial_xf=y^2$ and $\partial_yf=2xy$, its action is $1\cdot1+0\cdot0=1$. Direct substitution gives $f(\gamma(t))=t(1+t^2)^2$, whose derivative at zero is also one.
2. Differentiate the coordinate definitions: $v^u=v^x=2$ and $v^w=v^y+2xv^x=-1+4=3$. The new tuple is $(2,3)$ at coordinate point $(u,w)=(1,4)$. It describes the same derivation, since the chain rule changes both function derivatives and vector components together.
