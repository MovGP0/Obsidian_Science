> [!TLDR]
> Sum, product, quotient, and chain rules reduce derivatives of compound functions to derivatives of their parts.

## Hypotheses and notation

Let $u$ and $v$ be real functions differentiable at an interior point $x$, and let $c$ be constant with respect to $x$. The basic rules are

$$
(u+v)'=u'+v',\qquad (cu)'=cu',\qquad
(uv)'=u'v+uv'.
$$

All functions on the right are evaluated at the same input $x$. The sum rule follows by splitting the difference quotient into two quotients and applying the limit law. A constant factor can be taken outside the limit. Subtraction uses the same rule with $c=-1$.

The product rule has two terms because either factor can change. Add and subtract $u(x+h)v(x)$ in its difference quotient:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{u(x+h)v(x+h)-u(x)v(x)}h
={}&u(x+h)\frac{v(x+h)-v(x)}h\\
&+v(x)\frac{u(x+h)-u(x)}h.
\end{aligned}
$$

Differentiability implies continuity, so $u(x+h)\to u(x)$. Taking limits gives $uv'+vu'$. Merely multiplying $u'$ and $v'$ gives the wrong result. [[07.03 The derivative]] provides the limit definition used here.

## Quotients and compositions

When $v(x)\ne0$, the quotient rule is

$$
\left(\frac uv\right)'=\frac{u'v-uv'}{v^2}.
$$

One can establish differentiability of $q=u/v$ directly from its difference quotient. The nonzero denominator and continuity of $v$ keep that denominator away from zero locally. For the formula, write $u=qv$. The product rule gives $u'=q'v+qv'$. Solving for $q'$ and substituting $q=u/v$ yields the stated result. The rule does not assign derivatives at zeros of $v$.

For a composition $F(x)=f(g(x))$, assume $g$ is differentiable at $x$ and $f$ at $g(x)$. The chain rule is

$$
F'(x)=f'(g(x))g'(x).
$$

The outer derivative is evaluated at the inner output. A useful explanation uses first-order increments. Let $k=g(x+h)-g(x)=g'(x)h+o(h)$. Then

$$
f(g(x)+k)-f(g(x))=f'(g(x))k+o(k).
$$

Here $k=O(h)$ because $k/h$ has a finite limit. Thus the last remainder is also $o(h)$, and the coefficient of $h$ is $f'(g(x))g'(x)$. This reasoning covers $g'(x)=0$ too; no division by an inner increment that might be zero is needed.

## Worked example: choose rules from structure

Differentiate

$$
F(x)=\frac{(x^2+1)^3}{x-2},\qquad x\ne2.
$$

First identify a quotient with $u=(x^2+1)^3$ and $v=x-2$. The numerator is a composition: a cube applied to $x^2+1$. Using [[07.05 Elementary derivatives]],

$$
u'=3(x^2+1)^2(2x)=6x(x^2+1)^2,\qquad v'=1.
$$

Apply the quotient rule before simplifying:

$$
\begin{aligned}
F'(x)
&=\frac{6x(x^2+1)^2(x-2)-(x^2+1)^3}{(x-2)^2}\\
&=\frac{(x^2+1)^2[6x(x-2)-(x^2+1)]}{(x-2)^2}\\
&=\frac{(x^2+1)^2(5x^2-12x-1)}{(x-2)^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Factoring exposes a common factor and reduces the algebra. The domain stays $x\ne2$. Simplification must not silently restore an input excluded by the original function.

For comparison, the product $x^2e^{3x}$ has derivative $2xe^{3x}+3x^2e^{3x}$. The first term differentiates $x^2$. The second differentiates $e^{3x}$ and includes the inner derivative $3$.

## Common errors

Keep the order $u'v-uv'$ in the quotient numerator. Reversing it changes the sign. Square the denominator, not the numerator. For nested compositions, write each stage. The function $\sin((x^2+1)^3)$ has three layers, so its derivative contains the factors $\cos((x^2+1)^3)$, $3(x^2+1)^2$, and $2x$.

## Practice

1. Differentiate $y=(x^2+1)\sin x$.
2. Differentiate $y=\ln(1+x^2)$ and state its real domain.

## Worked answers

1. The product rule gives

$$
y'=(2x)\sin x+(x^2+1)\cos x.
$$

Neither term can be omitted. All factors and derivatives exist for every real $x$.

2. The inner function is $g(x)=1+x^2$, with derivative $2x$. The outer derivative of $\ln t$ is $1/t$ for $t>0$. Hence

$$
y'=\frac1{1+x^2}\,2x=\frac{2x}{1+x^2}.
$$

Since $1+x^2>0$ for every real $x$, the original function and its derivative both have domain $\mathbb R$.
