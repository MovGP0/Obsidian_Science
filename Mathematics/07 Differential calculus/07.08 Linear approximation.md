> [!TLDR]
> A tangent line estimates a function near its base point; an error bound states how far that estimate can be trusted.

## The first-order model

Let $f$ be differentiable at an interior point $a$. Write $h=x-a$ for an input increment. The derivative definition gives

$$
f(a+h)=f(a)+f'(a)h+r(h),\qquad
\lim_{h\to0}\frac{r(h)}h=0.
$$

The remainder $r(h)=o(h)$ is smaller than first order as $h\to0$. Omitting it gives the linear approximation

$$
L(x)=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a).
$$

This is the tangent line in [[07.03 The derivative]]. Differentiability states what happens as the increment shrinks, but does not supply a numerical radius of accuracy. A small input change must be judged relative to the function and its base point.

The symbol $dx$ denotes a chosen input increment. The differential $df$ at $a$ is the linear expression $df=f'(a)\,dx$. With $dx=h$, it estimates the actual output change $\Delta f=f(a+h)-f(a)$:

$$
\Delta f=df+r(h).
$$

Thus a differential is generally not an exact finite output change. It is the value of the first-order linear map on an increment. All variables in this article are abstract real numbers.

## Worked example: estimate a square root

Estimate $\sqrt{4.1}$ using $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$ at $a=4$. The base values are $f(4)=2$ and $f'(4)=1/(2\sqrt4)=1/4$. For $h=0.1$,

$$
L(4.1)=2+\frac14(0.1)=2.025.
$$

The predicted change is $df=0.025$. The true value is approximately $2.02484567$, so the tangent overestimates by about $0.00015433$. This error at one input is small, but an observation at one input is not a general bound.

## An error bound and a useful interval

Assume now that $f$ is twice continuously differentiable on an interval containing $a$ and $x$. For $x\ne a$, Taylor's theorem gives a point $\xi$ strictly between $a$ and $x$ such that

$$
f(x)-L(x)=\frac{f''(\xi)}2(x-a)^2.
$$

At $x=a$, the error is exactly zero. If $|f''(t)|\le M$ throughout that interval, then

$$
|f(x)-L(x)|\le\frac M2|x-a|^2.
$$

This quadratic bound requires stronger hypotheses than differentiability alone. For the square root,

$$
f''(x)=-\frac1{4x^{3/2}}\qquad(x>0).
$$

On $[4,4.1]$, its largest absolute value is $1/32$, at the left endpoint. Therefore

$$
|\sqrt{4.1}-2.025|\le\frac1{64}(0.1)^2=0.00015625.
$$

The negative second derivative also proves that the tangent lies above the curve. The error's sign and its numerical estimate agree with this conclusion.

To choose an interval in advance, start with $[3.5,4.5]$. There $|f''|\le M=1/(4\cdot3.5^{3/2})$. For a target absolute error of $0.001$, it is sufficient that

$$
|h|\le\sqrt{\frac{2(0.001)}M}\approx0.229.
$$

This radius lies within the interval used to obtain $M$. Using the rounded-down radius, every input with $|x-4|\le0.228$ has the stated guaranteed accuracy. The condition is sufficient and need not be the largest possible interval.

## Limitations

Near a singularity or domain boundary, a tangent estimate can fail quickly. For $1/x$ near zero, the second derivative grows rapidly. Relative error, $|f-L|/|f|$, also becomes unsuitable when the true output is zero or very small. State whether a target error is absolute or relative. Do not assume a quadratic remainder from the existence of just one derivative; only the $o(h)$ property follows in that general setting.

## Practice

1. Estimate $\ln(1.02)$ from $a=1$, and bound its absolute error on $[1,1.02]$.
2. For $f(x)=x^2$ at $a=3$, calculate $df$, $\Delta f$, and their difference for an increment $h$. Find the increments that give absolute error at most $0.01$.

## Worked answers

1. Since $\ln1=0$ and $f'(1)=1$, the estimate is $0.02$. Also $f''(x)=-1/x^2$, so $|f''|\le1$ on the interval. The error is at most $(1/2)(0.02)^2=0.0002$. Since $f''<0$, the tangent estimate is an overestimate.

2. We have $f'(3)=6$, hence $df=6h$. Exact expansion gives $\Delta f=(3+h)^2-9=6h+h^2$. Thus $\Delta f-df=h^2$ exactly. The requirement $h^2\le0.01$ is equivalent to $|h|\le0.1$. This directly shows the distinction between a differential and the actual finite change.
