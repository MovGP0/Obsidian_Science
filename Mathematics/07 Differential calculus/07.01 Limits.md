> [!TLDR]
> A limit describes function values when the input approaches a point or becomes arbitrarily large.

## What approaches what?

Let $f:D\to\mathbb R$ be a real function. In the expression $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L$, the input $x$ approaches $a$ through points of the domain $D$, and the outputs approach $L$. Assume $a$ is an accumulation point of $D$: every interval about $a$ contains a domain point other than $a$. The value $f(a)$ can differ from $L$, or it can be undefined. A limit concerns nearby inputs with $x\ne a$.

For a finite limit, the precise definition is: for every output tolerance $\varepsilon>0$, there is an input tolerance $\delta>0$ such that

$$
x\in D,\quad 0<|x-a|<\delta
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad |f(x)-L|<\varepsilon.
$$

The word “every” matters. A finite table can suggest a limit, but it cannot prove this condition. The tolerance $\delta$ can depend on $\varepsilon$ and on the function. It must work for all eligible inputs in the interval.

For example, $f(x)=3x+1$ has limit $7$ at $a=2$, because $|f(x)-7|=3|x-2|$. Given any $\varepsilon>0$, choose $\delta=\varepsilon/3$. Then $0<|x-2|<\delta$ gives $|f(x)-7|<\varepsilon$. This is a proof for every tolerance, rather than a test of selected inputs.

## Left and right limits

The notation $x\to a^-$ restricts inputs to $x<a$, while $x\to a^+$ restricts them to $x>a$. If the domain approaches $a$ from both sides, a two-sided limit is $L$ exactly when both one-sided limits exist and equal $L$.

For $f(x)=|x|/x$, defined for $x\ne0$, the right-hand values are $1$ and the left-hand values are $-1$. Hence

$$
\lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{|x|}{x}=1,\qquad
\lim_{x\to0^-}\frac{|x|}{x}=-1.
$$

The two-sided limit does not exist. One cannot average the answers. At a domain endpoint, such as $0$ for $\sqrt{x}$, the limit through the domain uses only the available side.

## Infinite behaviour

The statement $f(x)\to+\infty$ as $x\to a$ means that, for every threshold $M>0$, all sufficiently close eligible inputs give $f(x)>M$. Infinity is not a real output value. For example, $1/x^2\to+\infty$ at zero: $0<|x|<1/\sqrt M$ implies $1/x^2>M$. In contrast, $1/x$ tends to $+\infty$ from the right and $-\infty$ from the left.

Assume the domain $D$ is unbounded above. The statement $\lim_{x\to+\infty}f(x)=L$ means that for each $\varepsilon>0$, there is $R$ such that $x>R$ in the domain implies $|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon$. Here the input, rather than the output, is unbounded. These are different uses of the infinity symbol.

## Worked example: remove an algebraic obstruction

Find $\lim_{x\to2}(x^2-4)/(x-2)$. Direct substitution gives $0/0$, which is undefined and does not decide the limit. Factor and cancel:

$$
\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}
=\frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2}=x+2\qquad(x\ne2).
$$

Cancellation is valid on the punctured neighbourhood used in the limit. Thus the limit is $4$, although the original expression has no value at $2$. The sum and product limit laws justify substitution into the resulting polynomial. A quotient limit law requires a nonzero limiting denominator.

For an example at infinity, divide by the highest power of $x$:

$$
\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{2x^2-3}{x^2+1}
=\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{2-3/x^2}{1+1/x^2}=2.
$$

Since $1/x^2\to0$, the new numerator tends to $2$ and the new denominator to $1$. The quotient law applies. These ideas support [[07.02 Continuity]] and [[07.03 The derivative]].

## Practice

1. Find $\lim_{x\to0}(\sqrt{1+x}-1)/x$ without differentiation.
2. For $g(x)=1/(x-3)$, find both one-sided limits at $3$ and the limit as $x\to+\infty$.

## Worked answers

1. Multiply by the conjugate, keeping $x\ne0$:

$$
\frac{\sqrt{1+x}-1}{x}
=\frac{(1+x)-1}{x(\sqrt{1+x}+1)}
=\frac1{\sqrt{1+x}+1}.
$$

The denominator tends to $2$, so the limit is $1/2$. The domain near zero permits approach from both sides.

2. As $x\to3^-$, the denominator is negative and approaches zero, so $g(x)\to-\infty$. From the right it is positive, so $g(x)\to+\infty$. No two-sided limit exists at $3$. As $x\to+\infty$, the denominator grows without bound, so its reciprocal tends to zero.
