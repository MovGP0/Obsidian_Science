> [!TLDR]
> A function is continuous at a point when its nearby values tend to its value at that point.

## Three different questions

Let $f:D\to\mathbb R$ and $a\in D$. The function value $f(a)$ is the output assigned at exactly $a$. The limit $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)$ concerns outputs at nearby domain points. At an accumulation point $a$ of $D$, continuity connects these objects:

$$
f\text{ is continuous at }a
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\lim_{\substack{x\to a\\x\in D}}f(x)=f(a).
$$

At an accumulation point, this requires a defined value, an existing finite limit, and equality of the two. At a domain endpoint, the limit uses the available side. More generally, continuity relative to $D$ means: for every $\varepsilon>0$, there is $\delta>0$ such that $x\in D$ and $|x-a|<\delta$ imply $|f(x)-f(a)|<\varepsilon$. This formulation also covers isolated domain points.

Continuity is local. A function can be continuous at one point and discontinuous at another. Continuity on an interval means continuity at every point of the interval, with one-sided continuity at each included endpoint. See [[07.01 Limits]] for precise meanings of the relevant limits.

## Continuous extension

Suppose $f$ is not defined at $a$, but its finite limit there is $L$. Keep all old values and define a new function $F$ with $F(a)=L$. Then $F$ is a continuous extension at $a$. It is a new function because its domain contains an additional point.

For example, let

$$
f(x)=\frac{x^2-1}{x-1},\qquad D=\mathbb R\setminus\{1\}.
$$

For $x\ne1$, factorisation gives $f(x)=x+1$. Thus the limit at $1$ is $2$, and $F(x)=x+1$ for all real $x$ is a continuous extension. It is the only continuous extension there, because continuity forces $F(1)=2$. The original fraction remains undefined at $1$.

If a function already assigns the wrong value at $a$, changing that single value can repair the discontinuity. If the two one-sided limits differ, changing one point cannot repair it. An unbounded limit or persistent oscillation also prevents a real continuous extension. For $\sin(1/x)$ at zero, arbitrarily close inputs give outputs $1$ and $-1$, so no single assigned value can fix the limit.

## Worked example: join two formulas

Find real $k$ and $c$ that make the following function continuous on $\mathbb R$:

$$
F(x)=
\begin{cases}
kx+1,&x<2,\\
c,&x=2,\\
x^2-1,&x>2.
\end{cases}
$$

Each polynomial branch is continuous within its own open interval. Only the join needs a check. The left-hand limit is $2k+1$, while the right-hand limit is $2^2-1=3$. Therefore

$$
2k+1=3\quad\Longrightarrow\quad k=1.
$$

The common limit is then $3$, so the point value must be $c=3$. Both conditions are necessary. Choosing only $c=3$ leaves a jump unless $k=1$ too.

## What continuity permits

Sums, products, and compositions of continuous functions remain continuous wherever defined. Quotients remain continuous where their denominators are nonzero. Thus $\sqrt{x-1}/(x-3)$ is continuous on $[1,3)\cup(3,\infty)$ relative to that domain. The endpoint $1$ uses a right-hand check, and $3$ is excluded.

The intermediate value theorem states that a continuous function on $[a,b]$ takes every value between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$. It need not take each value only once. The extreme value theorem states that a continuous real function on a closed bounded interval attains a minimum and a maximum. The hypotheses matter: $1/x$ on $(0,1)$ has neither a minimum nor a maximum.

Continuity does not require a tangent. The function $|x|$ is continuous at zero, but its left and right slopes differ. [[07.03 The derivative]] introduces the stronger condition of differentiability.

## Practice

1. Define $h(x)=\sin x/x$ for $x\ne0$ and $h(0)=5$. Given the radian limit $\lim_{x\to0}\sin x/x=1$, decide whether $h$ is continuous and give a repair.
2. Prove that $p(x)=x^3+x-1$ has exactly one zero in $(0,1)$.

## Worked answers

1. The nearby-value limit is $1$, but the assigned value is $5$. Since they differ, $h$ is discontinuous at zero. Replacing $h(0)$ by $1$ makes it continuous there. All other values remain as given.

2. The polynomial is continuous on $[0,1]$, and $p(0)=-1$, $p(1)=1$. The intermediate value theorem gives an interior zero. For $v>u$,

$$
p(v)-p(u)=(v-u)(v^2+uv+u^2+1)>0.
$$

The second factor is positive because $v^2+uv+u^2=(v+u/2)^2+3u^2/4\ge0$. Thus $p$ is strictly increasing and cannot have two zeros. Continuity gives existence; the additional increasing-property argument gives uniqueness.
