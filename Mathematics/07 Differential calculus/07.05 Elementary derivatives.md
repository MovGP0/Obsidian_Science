title: "Elementary derivatives"

> [!TLDR]
> Elementary derivative formulas apply on specified real domains and combine with the chain rule for more complex inputs.

## Powers and their domains

All derivatives here are real derivatives at interior domain points. For an integer $n\ge2$, the binomial expansion gives

$$
\frac{(x+h)^n-x^n}{h}=nx^{n-1}+\binom n2x^{n-2}h+\cdots+h^{n-1}.
$$

Every term after the first tends to zero, proving $(x^n)'=nx^{n-1}$. For $n=1$, the derivative of $x$ is directly $1$, which obeys the same formula. The constant $1=x^0$ has derivative zero. For a general real exponent $p$, define $x^p=e^{p\ln x}$ on $x>0$. The chain rule then gives

$$
\frac{d}{dx}x^p=e^{p\ln x}\frac p x=px^{p-1}.
$$

Negative integer powers also have a real meaning on $x<0$ and obey the rule for $x\ne0$. Rational powers with odd denominators can have negative inputs, depending on the definition. Do not extend the definition $e^{p\ln x}$ to negative inputs without a separate justification. The function $\sqrt{x}$ is defined at zero, but its derivative $1/(2\sqrt{x})$ applies only for $x>0$. Its right difference quotient is unbounded at zero.

## Exponentials and logarithms

The number $e$ can be characterised as the positive base for which $\lim_{h\to0}(e^h-1)/h=1$. Using $e^{x+h}=e^xe^h$,

$$
\frac{e^{x+h}-e^x}{h}=e^x\frac{e^h-1}{h}\longrightarrow e^x.
$$

Thus $(e^x)'=e^x$ on $\mathbb R$. For $b>0$, write $b^x=e^{x\ln b}$ to obtain $(b^x)'=b^x\ln b$. This includes $b=1$, whose exponential is constant. A logarithm of base $b$ additionally requires $b\ne1$.

From the inverse relation $x=e^y$, with $y=\ln x$, differentiation gives $1=e^yy'=xy'$. Hence

$$
(\ln x)'=\frac1x\quad(x>0),\qquad
(\log_bx)'=\frac1{x\ln b}\quad(x>0).
$$

The inverse derivative theorem justifies this step; see [[07.07 Implicit and inverse differentiation]]. On each interval excluding zero, $\ln|x|$ also has derivative $1/x$. The absolute value permits negative inputs but does not make the expression defined at zero.

## Trigonometric functions

Angles must be in radians. The geometric limits

$$
\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h}=1,\qquad
\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos h-1}{h}=0
$$

combine with the sine addition formula to give

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\sin(x+h)-\sin x}{h}
&=\sin x\frac{\cos h-1}{h}+\cos x\frac{\sin h}{h}\\
&\longrightarrow\cos x.
\end{aligned}
$$

The cosine addition formula similarly gives $(\cos x)'=-\sin x$. These formulas hold for all real inputs. Since $\tan x=\sin x/\cos x$, the quotient rule gives

$$
(\tan x)'=\frac{\cos^2x+\sin^2x}{\cos^2x}
=\sec^2x,
$$

where $\sec x=1/\cos x$. Exclude $x=\pi/2+k\pi$ for integer $k$. If instead the input is a number of degrees $d$, the function is $\sin(\pi d/180)$ and its derivative is $(\pi/180)\cos(\pi d/180)$. The radians convention avoids this extra factor.

## Worked example with a domain check

Differentiate $f(x)=x^{3/2}+\ln x-\cos(2x)$. The logarithm restricts the domain to $x>0$, which also supports the real power. Use the sum rule and the inner derivative of $2x$:

$$
f'(x)=\frac32x^{1/2}+\frac1x+2\sin(2x),\qquad x>0.
$$

The final sign is positive because the original minus sign multiplies the minus sign in the cosine derivative. At $x=1$, the derivative is $5/2+2\sin2$. A formula must preserve signs and domains as well as the form of each term. For compound arguments, use [[07.04 Differentiation rules]].

## Practice

1. Differentiate $g(x)=5^x+\log_5x$ and state its domain.
2. Differentiate $q(x)=\ln|x|+\tan x$ and list all excluded inputs.

## Worked answers

1. The two derivative formulas give

$$
g'(x)=5^x\ln5+\frac1{x\ln5},\qquad x>0.
$$

Although the exponential accepts every real input, the sum uses the common domain of both terms. The logarithm restricts it to positive inputs.

2. For negative $x$, $\ln|x|=\ln(-x)$, whose derivative is $(-1)/(-x)=1/x$. For positive $x$, the ordinary logarithm formula applies. Therefore

$$
q'(x)=\frac1x+\sec^2x.
$$

Exclude zero and every $\pi/2+k\pi$, $k\in\mathbb Z$. The formula holds on each remaining open interval separately.
