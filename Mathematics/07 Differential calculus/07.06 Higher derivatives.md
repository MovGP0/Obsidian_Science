> [!TLDR]
> Repeated derivatives describe changes in slope and help identify concavity, stationary points, and local extrema.

## Repeated differentiation

If the derivative function $f'$ is itself differentiable, its derivative is $f''$, the second derivative. Repetition gives $f'''$ and $f^{(n)}$, the derivative of order $n$. The notation $f^{(n)}$ is not a power. An equivalent notation is $d^nf/dx^n$. Each stage needs its own existence check; a function can have one derivative without having two.

For example, if $f(x)=x^4-2x^2$, repeated use of [[07.05 Elementary derivatives]] gives

$$
f'(x)=4x^3-4x,\quad f''(x)=12x^2-4,\quad
f'''(x)=24x,\quad f^{(4)}(x)=24.
$$

All further derivatives are zero. For $\sin x$, successive derivatives instead cycle through $\cos x,-\sin x,-\cos x,\sin x$.

## Slope, concavity, and stationary points

On an interval where $f$ is differentiable, $f'>0$ implies strict increase and $f'<0$ implies strict decrease. These implications follow from the mean value theorem. It states that continuity on $[u,v]$ and differentiability on $(u,v)$ give an interior point $t$ with $f(v)-f(u)=f'(t)(v-u)$. If $u<v$ and every derivative in the interval is positive, this difference is positive. The argument works for any pair $u<v$ in the interval and proves strict increase. Negative derivatives give strict decrease by the same argument. A derivative sign at just one point is insufficient.

A function is convex, or concave upward, when its graph lies below each chord joining two of its points. Concavity downward reverses the inequality. For a twice differentiable function on an interval, $f''\ge0$ implies convexity, and $f''\le0$ implies concavity downward. Strict signs give strict bending. The second derivative describes changes in slope, not whether the function is positive or increasing.

A stationary point $c$ satisfies $f'(c)=0$. A local minimum means $f(c)\le f(x)$ for every sufficiently close domain point $x$. A local maximum reverses the inequality. At an interior differentiable local extremum, $f'(c)=0$. This necessary condition is not sufficient: $x^3$ is stationary at zero with no extremum. Endpoints and nondifferentiable points can also be extrema and require separate checks.

For a stationary point $c$, the first derivative test uses signs on its two sides. More generally, the test requires continuity at $c$ and differentiability on adjacent open intervals. A change from negative to positive gives a local minimum; positive to negative gives a local maximum. With no sign change there is no extremum, provided the derivative has a fixed strict sign on each adjacent interval. If $f$ is twice continuously differentiable near $c$ and $f'(c)=0$, then $f''(c)>0$ gives a strict minimum and $f''(c)<0$ a strict maximum. A value $f''(c)=0$ leaves this second derivative test inconclusive.

## Worked example: classify a polynomial

For $f(x)=x^4-2x^2$ on $\mathbb R$, factor the first derivative:

$$
f'(x)=4x(x-1)(x+1).
$$

The stationary inputs are $-1,0,1$. The second derivative values are $f''(-1)=8$, $f''(0)=-4$, and $f''(1)=8$. Thus $(-1,-1)$ and $(1,-1)$ are local minima, while $(0,0)$ is a local maximum.

The first derivative signs are negative on $(-\infty,-1)$, positive on $(-1,0)$, negative on $(0,1)$, and positive on $(1,\infty)$. This confirms the classifications and gives every interval of increase and decrease.

For concavity, solve $12x^2-4=0$. This gives $x=\pm1/\sqrt3$. The second derivative is positive outside these inputs and negative between them. Concavity therefore changes at both. The output value is

$$
f(\pm1/\sqrt3)=\frac19-\frac23=-\frac59.
$$

These are inflection points: the function is continuous there and its concavity changes. A zero second derivative alone does not prove an inflection. For $x^4$, the second derivative vanishes at zero but stays nonnegative on both sides.

The two minima in the worked example are also global, since $f(x)=(x^2-1)^2-1\ge-1$. This algebraic bound gives a stronger conclusion than the local test. There is no global maximum on $\mathbb R$, because $f(x)\to+\infty$ as $|x|\to\infty$.

## Practice

1. Classify the stationary point at zero for $p(x)=x^4$, $q(x)=-x^4$, and $r(x)=x^3$.
2. Find the global extrema of $s(x)=x^3-3x$ on $[-2,2]$ and classify its interior stationary points.

## Worked answers

1. The first and second derivatives of all three functions vanish at zero. Since $x^4\ge0$ with equality only there, $p$ has a strict minimum. Since $-x^4\le0$, $q$ has a strict maximum. For $r$, $r'(x)=3x^2>0$ on both sides, so no extremum occurs. Its second derivative $6x$ changes sign, giving a stationary inflection.

2. The derivative $s'(x)=3(x^2-1)$ vanishes at $-1$ and $1$. Evaluate all candidates, including endpoints: $s(-2)=-2$, $s(-1)=2$, $s(1)=-2$, $s(2)=2$. The global minimum is $-2$ at $-2$ and $1$; the global maximum is $2$ at $-1$ and $2$. Since $s''(-1)=-6$ and $s''(1)=6$, the interior points are a local maximum and minimum respectively. An interior-only search would miss endpoint occurrences of the global values.
