> [!TLDR]
> The derivative is the limit of nearby secant slopes and gives the slope of a function's tangent line.

## From two points to a local slope

Let $f$ be a real function on an open interval containing $a$. For a nonzero input increment $h$, the points $(a,f(a))$ and $(a+h,f(a+h))$ determine a secant line. Its slope is the difference quotient

$$
\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}h.
$$

The denominator is an input change. The numerator is the corresponding output change. The derivative at $a$ is the finite two-sided limit

$$
f'(a)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}h,
$$

if it exists. Equivalent notation is $\left.\frac{df}{dx}\right|_{x=a}$. Here $d/dx$ is a limit-based operation, not a quotient of independent numbers. A derivative at a point is a number. The derivative function $f'$ assigns such a number to each point where the derivative exists.

The tangent line at $a$ is

$$
y=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a).
$$

A tangent need not meet the graph at only one point or remain on one side of it. Its defining local property is that its slope is the limit of the secant slopes.

## Worked example from the definition

Take $f(x)=x^2$. At any real $a$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}h
&=\frac{(a+h)^2-a^2}h\\
&=\frac{2ah+h^2}h=2a+h\qquad(h\ne0).
\end{aligned}
$$

Only after cancellation do we take the limit. As $h\to0$, the slope tends to $2a$. Thus $f'(a)=2a$, and the derivative function is $f'(x)=2x$. At $a=1$, the tangent is $y=1+2(x-1)=2x-1$. For $h=0.8$, the secant slope is $2.8$; for $h=0.3$, it is $2.3$.

![[math-07-secant-to-tangent.svg|**Fig. 07.1** Secant slopes for x² approach the tangent slope 2 at x = 1 as the second point approaches the first.]]

The red tangent is the same in both panels. The orange secant changes because its second point changes. Negative $h$ also gives slopes $2+h$ and approaches the same limit from below. A two-sided derivative needs both approaches to agree.

## The local approximation behind the definition

If $f'(a)=m$, define the remainder $r(h)=f(a+h)-f(a)-mh$. The derivative definition says $r(h)/h\to0$, so

$$
f(a+h)=f(a)+mh+r(h),\qquad r(h)=o(h).
$$

The notation $o(h)$ means that the remainder divided by $h$ tends to zero. It describes an error smaller than first order and is the basis of [[07.08 Linear approximation]]. In particular, $mh\to0$ and $r(h)\to0$, so differentiability implies continuity. The reverse implication fails.

## When the derivative fails

For $f(x)=|x|$ at zero, the quotient is $|h|/h$. It equals $1$ for positive $h$ and $-1$ for negative $h$. The one-sided limits differ. There is no derivative at zero, although the function is continuous there.

For $f(x)=\sqrt[3]{x}$ at zero, the quotient is $h^{-2/3}$ and grows without bound. The graph has a vertical tangent, but the ordinary real derivative is not finite. Do not treat infinity as an ordinary real derivative value. At an endpoint, only a one-sided derivative may be available; identify it as such.

Do not set $h=0$ in the original quotient: this gives $0/0$, not a slope. Do not infer a derivative from a smooth-looking plot or a single small numerical increment. Subtraction of nearly equal outputs can also cause loss of numerical precision. An exact limit argument avoids that uncertainty.

## Practice

1. Use the definition to differentiate $f(x)=3x+4$ at any real $a$.
2. Use the definition to differentiate $g(x)=1/x$ at $a\ne0$, then find its tangent at $a=2$.

## Worked answers

1. For every nonzero $h$, the quotient is

$$
\frac{3(a+h)+4-(3a+4)}h=\frac{3h}h=3.
$$

Its limit is $3$. A straight line has the same secant and tangent slope everywhere.

2. Restrict $h$ so that $a+h\ne0$. Combining fractions gives

$$
\frac{1/(a+h)-1/a}h
=\frac{-h}{a(a+h)h}
=-\frac1{a(a+h)}.
$$

The limit is $-1/a^2$. At $a=2$, the graph point is $(2,1/2)$ and the slope is $-1/4$. Its tangent is $y=1/2-(x-2)/4=1-x/4$.
