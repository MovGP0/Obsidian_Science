---
title: "Abelian phase connections"
---

> [!TLDR]
> With the convention D = d + ia, a frame phase change by exp(iχ) sends a to a + dχ and cancels the derivative of the component phase.

## Fix the sign convention

Consider a complex line bundle with a Hermitian inner product. Choose a local unit frame $e$, so a section has the form $s=e\psi$ with complex component $\psi$. A unitary frame change is $e'=e\,g$, where $g=e^{i\chi}$ and $\chi$ is a smooth real function on the local patch. The same section then has components

$$
\psi'=e^{-i\chi}\psi.
$$

Use the covariant derivative $D=d+ia$, where $a$ is a real one-form and $i^2=-1$. Thus the connection form is $A=ia$. This anti-Hermitian form preserves the Hermitian inner product. A different convention, such as $D=d-ia$, requires corresponding sign changes everywhere.

The group $U(1)$ consists of complex numbers with modulus one. It is **Abelian**, which means its elements commute. Therefore $g^{-1}Ag=A$, and the general connection transformation gives

$$
ia'=ia+e^{-i\chi}d(e^{i\chi})=ia+i\,d\chi,
\qquad a'=a+d\chi.
$$

A smooth $U(1)$-valued map need not have a single real phase function on the entire domain. This calculation uses a local phase, which always exists on a sufficiently small patch.

## Check the cancellation explicitly

Differentiate the transformed component and keep both product terms:

$$
d\psi'=e^{-i\chi}d\psi-i e^{-i\chi}\psi\,d\chi.
$$

Add the transformed connection term:

$$
ia'\psi'=i(a+d\chi)e^{-i\chi}\psi
=ie^{-i\chi}a\psi+i e^{-i\chi}\psi\,d\chi.
$$

The two terms containing $d\chi$ have equal magnitude and opposite signs. Hence

$$
D'\psi'=d\psi'+ia'\psi'=e^{-i\chi}(d\psi+ia\psi)=e^{-i\chi}D\psi.
$$

This identity holds for every section, not just for a selected example. It is the reason for the inhomogeneous term $d\chi$ in $a'$.

## Worked example in two variables

Take the trivial line bundle over $\mathbb R^2$ with coordinates $(x,y)$. Set $a=x\,dy$, $\psi=e^x$, and $\chi=xy$. First compute the original derivative:

$$
D\psi=e^x dx+i x e^x dy.
$$

The transformed fields are

$$
\psi'=e^{x-ixy},\qquad
a'=x\,dy+d(xy)=y\,dx+2x\,dy.
$$

Differentiate the exponent using $d(x-ixy)=(1-iy)dx-ix\,dy$:

$$
d\psi'=e^{x-ixy}\bigl((1-iy)dx-ix\,dy\bigr).
$$

Adding $ia'\psi'=e^{x-ixy}(iy\,dx+2ix\,dy)$ leaves

$$
D'\psi'=e^{x-ixy}(dx+ix\,dy)=e^{-ixy}D\psi.
$$

The cancellation occurs separately in the $dx$ and $dy$ coefficients. The curvature is also unchanged: $da=dx\wedge dy$, while $da'=d(y\,dx+2x\,dy)=-dx\wedge dy+2dx\wedge dy=dx\wedge dy$.

## What remains unchanged

The scalar $|\psi|^2$ is independent of the unit frame. Given a metric on the base, the squared norm of $D\psi$ is also unchanged, since multiplication by $e^{-i\chi}$ preserves complex norms. The component $\psi$ and the real connection form $a$ generally change. Calling all of them invariant would erase this distinction; see [[26.07 Covariance and invariants]].

## Practice

1. On $\mathbb R$, let $a=2x\,dx$. Find a real phase $\chi$ that gives $a'=0$, and check covariance for $\psi=1$.
2. On $\mathbb R^2$, take $a=y\,dx$ and $\chi=x^2-y$. Compute $a'$ and verify that $da'=da$.

## Worked answers

1. Choose $\chi=-x^2$, so $a'=2x\,dx-2x\,dx=0$. The new component is $\psi'=e^{ix^2}$. Thus $D'\psi'=d\psi'=2ix e^{ix^2}dx$, which equals $e^{ix^2}D\psi$ because $D\psi=2ix\,dx$.
2. Since $d\chi=2x\,dx-dy$, $a'=(y+2x)dx-dy$. Its derivative is $dy\wedge dx+2dx\wedge dx=-dx\wedge dy$. This is also $d(y\,dx)$ because $dx\wedge dx=0$ and $d(dy)=0$.
