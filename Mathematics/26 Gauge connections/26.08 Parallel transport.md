> [!TLDR]
> Parallel transport solves the connection equation along a path; transport around a loop can return a vector changed by holonomy.

## Turn a connection into an ordinary differential equation

Let $E\to M$ be a vector bundle with connection $D=d+A$. Let $\gamma:[0,1]\to M$ be a smooth path, with a piecewise smooth path handled one segment at a time. A vector along the path has components $v(t)$ in a selected frame over the path. It is **parallel** when its covariant derivative in the path direction is zero:

$$
\frac{dv}{dt}+B(t)v(t)=0,\qquad
B(t)=A_{\gamma(t)}(\dot\gamma(t)).
$$

This is a linear ordinary differential equation. A prescribed initial vector $v(0)$ determines a unique solution on the path when the connection is smooth. The resulting linear isomorphism from the initial fibre to the final fibre is parallel transport.

Write $v(t)=T(t)v(0)$. Then $\dot T=-B(t)T$ and $T(0)=I$. If all $B(t)$ commute with each other, the solution is

$$
T(1)=\exp\left(-\int_0^1 B(t)\,dt\right).
$$

Without commutation, this ordinary exponential need not solve the equation. One can solve successively, or use the path-ordered exponential. Its first terms are

$$
T(1)=I-\int_0^1B(t_1)dt_1
+\int_{0<t_2<t_1<1}B(t_1)B(t_2)dt_2dt_1-\cdots.
$$

Later times appear to the left. This ordering follows by substituting the integral equation $T(t)=I-\int_0^t B(u)T(u)du$ into itself.

## Worked example: two paths with the same endpoints

Take a complex line over $\mathbb R^2$ and use $A=ia$ with $a=x\,dy$. Scalars commute, so transport is multiplication by $\exp(-i\int_\gamma a)$.

Compare paths from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$. Path $\gamma_1$ goes first horizontally to $(1,0)$, then vertically to $(1,1)$. On the horizontal segment $dy=0$, so the integral is zero. On the vertical segment $x=1$, so $\int_0^1 x\,dy=1$. Thus $T_1=e^{-i}$.

Path $\gamma_2$ goes first vertically to $(0,1)$, then horizontally to $(1,1)$. The first integral is zero because $x=0$; the second is zero because $dy=0$. Thus $T_2=1$. The transported vectors differ even though both paths have the same endpoints.

The loop that follows $\gamma_1$ and then the reverse of $\gamma_2$ is counterclockwise around the unit square. Its transport is $e^{-i}$. Stokes' theorem confirms

$$
\oint a=\int_{[0,1]^2}da
=\int_{[0,1]^2}dx\wedge dy=1.
$$

The orientation is essential: reversing the loop changes the integral to $-1$ and the transport to $e^i$.

## Holonomy and frame changes

For a closed path based at $p$, transport is an automorphism $H_\gamma:E_p\to E_p$, called its **holonomy**. In a frame it is a matrix. Under $e'=eg$, endpoint components give

$$
T'_\gamma=g(\gamma(1))^{-1}T_\gamma g(\gamma(0)).
$$

For a loop, $H'_\gamma=g(p)^{-1}H_\gamma g(p)$. Thus the matrix can change by conjugation, while its trace and eigenvalues do not. For $U(1)$, conjugation has no effect.

Curvature controls small contractible loops. For a positively oriented small coordinate rectangle, the leading change is $H=I-F_{xy}\,\Delta x\Delta y$ plus terms of higher order. Large loops also depend on the domain and the connection along the full path. Even zero curvature can allow nonidentity holonomy around a hole; see [[26.09 Pure gauge and global limits]].

## Practice

1. On the real line with $A=2x\,dx$ in a real line bundle, transport $v(0)=3$ from $x=0$ to $x=2$.
2. For $a=\beta x\,dy$, with real constant $\beta$, find $U(1)$ transport around a counterclockwise rectangle of width $b$ and height $c$.

## Worked answers

1. The equation is $dv/dx+2xv=0$. Divide by $v$ for the nonzero solution and integrate: $\log(v/3)=-x^2$. Therefore $v(2)=3e^{-4}$. This real connection does not preserve the usual fibre norm.
2. Since $da=\beta\,dx\wedge dy$, Stokes' theorem gives $\oint a=\beta bc$. With $D=d+ia$, the multiplier is $e^{-i\beta bc}$. The sign follows from the negative integral in the parallel transport equation.
