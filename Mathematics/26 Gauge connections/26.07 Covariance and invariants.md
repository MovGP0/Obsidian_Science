---
title: "Covariance and invariants"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Covariant objects change by a specified representation, while invariant combinations have the same value in every allowed frame.

## A transformation rule is part of the object

Let $s=e\psi$ be a section of a complex vector bundle with local frame $e$. Use the convention $e'=eg$, so $\psi'=g^{-1}\psi$. The component column changes, but the geometric section does not. We say the components transform **covariantly** when they obey their specified linear transformation rule. In this usage, covariant means consistent with the representation; it is not a claim about whether an index is written above or below.

For a connection $D=d+A$ with $A'=g^{-1}Ag+g^{-1}dg$, the derivative obeys

$$
D'\psi'=g^{-1}D\psi.
$$

The curvature $F=dA+A\wedge A$ obeys a different representation rule:

$$
F'=g^{-1}Fg.
$$

This is conjugation, also called the adjoint action on endomorphisms. Neither formula says the numerical component entries stay fixed. An **invariant** is a quantity whose value is unchanged by the allowed transformation.

## Norms require a metric hypothesis

If $g$ is unitary, then $g^\dagger g=I$, where $\dagger$ means conjugate transpose. It follows that

$$
\psi'^\dagger\psi'
=\psi^\dagger(g^{-1})^\dagger g^{-1}\psi
=\psi^\dagger\psi.
$$

Thus the usual component norm is invariant under unitary frame changes. It is not invariant under every invertible matrix. For a general frame, write the Hermitian inner product as $\psi^\dagger H\psi$, where $H_{ab}=\langle e_a,e_b\rangle$. The frame metric changes as $H'=g^\dagger H g$, so

$$
\psi'^\dagger H'\psi'=\psi^\dagger H\psi.
$$

The metric must change with a nonorthonormal basis. This restores the same geometric length.

## Worked example: covariant entries and invariant scalars

At one point, let an endomorphism have matrix

$$
K=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
g=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Compute $Kg=\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$ and left multiply by $g^{-1}=\operatorname{diag}(1/2,1)$. The new matrix is

$$
K'=g^{-1}Kg=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&3\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its upper-right entry changed from $2$ to $1$. Its trace is still $4$, its determinant is still $3$, and its eigenvalues are still $1$ and $3$. In general, cyclicity of the trace gives $\operatorname{tr}(g^{-1}Kg)=\operatorname{tr}(K)$, while multiplicativity of the determinant gives $\det(K')=\det(g^{-1})\det(K)\det(g)=\det(K)$.

For a vector with $\psi=(2,1)^T$ and $H=I$, the new components are $\psi'=(1,1)^T$. The ordinary coordinate squares would give $5$ and $2$. But $H'=\operatorname{diag}(4,1)$, so $\psi'^\dagger H'\psi'=4+1=5$. There is no change in the actual vector length.

## Invariants built from curvature

For fixed tangent vectors $X,Y$ at a point, $F(X,Y)$ is an endomorphism. Therefore $\operatorname{tr}(F(X,Y))$ and its characteristic polynomial are frame independent. The differential form $\operatorname{tr}(F\wedge F)$ is also invariant because conjugation cancels inside the trace.

Do not confuse the four-form $\operatorname{tr}(F\wedge F)$ with a pointwise norm of curvature. A scalar norm needs a metric on the base to contract form indices and an inner product on the endomorphisms. On a two-dimensional base, every four-form is zero even if curvature is nonzero. The trace alone can also miss curvature: a nonzero traceless matrix has zero trace.

## Practice

1. Let $K=\operatorname{diag}(2,-2)$. Compute $\operatorname{tr}K$, $\operatorname{tr}(K^2)$, and $\det K$. What does the first value fail to detect?
2. Let $\psi=(1,i)^T$ and $g=\operatorname{diag}(e^{i\theta},e^{-i\theta})$. Verify norm invariance directly.

## Worked answers

1. The trace is $2-2=0$, but $K^2=4I$, so $\operatorname{tr}(K^2)=8$ and $\det K=-4$. A zero trace does not imply that $K=0$. The other two invariants detect nonzero information in this example.
2. The new column is $(e^{-i\theta},i e^{i\theta})^T$. Each entry has squared modulus $1$, so $\psi'^\dagger\psi'=2=\psi^\dagger\psi$. This calculation works because both diagonal factors have modulus one.
