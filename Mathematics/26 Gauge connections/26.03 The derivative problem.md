title: "The derivative problem"

> [!TLDR]
> Differentiating transformed components produces a derivative of the frame change, so the ordinary component derivative is not frame covariant.

## What a derivative must compare

Let $s=e\psi$ be a section of a vector bundle over an open set $U$. The frame $e$ is a row of basis sections and $\psi$ is a column of scalar functions. Under a smooth invertible frame change $e'=eg$, the components obey $\psi'=g^{-1}\psi$. A useful derivative of the section should have components that transform by the same inverse matrix. This requirement is called **covariance**.

The ordinary exterior derivative $d\psi$ differentiates every component. It is a column of one-forms. If $d\psi$ were already covariant, we would have $d\psi'=g^{-1}d\psi$. The product rule instead gives

$$
d\psi'=d(g^{-1}\psi)=(dg^{-1})\psi+g^{-1}d\psi.
$$

The additional term is usually nonzero. It comes from the changing basis, even if the underlying section has no change in a fixed trivialization.

## Differentiate the inverse carefully

Start from $g^{-1}g=I$, where $I$ is the identity matrix. Differentiate and apply the product rule:

$$
(dg^{-1})g+g^{-1}dg=0.
$$

Right multiplication by $g^{-1}$ gives

$$
dg^{-1}=-g^{-1}(dg)g^{-1}.
$$

Use this identity in the derivative of $\psi'$:

$$
d\psi'=g^{-1}d\psi-g^{-1}(dg)\psi'.
$$

This is an equality of columns of one-forms. Evaluated on a tangent vector $X$, it becomes

$$
X(\psi')=g^{-1}X(\psi)-g^{-1}X(g)\psi'.
$$

Do not change the order of these factors for matrix-valued $g$. The inverse identity already fixes the correct order.

## Worked example: a constant section in a rotating basis

Take a real rank-two trivial bundle over $\mathbb R$ with coordinate $x$. In a constant frame, let $\psi=(1,0)^T$. Thus $d\psi=0$. Let $g=R(x)$ and introduce the fixed matrix

$$
J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
R(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos x&-\sin x\\\sin x&\cos x\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Direct differentiation gives $dR=R J\,dx$. Therefore $g^{-1}dg=J\,dx$. The transformed components and derivative are

$$
\psi'=\begin{pmatrix}\cos x\\-\sin x\end{pmatrix},\qquad
d\psi'=\begin{pmatrix}-\sin x\\-\cos x\end{pmatrix}dx.
$$

The inverse-rule formula predicts $d\psi'=-J\psi'\,dx$. Multiplying $J\psi'=(\sin x,\cos x)^T$ confirms the result. A zero derivative in one frame has become a nonzero derivative in another frame. Ordinary component differentiation therefore fails the desired transformation rule.

This does not mean ordinary differentiation is invalid. It means that $d\psi$ describes the derivative relative to the particular frame. In a trivial bundle, declaring one global frame to be constant selects a valid derivative operation, but the same operation needs an extra matrix term in other frames.

## How the correction is determined

Seek a derivative of the form $D\psi=d\psi+A\psi$, where $A$ is a matrix-valued one-form. Its transformed version must include a term that cancels $-g^{-1}dg\,\psi'$. For the rotating example, $A=0$ in the original frame requires $A'=J\,dx$ in the new frame. Then

$$
d\psi'+A'\psi'=-J\psi'\,dx+J\psi'\,dx=0.
$$

The full derivation of $A'$ is in [[26.04 Covariant derivatives]]. The key point is already visible: connection coefficients compensate for variation of the chosen frame.

## Practice

1. For a complex line, let $g(x)=e^{ix^2}$ and $\psi(x)=1$. Compute $d\psi'$ and identify the extra term.
2. Let $g(x)=\operatorname{diag}(e^x,e^{-x})$ and $\psi=(x,1)^T$. Check the inverse derivative formula component by component.

## Worked answers

1. We have $\psi'=e^{-ix^2}$, so $d\psi'=-2ix e^{-ix^2}dx$. Since $d\psi=0$ and $g^{-1}dg=2ix\,dx$, the entire result is the extra term $-(g^{-1}dg)\psi'$.
2. The new column is $(xe^{-x},e^x)^T$, with derivative $((1-x)e^{-x},e^x)^Tdx$. Meanwhile $g^{-1}d\psi=(e^{-x},0)^Tdx$ and $g^{-1}dg=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)dx$. Subtracting its product with $\psi'$ gives the same derivative.
