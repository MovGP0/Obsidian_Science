---
title: "Curvature or field strength"
---

> [!TLDR]
> The curvature of D = d + A is F = dA + A ∧ A; the product term records the failure of matrix coefficients to commute.

## Define the product before using it

Let $A=(A^a{}_b)$ be a matrix-valued one-form for a connection on a vector bundle. Matrix multiplication combines with the exterior product by

$$
(A\wedge A)^a{}_b=\sum_c A^a{}_c\wedge A^c{}_b.
$$

The order of matrix factors matters, even though the one-form factors anticommute. Extend $D=d+A\wedge$ to bundle-valued forms. For a component column $\psi$, the graded product rule gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
D^2\psi
&=d(d\psi+A\psi)+A\wedge(d\psi+A\psi)\\
&=dA\,\psi-A\wedge d\psi+A\wedge d\psi+(A\wedge A)\psi\\
&=(dA+A\wedge A)\psi.
\end{aligned}
$$

We used $d^2\psi=0$ and the minus sign in $d(A\psi)=dA\,\psi-A\wedge d\psi$, because $A$ has degree one. The **curvature two-form**, also called field strength in gauge terminology, is

$$
F=dA+A\wedge A.
$$

In coordinates, $A=\sum_i A_i dx^i$ and

$$
F=\frac12\sum_{i,j}F_{ij}dx^i\wedge dx^j,\qquad
F_{ij}=\partial_i A_j-\partial_j A_i+[A_i,A_j],
$$

where $[P,Q]=PQ-QP$. Equivalently, $[D_i,D_j]\psi=F_{ij}\psi$. This coordinate formula assumes coordinate vector fields, which commute. For arbitrary vector fields $X,Y$, curvature is $[\nabla_X,\nabla_Y]-\nabla_{[X,Y]}$.

## Why the Abelian case is simpler

For a complex line, write $A=ia$ with $a$ real. Then $A\wedge A=-a\wedge a=0$, because a scalar one-form wedges with itself to zero. Thus $F=i\,da$. For the real curvature form $f=da$, a phase change $a'=a+d\chi$ gives $f'=da+d^2\chi=f$.

For matrices, the expression $A\wedge A$ need not vanish. Anticommuting $dx$ and $dy$ changes the order of the matrix coefficients in the resulting difference; it does not force that difference to zero.

## Worked example: constant coefficients with nonzero curvature

On $\mathbb R^2$, take the trivial real rank-two bundle and set

$$
P=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
Q=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
A=P\,dx+Q\,dy.
$$

All coefficients are constant, so $dA=0$. The wedge product is

$$
A\wedge A=PQ\,dx\wedge dy+QP\,dy\wedge dx
=(PQ-QP)dx\wedge dy.
$$

Compute $PQ=\operatorname{diag}(1,0)$ and $QP=\operatorname{diag}(0,1)$. Therefore

$$
F=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}dx\wedge dy.
$$

The curvature is nonzero although $dA=0$. On the column $v=(1,2)^T$, the commutator gives $[D_x,D_y]v=(1,-2)^T$. This identifies a measurable algebraic failure of the two derivative operations to commute. No variation of the coefficient matrices is needed.

## Transformation and interpretation

With $e'=eg$ and $A'=g^{-1}Ag+g^{-1}dg$, curvature transforms as $F'=g^{-1}Fg$. One way to see this is to apply $D'^2$ to $\psi'=g^{-1}\psi$: covariance of $D$ gives $D'^2\psi'=g^{-1}D^2\psi$. Since $\psi$ is arbitrary, the curvature matrices must be conjugate.

Nonzero curvature prevents the connection from being changed to zero on a neighborhood. Zero curvature guarantees local removal on sufficiently small contractible patches, but it does not settle global transport around holes; see [[26.09 Pure gauge and global limits]].

## Practice

1. For a line connection $A=i x^2dy$ on $\mathbb R^2$, compute $F$.
2. Let $A=P\,dx+2P\,dy$ for any constant square matrix $P$. Compute $F$ and explain why the product term vanishes.

## Worked answers

1. The scalar product term is zero. Differentiate $x^2dy$: $d(x^2dy)=2x\,dx\wedge dy$. Thus $F=2ix\,dx\wedge dy$. It vanishes on the line $x=0$, but it is not zero on any open neighborhood of that line.
2. Here $dA=0$ and $[P,2P]=2P^2-2P^2=0$. Hence $F=0$. Matrix-valued forms can have a zero product term when their coefficients commute; the term is not automatically nonzero merely because matrices occur.
