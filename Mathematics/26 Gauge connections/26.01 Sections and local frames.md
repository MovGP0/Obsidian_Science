> [!TLDR]
> A section chooses one vector in each fibre; a local frame expresses those vectors by scalar component functions.

## Fibres, sections, and components

Let $M$ be a smooth manifold and let $\pi:E\to M$ be a real or complex vector bundle of rank $r$. For each point $p\in M$, the **fibre** $E_p=\pi^{-1}(p)$ is an $r$-dimensional vector space. A smooth **section** is a map $s:M\to E$ with $\pi(s(p))=p$. This condition says that $s(p)$ belongs to the fibre above $p$, rather than to an unrelated fibre.

On an open set $U\subseteq M$, choose smooth sections $e_1,\ldots,e_r$ whose values form a basis of $E_p$ at each $p\in U$. This list is a **local frame**. Write it as the row $e=(e_1,\ldots,e_r)$. There are unique smooth scalar functions $\psi^1,\ldots,\psi^r$ such that

$$
s|_U=\sum_{a=1}^r e_a\psi^a=e\psi,
\qquad
\psi=\begin{pmatrix}\psi^1\\\vdots\\\psi^r\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The index $a$ labels basis vectors. It does not label coordinates on $M$. The column $\psi$ represents the section in the selected frame. It is not the section itself. The notation $e\psi$ means a linear combination in each fibre; it does not identify different fibres.

A trivial bundle $E=M\times\mathbb R^r$ has a global constant frame. A general bundle need only have frames on open sets. The need for several frames does not prevent a section from being globally defined: its component formulas must agree on overlaps through the transition matrices.

## A frame change at the same point

Let $g:U\to\operatorname{GL}(r,\mathbb R)$ be smooth and set $e'=eg$. The columns of $g$ give the new basis vectors in the old basis. Since the geometric section is unchanged,

$$
e\psi=e'\psi'=eg\psi',
\qquad
\psi'=g^{-1}\psi.
$$

Invertibility makes both the frame and the component column unique. In the complex case, replace $\mathbb R$ by $\mathbb C$. If frames must preserve a Hermitian inner product, use unitary matrices rather than all invertible matrices.

## Worked example: rotating a real frame

Use the standard basis of $\mathbb R^2$ and let $s(p)$ have component column $\psi=(1,\sqrt3)^T$. Rotate the basis through $\theta=\pi/6$:

$$
g=R(\theta)=
\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Since $R^{-1}=R^T$, the new components are

$$
\psi'=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt3/2&1/2\\-1/2&\sqrt3/2\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}1\\\sqrt3\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt3\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Reconstruction gives $\sqrt3\,e'_1+e'_2=e_1+\sqrt3\,e_2$. The lengths agree because the matrix is orthogonal: $1+3=3+1$.

For a complex line bundle, a frame consists of one nonzero vector $e(p)$. If $e'=e\,\exp(i\chi)$, then $\psi'=\exp(-i\chi)\psi$. A phase in a component therefore depends on the frame. The value of $|\psi|$ is unchanged under this unitary change.

## Common errors

Do not add vectors in different fibres without a rule that compares those fibres. A local frame supplies coordinates for them, but the comparison then depends on that frame. A connection supplies a more structured comparison; see [[26.04 Covariant derivatives]]. Also distinguish a basis index from a base coordinate index. A rank-two bundle can lie over a base of any dimension.

## Practice

1. If $e'_1=2e_1$ and $e'_2=e_1+e_2$, find the new components of $s=3e_1+4e_2$.
2. In a complex line, let $e'=ie$ and $s=e(2-i)$. Find $\psi'$ and verify reconstruction.

## Worked answers

1. Here $g=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. Solve $g\psi'=(3,4)^T$: the second row gives $\psi'^2=4$ and the first gives $2\psi'^1+4=3$. Thus $\psi'=(-1/2,4)^T$. Substitution gives $(-1/2)(2e_1)+4(e_1+e_2)=3e_1+4e_2$.
2. Since $g=i$, its inverse is $-i$. Therefore $\psi'=(-i)(2-i)=-1-2i$. Reconstruction gives $ie(-1-2i)=e(2-i)$, as required.
