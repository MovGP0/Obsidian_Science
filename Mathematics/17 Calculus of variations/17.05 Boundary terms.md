---
title: "Boundary terms"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Fixed boundary values restrict the variations, while free boundary values produce additional stationarity conditions from the boundary term.

## Keep the bracket until the restrictions are known

For a smooth integral functional $J[y]=\int_a^b F(x,y,y')dx$, integration by parts gives

$$
\delta J[y;\eta]
=\int_a^b\left(F_z-\frac{dF_p}{dx}\right)\eta\,dx
+[F_p\eta]_a^b.
$$

Here $\eta$ is the initial change in $y$, and $F_z,F_p$ denote derivatives with respect to the function-value and derivative arguments. The last term is explicitly $F_p(b)\eta(b)-F_p(a)\eta(a)$. The minus sign at the initial endpoint matters.

First use compactly supported variations, which vanish near the endpoints. They give the interior equation $F_z-dF_p/dx=0$ by the fundamental lemma. Then use all remaining admissible endpoint changes. This separates two logically different conditions: an equation in the interval and conditions on its boundary.

If $y(a)$ is prescribed, then $\eta(a)=0$ and no stationarity condition follows from that endpoint bracket. If $y(b)$ is free while the coordinate $b$ is fixed, $\eta(b)$ is arbitrary. After the interior equation is satisfied, stationarity requires

$$
F_p(b,y(b),y'(b))=0.
$$

This is a **natural boundary condition**. If both function values are free, the corresponding condition holds at both endpoints. A natural condition is not automatically $y'=0$; it is $F_p=0$, which depends on the integrand.

## Worked example: one free value

Consider

$$
J[y]=\int_0^1\left(\frac12y'^2+y\right)dx,
\qquad y(0)=0,
$$

with $y(1)$ free. The first variation is $\int_0^1(y'\eta'+\eta)dx$. After integration by parts,

$$
\delta J=\int_0^1(1-y'')\eta\,dx+y'(1)\eta(1),
$$

because $\eta(0)=0$. Interior tests give $y''=1$. The free final value gives $y'(1)=0$. Writing $y=x^2/2+C_1x+C_2$, we obtain $C_2=0$, $C_1=-1$, and

$$
y_*(x)=\frac12x^2-x,\qquad y_*(1)=-\frac12.
$$

For any $h$ with $h(0)=0$, the functional difference is

$$
J[y_*+h]-J[y_*]
=[y_*'h]_0^1+\int_0^1(1-y_*'')h\,dx
+\frac12\int_0^1h'^2dx
=\frac12\int_0^1h'^2dx.
$$

The boundary term is zero because of the natural condition at $1$ and the fixed value at $0$. Equality again requires $h=0$, so this candidate is the unique global minimizer. Imposing $y(1)=0$ would change the admissible set and give a different minimizer.

## Endpoint terms and moving endpoints

If the functional contains a final-value term,

$$
\widetilde J[y]=\int_a^bF(x,y,y')dx+B(y(b)),
$$

its variation adds $B'(y(b))\eta(b)$. A free final value at fixed $b$ therefore gives $F_p(b)+B'(y(b))=0$. Discarding the bracket would lose this condition.

A moving endpoint coordinate is a different problem. At a moving right endpoint $(b,y(b))$, let $\Delta b$ and $\Delta Y$ denote the changes of its coordinates. The initial change of the function at the old coordinate is $\eta(b)=\Delta Y-y'(b)\Delta b$. The upper integration limit also adds $F(b)\Delta b$. Thus the right-endpoint contribution becomes

$$
F_p(b)\Delta Y+(F-y'F_p)(b)\Delta b.
$$

If both coordinate changes are independent, both coefficients must vanish. If the endpoint lies on a prescribed curve, the two changes are related and a corresponding transversality condition follows. This formula assumes no extra endpoint cost. It does not replace the fixed-coordinate condition used above.

## Practice

1. For $J[y]=\int_0^1\frac12(y'-1)^2dx$, fix $y(0)=0$ and leave $y(1)$ free. Find the stationary curve.
2. For $J[y]=\int_0^1\frac12y'^2dx+\frac{k}{2}(y(1)-d)^2$, with $k>0$ and $y(0)=0$, derive the natural condition and solve it.

### Worked answers

1. Here $F_p=y'-1$, so the interior equation is $y''=0$ and the natural condition is $y'(1)-1=0$. The affine solution with initial value zero is $y=x$. Notice that its endpoint derivative is $1$, not $0$.
2. The interior equation is $y''=0$, so $y=cx$. The endpoint derivative contributes $c\eta(1)$, and the final-value cost contributes $k(c-d)\eta(1)$. Since $\eta(1)$ is arbitrary, $c+k(c-d)=0$. Therefore $c=kd/(1+k)$ and $y(x)=kdx/(1+k)$.
