> [!TLDR]
> Constraints restrict the allowed directions; an integral constraint usually uses a scalar multiplier, while a pointwise constraint usually uses a multiplier function.

## Restricted stationarity

An unrestricted stationary function has zero first variation in every direction permitted by the endpoint data. A constrained stationary function needs zero first variation only along changes that remain in the constraint set. These requirements differ. A constrained minimum can have a nonzero unrestricted first variation.

Suppose that $J[y]$ is to be minimized subject to a scalar equality $G[y]=M$. At a regular candidate, the first variation of $G$ is a nonzero linear functional. Under the differentiability hypotheses of a multiplier theorem, constrained stationarity implies that a constant $\lambda\in\mathbb R$ exists with

$$
\delta(J+\lambda(G-M))[y;\eta]=0
$$

for every endpoint-compatible direction $\eta$. Geometrically, $\delta J$ vanishes on the kernel of $\delta G$ and is therefore a scalar multiple of $\delta G$. The sign of the multiplier is a convention; changing from $J+\lambda G$ to $J-\lambda G$ changes $\lambda$, not the candidate function. One must also impose the original constraint after solving the augmented equations.

## Worked example: a fixed integral

Minimize

$$
J[y]=\frac12\int_0^1y'^2dx,
\qquad y(0)=y(1)=0,\qquad \int_0^1y\,dx=1.
$$

Use the augmented functional

$$
\mathcal L[y,\lambda]
=\int_0^1\left(\frac12y'^2+\lambda y\right)dx-\lambda.
$$

Variation in $y$ gives $\lambda-y''=0$. Variation in the scalar $\lambda$ gives the integral constraint. Solving $y''=\lambda$ with the endpoint values gives $y=(\lambda/2)x(x-1)$. Since $\int_0^1x(x-1)dx=-1/6$, the integral condition is $-\lambda/12=1$. Therefore

$$
\lambda=-12,\qquad y_*(x)=6x(1-x).
$$

This is more than a stationary candidate. Write another constrained input as $y_*+h$, where $h(0)=h(1)=0$ and $\int_0^1h\,dx=0$. Then

$$
J[y_*+h]-J[y_*]
=\int_0^1y_*'h' dx+\frac12\int_0^1h'^2dx.
$$

The first integral is $[y_*'h]_0^1-\int_0^1y_*''h\,dx=12\int_0^1h\,dx=0$. Thus the difference is nonnegative, with equality only when $h=0$. The value is $J[y_*]=\frac12\int_0^1(6-12x)^2dx=6$. Without the integral constraint the minimizer would be zero, which is not admissible here.

## Pointwise constraints require local multipliers

Let $u,v$ be functions and impose $u(x)^2+v(x)^2=1$ at every point. There is one condition for each $x$, so introduce a multiplier function $\lambda(x)$:

$$
\mathcal L[u,v,\lambda]
=\int_a^b\left(\frac12(u'^2+v'^2)
+\frac{\lambda(x)}2(u^2+v^2-1)\right)dx.
$$

Independent variations give

$$
-u''+\lambda u=0,\qquad -v''+\lambda v=0,
\qquad u^2+v^2=1.
$$

To check the sign, differentiate the constraint twice. It gives $uu''+vv''=-(u'^2+v'^2)$. Substituting $u''=\lambda u$ and $v''=\lambda v$ then gives $\lambda=-(u'^2+v'^2)$. The general method for a pointwise constraint uses a multiplier function, because the constraint is imposed independently at each point.

Locally parameterize the circle by $u=\cos\theta$, $v=\sin\theta$. The objective becomes $\frac12\int_a^b\theta'^2dx$, so $\theta''=0$. This agrees with the multiplier equations: $\theta$ is affine, so $\theta'$ is constant and $\lambda=-\theta'^2$ is constant. Thus, stationarity makes the multiplier constant in this particular example. Endpoint data can permit different integer winding choices; the stationarity equations alone do not choose the global minimum among all such classes.

## Limits and errors

The multiplier method needs a suitable regularity condition on the constraint derivative. A degenerate equality such as $G[y]=\int y^2dx=0$ has zero derivative at its only feasible function and does not meet the regular hypothesis. Also distinguish an exactly admissible family from a direction that satisfies only the linearized constraint, as in [[17.02 Admissible variations]]. Inequality constraints require additional sign and complementarity conditions and are not covered by the equality calculation here.

## Practice

1. Replace the integral condition in the worked example by $\int_0^1y\,dx=m$, where $m\in\mathbb R$. Find the minimizer and its value.
2. Minimize $\frac12\int_0^1(u'^2+v'^2)dx$ subject to $u+v=x$ pointwise, with $(u,v)(0)=(0,0)$ and $(u,v)(1)=(1/2,1/2)$. Derive equations with a multiplier function and solve them.

### Worked answers

1. The same integration gives $\lambda=-12m$, so $y_*=6mx(1-x)$. Its derivative is $6m(1-2x)$, and $J[y_*]=18m^2\int_0^1(1-2x)^2dx=6m^2$. The zero-integral perturbation proof works for every $m$.
2. Add $\lambda(x)(u+v-x)$ to the integrand. The equations are $u''=\lambda$, $v''=\lambda$, and $u+v=x$. Taking two derivatives of the constraint gives $u''+v''=0$, hence $2\lambda=0$. Both functions are affine, and the endpoint data give $u=v=x/2$. Expansion around this pair shows a nonnegative quadratic difference, so it is a minimizer.
