> [!TLDR]
> A restriction imposed before variation removes test directions, so stationarity of the restricted functional can omit an equation of the original problem.

## Restriction changes what can be tested

Suppose that $J[\mathbf y]$ depends on several functions. A substitution $\mathbf y=R[\mathbf q]$ gives a reduced functional $K[\mathbf q]=J[R[\mathbf q]]$. The chain rule gives

$$
\delta K[\mathbf q;\boldsymbol\xi]
=\delta J[R[\mathbf q];DR[\mathbf q]\boldsymbol\xi],
$$

where $DR[\mathbf q]\boldsymbol\xi$ is the initial change in the original functions caused by a change $\boldsymbol\xi$ in the reduced variables. Thus the reduced equation tests only directions in the range of $DR$. If this range is smaller than the original admissible direction space, some first-variation conditions are absent.

Every unrestricted stationary point that lies in the restricted class is stationary under the restricted directions. The converse need not hold. A restricted minimum can still decrease in an omitted direction. This is a statement about which problem is being solved, not an error in the chain rule.

## Worked example: one equation disappears

Let

$$
J[u,v]=\int_0^1\left(\frac12u'^2+\frac12v'^2+v\right)dx,
$$

with $u(0)=u(1)=v(0)=v(1)=0$. For independent zero-endpoint changes $\eta$ in $u$ and $\zeta$ in $v$,

$$
\delta J[u,v;\eta,\zeta]
=\int_0^1(u'\eta'+v'\zeta'+\zeta)dx
=\int_0^1(-u''\eta+(1-v'')\zeta)dx.
$$

Independence gives the two equations $u''=0$ and $v''=1$. Solving the endpoint problem yields

$$
u_*(x)=0,\qquad v_*(x)=\frac12x(x-1).
$$

Now substitute the restriction $v=0$ before taking the variation. The reduced functional is

$$
K[u]=J[u,0]=\frac12\int_0^1u'^2dx.
$$

Its only Euler–Lagrange equation is $u''=0$, so it has the minimum $u=0$. Lifted to the original variables, this gives $(u,v)=(0,0)$. It fails the original equation $v''=1$.

The failure is visible without solving any differential equation. At $(0,0)$ choose an omitted direction $\eta=0$ and $\zeta=-x(1-x)$. Then

$$
\delta J[0,0;0,\zeta]=\int_0^1\zeta\,dx=-\frac16.
$$

The corresponding exact change is

$$
J[0,\varepsilon\zeta]-J[0,0]
=-\frac{\varepsilon}{6}+\frac{\varepsilon^2}{6}<0
\quad(0<\varepsilon<1).
$$

The reduced minimizer is therefore not stationary in the original problem. The restriction removed the direction needed to detect this decrease.

## A different restriction can combine equations

In the same problem impose $u=v=q$ before variation. The reduced integrand is $q'^2+q$, giving $1-2q''=0$. Its stationary solution with zero endpoint values is $q=x(x-1)/4$. This equation is the sum of the two original equations after substitution. The difference of those equations is lost because allowed changes obey $\eta=\zeta$. In fact, no function with $u=v$ can satisfy both $u''=0$ and $v''=1$.

The finite-dimensional analogue is $f(a,b)=a^2+(b-1)^2$. On $b=0$, the reduced derivative $d f(a,0)/da=2a$ vanishes at $a=0$. But the full gradient there is $(0,-2)$, so the point is not unrestricted stationary. The figure shows this distinction through level curves.

## Valid reductions and approximation

A substitution that parameterizes the entire constraint set can be valid for that constrained problem. For example, parameterizing a circle by an angle preserves its tangent directions locally. It still solves a constrained problem rather than the unrestricted ambient one. An invertible change of variables preserves all directions and therefore preserves stationarity.

A finite trial family, such as $y(x)=\sum_{k=1}^N a_k\phi_k(x)$, usually gives only $N$ equations. These require the residual to have zero integral against the selected basis functions, not against every test function. Such methods can be useful approximations, but an exact solution requires a separate argument. Always state the restriction, derive the reduced equations, and check the resulting candidate against the original first variation when an unrestricted solution is claimed.

## Practice

1. For $f(a,b)=a^2+b^2+2b$, find the minimum on $b=0$ and the unrestricted minimum. Check the full gradient at the restricted point.
2. In the worked functional, impose $u=0$ before variation and leave $v$ free with zero endpoint values. Does the reduced solution satisfy the full equations? Explain why this does not contradict the warning above.

### Worked answers

1. On $b=0$, $f=a^2$, so the restricted minimum is $(0,0)$. Completing the square gives $f=a^2+(b+1)^2-1$, with unrestricted minimum $(0,-1)$. The gradient at $(0,0)$ is $(0,2)$, so that restricted point is not unrestricted stationary.
2. The reduced equation is $v''=1$, giving $v=x(x-1)/2$. The omitted equation is $u''=0$, which the imposed $u=0$ happens to satisfy. Thus the lifted solution satisfies both full equations. A restriction can retain a correct solution; the point is that reduced stationarity alone does not guarantee the omitted equations. Here direct substitution verifies them.
