> [!TLDR]
> Integration by parts converts a vanishing first variation into the Euler–Lagrange differential equation for the candidate function.

## Setting and hypotheses

Consider the real functional

$$
J[y]=\int_a^b F(x,y(x),y'(x))\,dx,
\qquad y(a)=A,\quad y(b)=B.
$$

Assume that $F(x,z,p)$ has continuous derivatives through order two on the relevant region and that the candidate $y$ is $C^2$. These are convenient classical hypotheses. We vary $y$ through $y+\varepsilon\eta$, where $\eta\in C^1([a,b])$ and $\eta(a)=\eta(b)=0$. In this note, $F_z$ means the derivative with respect to the function-value argument and $F_p$ the derivative with respect to the derivative argument. After evaluation on $y$, both are functions of $x$.

The first variation is

$$
\delta J[y;\eta]=\int_a^b(F_z\eta+F_p\eta')\,dx.
$$

The difficulty is the derivative on the arbitrary test function. We transfer that derivative to the known coefficient with integration by parts:

$$
\int_a^bF_p\eta' dx
=[F_p\eta]_a^b-\int_a^b\frac{d}{dx}F_p\,\eta\,dx.
$$

The boundary bracket vanishes for the prescribed endpoint values. Therefore stationarity gives

$$
\int_a^b\left(F_z-\frac{d}{dx}F_p\right)\eta\,dx=0
$$

for every allowed $\eta$.

## Why the coefficient must vanish

The fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations states that if a continuous function $g$ satisfies $\int_a^b g\eta\,dx=0$ for every smooth compactly supported test function, then $g=0$ in $(a,b)$. To see the reason, suppose $g(x_0)>0$. Continuity gives a small interval on which $g>0$. Choose a nonnegative, nonzero smooth test function supported in that interval. Its product with $g$ has strictly positive integral, a contradiction. Apply the same argument to $-g$ to exclude negative values.

Here $g=F_z-dF_p/dx$ is continuous under the stated hypotheses. Thus, every classical stationary candidate satisfies the **Euler–Lagrange equation**

$$
F_z(x,y,y')-\frac{d}{dx}F_p(x,y,y')=0.
$$

The derivative $d/dx$ is a total derivative along the curve. In particular,

$$
\frac{dF_p}{dx}=F_{px}+F_{pz}y'+F_{pp}y''.
$$

Using a partial derivative with respect to $x$ alone would omit the dependence through $y$ and $y'$.

## Worked example: solve and verify

Minimize

$$
J[y]=\int_0^1\left(\frac12y'^2+y\right)dx,
\qquad y(0)=y(1)=0.
$$

Since $F_z=1$ and $F_p=y'$, the equation is $1-y''=0$. Integrating twice yields $y=x^2/2+C_1x+C_2$. The first endpoint gives $C_2=0$ and the second gives $C_1=-1/2$. Thus,

$$
y_*(x)=\frac12x(x-1).
$$

For any competitor $y_*+h$ with $h(0)=h(1)=0$, expand the functional:

$$
J[y_*+h]-J[y_*]
=\int_0^1(y_*'h'+h)dx+\frac12\int_0^1h'^2dx.
$$

The linear part equals $[y_*'h]_0^1+\int_0^1(1-y_*'')h\,dx=0$. Hence the difference is nonnegative. Equality forces $h'=0$ and the endpoint data force $h=0$. The candidate is the unique global minimizer in this class. Direct integration also gives $J[y_*]=1/24-1/12=-1/24$.

## Limits of the equation

The Euler–Lagrange equation is generally necessary, not sufficient, for a minimum. It can also describe maxima and saddle points. Some variational problems have minimizers that are not $C^2$; the integral stationarity statement then gives a weak equation, and extra regularity must be proved before using the classical form. Endpoint conditions are part of the problem, as explained in [[17.05 Boundary terms]]. See [[17.08 Second variation]] for tests beyond stationarity.

## Practice

1. Derive the equation for $J[y]=\int_0^1(y'^2+y^2)dx$ with fixed endpoint values.
2. Find the stationary curve for $J[y]=\int_0^1y'^2dx$ with $y(0)=2$ and $y(1)=5$, and show that it minimizes $J$.

### Worked answers

1. Here $F_z=2y$ and $F_p=2y'$. The equation $2y-2y''=0$ is $y''=y$. It follows from integration against every zero-endpoint test function, not from setting the original integrand to zero.
2. The equation is $-2y''=0$, so $y=C_1x+C_2$. The endpoint values give $y_*=2+3x$. For $y=y_*+h$, expansion gives $J[y]-J[y_*]=6[h]_0^1+\int_0^1h'^2dx=\int_0^1h'^2dx$. This is nonnegative and vanishes only for $h=0$.
