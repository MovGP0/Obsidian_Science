title: "First variation"

> [!TLDR]
> The first variation is the derivative of a functional along an admissible perturbation, evaluated at zero perturbation.

## Definition and notation

Let $J$ be a real functional on a class of real functions on $[a,b]$. Let $y$ be a candidate and let $\eta$ be an admissible direction, as defined in [[17.02 Admissible variations]]. The **first variation** of $J$ at $y$ in direction $\eta$ is

$$
\delta J[y;\eta]
=\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}J[y+\varepsilon\eta]\right|_{\varepsilon=0}
=\lim_{\varepsilon\to0}\frac{J[y+\varepsilon\eta]-J[y]}{\varepsilon},
$$

when the two-sided limit exists. It is a directional derivative in a function space. For the smooth integral functionals below it is linear in $\eta$. More general directional derivatives need not have this linearity. A Fréchet derivative makes a stronger, uniform approximation with respect to a specified norm; merely calculating each directional limit does not prove Fréchet differentiability.

If $y$ is a local minimum and both signs of a small perturbation are allowed, ordinary one-variable calculus gives $\delta J[y;\eta]=0$. A function with zero first variation in every admissible direction is **stationary**. Stationarity is necessary under these conditions, but it does not prove a minimum.

## Differentiate an integral functional

Consider

$$
J[y]=\int_a^b F(x,y(x),y'(x))\,dx.
$$

Use $z$ and $p$ to denote the second and third arguments of $F$. Assume that $F,F_z,F_p$ are continuous on a neighborhood of the values reached by $y+\varepsilon\eta$, and that $y,\eta\in C^1([a,b])$. On the compact interval, for a sufficiently small parameter range, these hypotheses justify differentiation under the integral. The chain rule gives

$$
\delta J[y;\eta]
=\int_a^b\left(F_z(x,y,y')\eta+F_p(x,y,y')\eta'\right)dx.
$$

Both terms are required because perturbing $y$ also perturbs its derivative: $(y+\varepsilon\eta)'=y'+\varepsilon\eta'$. The first variation is a number for each $\eta$, not yet a differential equation. Integration by parts turns it into the form used in [[17.04 Euler–Lagrange equations]].

## Worked example: a direction that lowers a functional

Let

$$
J[y]=\int_0^1\left(\frac12y'^2+xy\right)dx,
\qquad y(0)=0,\quad y(1)=1.
$$

Test $y=x^2$ with $\eta=x(1-x)$. Here $F_z=x$ and $F_p=y'$, so

$$
\delta J[x^2;\eta]=\int_0^1(2x\eta'+x\eta)\,dx.
$$

Integration by parts gives $\int_0^1 2x\eta'\,dx=[2x\eta]_0^1-2\int_0^1\eta\,dx$. The bracket vanishes because $\eta$ has zero endpoint values. Thus,

$$
\delta J[x^2;\eta]
=\int_0^1(x-2)x(1-x)\,dx
=\int_0^1(3x^2-x^3-2x)\,dx
=1-\frac14-1=-\frac14.
$$

The negative value means that sufficiently small positive $\varepsilon$ lowers $J$. An exact expansion checks this conclusion:

$$
J[x^2+\varepsilon x(1-x)]
=\frac{11}{12}-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}+\frac{\varepsilon^2}{6}.
$$

The constant term is $\int_0^1(2x^2+x^3)dx=11/12$. The quadratic term comes from $\frac12\varepsilon^2\int_0^1(1-2x)^2dx=\varepsilon^2/6$. For $0<\varepsilon<3/2$, the exact difference from $J[x^2]$ is negative. In particular, $x^2$ is not stationary.

## What the derivative does and does not say

Testing one direction with a nonzero derivative disproves stationarity. Testing one direction with a zero derivative proves very little: other directions can still give a nonzero result. Furthermore, the equation $\delta J=0$ means zero for every allowed $\eta$, not that the integrand of the original functional is zero. A one-sided admissible family at a boundary of the admissible set gives a derivative inequality instead of the equality above.

## Practice

1. Find the first variation of $J[y]=\int_0^1(y'^2+y^4)dx$ for fixed endpoint values.
2. For $K[y]=\int_0^1 y'^2dx$ with $y(0)=0$, $y(1)=1$, show that $y=x$ is stationary for every $C^1$ direction with zero endpoint values.

### Worked answers

1. Differentiate $(y'+\varepsilon\eta')^2$ and $(y+\varepsilon\eta)^4$ at zero. The result is $\delta J[y;\eta]=\int_0^1(2y'\eta'+4y^3\eta)dx$. Here $\eta(0)=\eta(1)=0$ expresses the endpoint restrictions; it does not make either term separately vanish.
2. Since $y'=1$, $\delta K[x;\eta]=2\int_0^1\eta' dx=2[\eta]_0^1=0$. This uses every allowed direction and therefore proves stationarity. The stronger minimum claim follows from $K[x+\eta]-K[x]=\int_0^1\eta'^2dx\geq0$.
