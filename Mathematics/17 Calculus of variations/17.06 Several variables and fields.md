> [!TLDR]
> Each independently varied dependent function gives an Euler–Lagrange equation; on a multidimensional domain, integration by parts uses divergence and boundary normal derivatives.

## Several dependent functions on an interval

Let $\mathbf y=(y_1,\ldots,y_m)$ be a vector of real $C^2$ functions on $[a,b]$. For

$$
J[\mathbf y]=\int_a^bF(x,\mathbf y,\mathbf y')dx,
$$

perturb each component as $y_i+\varepsilon\eta_i$. When all endpoint values are fixed, every $\eta_i$ has zero endpoint values. The chain rule and componentwise integration by parts give

$$
\delta J
=\sum_{i=1}^m\int_a^b\left(F_{y_i}-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y_i'}\right)\eta_i\,dx.
$$

The components of the test function are independent. Set all but one to zero and apply the fundamental lemma to the remaining component. This gives one equation for each $i$:

$$
F_{y_i}-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y_i'}=0,\qquad i=1,\ldots,m.
$$

If a constraint links the components, this independence can fail. [[17.07 Constraints]] explains how multiplier functions recover the constrained equations.

## Worked example: a coupled pair

Consider

$$
J[u,v]=\int_0^1\left(\frac12u'^2+\frac12v'^2+uv\right)dx,
$$

with $u(0)=v(0)=0$ and $u(1)=v(1)=1$. Independent changes in $u$ and $v$ give

$$
u''=v,\qquad v''=u.
$$

The coupling is easier to solve after defining $s=u+v$ and $d=u-v$. Adding the equations gives $s''=s$; subtracting gives $d''=-d$. Their boundary values are $s(0)=0$, $s(1)=2$, and $d(0)=d(1)=0$. Thus,

$$
s(x)=\frac{2\sinh x}{\sinh1}.
$$

For $d$, the general solution is $A\cos x+B\sin x$. The initial value gives $A=0$, and the final value gives $B\sin1=0$. Since $\sin1\ne0$, $B=0$. Therefore

$$
u(x)=v(x)=\frac{\sinh x}{\sinh1}.
$$

This calculation derives stationarity. It does not assume in advance that $u=v$; that equality follows from the independent equations and the boundary data.

## A scalar function on a domain

Now let $u:\Omega\to\mathbb R$ be a scalar field on a bounded domain $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^n$ with smooth boundary. Write $\mathbf x=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$, $\partial_i u=\partial u/\partial x_i$, and $\nabla u=(\partial_1u,\ldots,\partial_nu)$. For

$$
J[u]=\int_\Omega F(\mathbf x,u,\nabla u)\,d\mathbf x,
$$

assume enough smoothness for the divergence theorem. If $p_i$ denotes the gradient argument corresponding to $\partial_i u$, the first variation is

$$
\delta J=\int_\Omega\left(F_u\eta+\sum_{i=1}^nF_{p_i}\partial_i\eta\right)d\mathbf x.
$$

For each term use $F_{p_i}\partial_i\eta=\partial_i(F_{p_i}\eta)-(\partial_iF_{p_i})\eta$. The divergence theorem yields

$$
\delta J=\int_\Omega\left(F_u-\sum_i\partial_iF_{p_i}\right)\eta\,d\mathbf x
+\int_{\partial\Omega}\eta\sum_iF_{p_i}n_i\,dS,
$$

where $\mathbf n$ is the outward unit normal. Fixed boundary values give $\eta=0$ on the boundary. Arbitrary interior tests then give $F_u-\sum_i\partial_iF_{p_i}=0$. On a free part of the boundary, the natural condition is $\sum_iF_{p_i}n_i=0$.

## Worked field example

On the unit disk $\Omega=\{(x_1,x_2):x_1^2+x_2^2<1\}$, take

$$
J[u]=\int_\Omega\left(\frac12|\nabla u|^2-4u\right)d\mathbf x,
\qquad u|_{\partial\Omega}=0.
$$

Here $F_u=-4$ and $F_{p_i}=\partial_i u$, so $-4-\Delta u=0$, where $\Delta u=\partial_1^2u+\partial_2^2u$. The function $u_*=1-x_1^2-x_2^2$ has boundary value zero and Laplacian $-4$. For a zero-boundary change $h$, integration by parts makes the linear change $\int_\Omega(\nabla u_*\cdot\nabla h-4h)d\mathbf x$ zero. The remaining difference is $\frac12\int_\Omega|\nabla h|^2d\mathbf x\geq0$, proving the minimum.

## Practice

1. Derive both equations for $\int_0^1\frac12(u'^2+v'^2+(u-v)^2)dx$ with fixed endpoint values.
2. For $\int_\Omega(\frac12|\nabla u|^2+\frac12u^2-fu)d\mathbf x$, find the interior equation and the natural condition on a free boundary part.

### Worked answers

1. Differentiation with respect to $u$ gives $u-v$ and with respect to $v$ gives $v-u$. The derivative arguments give $u'$ and $v'$. Hence $u''=u-v$ and $v''=v-u$. The opposite signs of the coupling follow from differentiating the same square with respect to different components.
2. Here $F_u=u-f$ and the vector of gradient derivatives is $\nabla u$. Thus $-\Delta u+u=f$. On a free boundary part the coefficient of arbitrary $\eta$ is $\nabla u\cdot\mathbf n=\partial_nu$, so $\partial_nu=0$ there. On prescribed boundary parts, $\eta=0$ instead.
