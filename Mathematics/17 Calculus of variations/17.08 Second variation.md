> [!TLDR]
> The second variation measures quadratic change near a stationary function and can distinguish a minimum, a maximum, and a saddle point.

## From first-order to second-order change

Let $y+\varepsilon\eta$ be a two-sided admissible family for a real functional $J$. Define $j(\varepsilon)=J[y+\varepsilon\eta]$. If this function has two derivatives at zero, the **second variation** is

$$
\delta^2J[y;\eta]
=j''(0).
$$

Some texts put a factor $1/2$ into the definition. Here we do not: the Taylor expansion is

$$
J[y+\varepsilon\eta]
=J[y]+\varepsilon\delta J[y;\eta]
+\frac{\varepsilon^2}{2}\delta^2J[y;\eta]+o(\varepsilon^2).
$$

At a stationary $y$, the linear term vanishes. A local minimum must then have $\delta^2J[y;\eta]\geq0$ for every allowed direction. A negative direction disproves a minimum. Directions of both signs establish a saddle, provided the quadratic expansion applies along them.

For a smooth first-derivative integral $J[y]=\int_a^b F(x,y,y')dx$, differentiating twice under the integral gives

$$
\delta^2J[y;\eta]
=\int_a^b\left(F_{zz}\eta^2+2F_{zp}\eta\eta'
+F_{pp}\eta'^2\right)dx.
$$

The partial derivatives are evaluated along $y$. As before, $z$ and $p$ are the function-value and derivative arguments. Assume $F$ has continuous second derivatives on a neighborhood of the varied curves, with enough control to justify differentiation. The mixed term contains a factor $2$ because it arises twice in the chain rule.

## Worked example: a stationary saddle

On $[0,1]$, with $y(0)=y(1)=0$, consider

$$
J_c[y]=\int_0^1(y'^2-cy^2)dx,
$$

where $c$ is a real constant. The zero function is stationary because every term in the first variation is linear in $y$ or $y'$. The second variation there is

$$
\delta^2J_c[0;\eta]=2\int_0^1(\eta'^2-c\eta^2)dx.
$$

Use the admissible directions $\eta_n(x)=\sin(n\pi x)$ for positive integers $n$. Since the integrals of $\sin^2(n\pi x)$ and $\cos^2(n\pi x)$ on $[0,1]$ are both $1/2$,

$$
\delta^2J_c[0;\eta_n]=(n\pi)^2-c,
\qquad J_c[\varepsilon\eta_n]=\frac{\varepsilon^2}{2}((n\pi)^2-c).
$$

Choose $c=2\pi^2$. For $n=1$, the second variation is $-\pi^2$, and every nonzero sufficiently small multiple lowers $J_c$. For $n=2$, it is $2\pi^2$, and the corresponding multiples raise $J_c$. Both families approach zero in the $C^1$ norm as $\varepsilon\to0$. Thus zero is a saddle even though its first variation vanishes in every direction.

## A sufficient estimate and its limits

For $c=0$, $J_0[y]=\int_0^1y'^2dx\geq0$, with equality only at the zero function under these endpoint values. This proves a strict global minimum. One can also prove positivity for some $c>0$ without solving an eigenvalue problem. Since $\eta(0)=0$, Cauchy–Schwarz gives

$$
\eta(x)^2=\left(\int_0^x\eta'(t)dt\right)^2
\leq x\int_0^x\eta'(t)^2dt
\leq x\int_0^1\eta'(t)^2dt.
$$

Integrate in $x$ to obtain $\int_0^1\eta^2dx\leq\frac12\int_0^1\eta'^2dx$. Hence, for $0\leq c<2$,

$$
J_c[\eta]\geq(1-c/2)\int_0^1\eta'^2dx>0
$$

for nonzero zero-endpoint $\eta$. This bound is sufficient, but not sharp. Failure of this estimate when $c\geq2$ is not evidence of failure of a minimum.

For a general nonlinear functional, positivity in each individual direction does not by itself supply uniform control of the Taylor remainder over a function-space neighborhood. A sufficient minimum theorem needs a norm, a bound on the remainder, and a suitable positive lower bound for the quadratic form. Conversely, a zero second variation is inconclusive. For $H[y]=(\int_0^1y^2dx)^2$, both first and second variations vanish at zero, yet $H[y]>0$ for every nonzero continuous $y$.

## Practice

1. Classify zero for $J[y]=-\int_0^1y'^2dx$ with zero endpoint values.
2. Determine the second variation at zero of $H[y]=-(\int_0^1y^2dx)^2$ and decide whether zero is a minimum or maximum.

### Worked answers

1. The first variation at zero vanishes. The second variation is $-2\int_0^1\eta'^2dx<0$ for every nonzero allowed direction. More directly, $J[y]\leq0$ with equality only for the zero function. Thus zero is the unique global maximum.
2. Along $y=\varepsilon\eta$, $H[\varepsilon\eta]=-\varepsilon^4(\int_0^1\eta^2dx)^2$. Its second derivative at zero is zero. Nevertheless, $H[y]<0$ for every nonzero continuous $y$, so zero is a strict global maximum. The fourth-order term decides the sign; the second variation alone cannot do so.
