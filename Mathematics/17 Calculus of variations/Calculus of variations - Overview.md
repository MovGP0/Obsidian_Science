> [!TLDR]
> Derive stationarity equations for functionals while keeping admissible variations and boundary contributions explicit.

## Scope

A functional assigns a scalar to a complete function rather than to one input value. Calculus of variations asks how that scalar changes when the whole function is changed in an allowed way.

## Starting knowledge

Use integration by parts from [[Integral calculus - Overview]], chain rules from [[Multivariable calculus - Overview]], and differential-equation language from [[Ordinary differential equations - Overview]].

## Topics to cover

- Functions and functionals: distinguish a pointwise rule from a rule that depends on a whole curve or field.
- Admissible variations: perturb a function by a small parameter times a test function and state endpoint or boundary restrictions.
- First variation: differentiate the functional with respect to the perturbation parameter and evaluate it at zero.
- Euler–Lagrange equations: integrate by parts and use arbitrary interior test functions to obtain a differential equation.
- Boundary terms: distinguish fixed endpoint data, free endpoints, natural boundary conditions, and compactly supported variations.
- Several variables and fields: derive equations for several dependent functions and for functions on a multidimensional domain.
- Constraints: introduce multiplier functions where appropriate and distinguish a constrained problem from an unrestricted one.
- Second variation: distinguish stationarity from a minimum and use a simple second-variation test.
- Changing the problem: explain why substituting a restriction before variation can remove an equation obtained in the unrestricted calculation.

## Learning outcomes

After studying this topic, you should be able to:

- Derive the Euler–Lagrange equation for a simple functional and identify the discarded or retained boundary term.
- Repeat the calculation when an endpoint is free and obtain its additional boundary condition.
- Compare restricted and unrestricted variations and identify which independent condition is lost.
