title: "Admissible variations"

> [!TLDR]
> An admissible variation changes a function while preserving the restrictions of the variational problem.

## A curve through a space of functions

Suppose that a functional $J$ is defined on an admissible set $\mathcal A$. To test a candidate $y\in\mathcal A$, introduce a real parameter $\varepsilon$ and a direction $\eta$:

$$
y_\varepsilon(x)=y(x)+\varepsilon\eta(x).
$$

Here $\eta$ is a function of $x$, not a small number. The parameter $\varepsilon$ controls the size of the change. Usually we require $y_\varepsilon\in\mathcal A$ for all sufficiently small positive and negative $\varepsilon$. This turns a question about functions into an ordinary one-variable question about $j(\varepsilon)=J[y_\varepsilon]$.

For a problem involving first derivatives, one can initially work with $y,\eta\in C^1([a,b])$. Smooth test functions are convenient when integration by parts is needed. The correct regularity depends on the functional: a formula containing $y''$ requires more derivatives than one containing only $y$ and $y'$.

## How boundary restrictions determine the direction

If the endpoint values are fixed at $y(a)=A$ and $y(b)=B$, the varied function must obey the same values. Since

$$
y_\varepsilon(a)=A+\varepsilon\eta(a),
\qquad y_\varepsilon(b)=B+\varepsilon\eta(b),
$$

the conditions are $\eta(a)=\eta(b)=0$. The derivatives of $\eta$ need not vanish. If the problem fixes endpoint slopes as well, then $\eta'(a)=\eta'(b)=0$ must also hold. A free endpoint value imposes no zero-value restriction there. These distinctions lead to [[17.05 Boundary terms]].

A compactly supported test function $\eta\in C_c^\infty((a,b))$ is zero on a neighborhood of both endpoints. It therefore preserves fixed endpoint data, including derivatives. Such tests are useful for finding interior equations. They do not probe free boundary values.

## Worked example: an allowed family

Take $\mathcal A=\{y\in C^1([0,1]):y(0)=0,\ y(1)=1\}$, candidate $y(x)=x$, and direction $\eta(x)=x(1-x)$. The direction vanishes at both endpoints, so every member of $y_\varepsilon=x+\varepsilon x(1-x)$ is admissible. For $K[y]=\int_0^1 y'^2\,dx$,

$$
y_\varepsilon'=1+\varepsilon(1-2x).
$$

Expand the square before integration:

$$
K[y_\varepsilon]
=1+2\varepsilon\int_0^1(1-2x)\,dx
+\varepsilon^2\int_0^1(1-2x)^2\,dx
=1+\frac{\varepsilon^2}{3}.
$$

The linear term vanishes because its integral is zero. Both signs of $\varepsilon$ increase $K$ in this direction. This is evidence about one direction; a full minimum test must cover every admissible competitor or direction.

## Constraints beyond endpoint data

For an integral restriction $\int_a^b y\,dx=M$, the straight family stays admissible precisely when $\int_a^b\eta\,dx=0$. Pointwise restrictions need separate care. If $y(x)\geq0$ and $y(x_0)=0$, a two-sided straight variation requires $\eta(x_0)=0$. One-sided variations can instead lead to inequalities, so the usual stationarity equality need not apply.

For nonlinear constraints, a tangent direction need not give an exactly admissible straight family. On the constraint $\int_0^1y^2\,dx=1$, differentiation gives the necessary tangent condition $\int_0^1y\eta\,dx=0$. But

$$
\int_0^1(y+\varepsilon\eta)^2\,dx
=1+\varepsilon^2\int_0^1\eta^2\,dx
$$

under this condition. Except for $\eta=0$, the straight family leaves the constraint. A corrected family is $(y+\varepsilon\eta)/\|y+\varepsilon\eta\|_2$, where $\|f\|_2=(\int_0^1f^2\,dx)^{1/2}$. It lies on the constraint and has initial direction $\eta$. This distinction is central to [[17.07 Constraints]].

## Practice

1. A problem fixes $y(0)=y(1)=0$ and $y'(0)=y'(1)=0$. Does $\eta=x(1-x)$ preserve all four values? Give a nonzero polynomial direction that does.
2. Show that $\eta=x(1-x)(x-1/2)$ preserves both zero endpoint values and an integral constraint $\int_0^1y\,dx=M$.

### Worked answers

1. Although $\eta(0)=\eta(1)=0$, its derivative is $1-2x$, which is nonzero at both endpoints. Thus, it is not allowed. The polynomial $\eta=x^2(1-x)^2$ has a double zero at each endpoint; its value and first derivative therefore vanish at both.
2. The factors $x$ and $1-x$ give zero endpoint values. Also $\eta(1-x)=-\eta(x)$. Substitution $t=1-x$ in the integral gives $\int_0^1\eta\,dx=-\int_0^1\eta\,dx$, so the integral is zero. Hence $\int_0^1(y+\varepsilon\eta)\,dx=M$ for every $\varepsilon$.
