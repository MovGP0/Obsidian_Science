> [!TLDR]
> Signature counts positive and negative directions of a nondegenerate symmetric form; null vectors do not imply a kernel.

## Diagonal forms and sign counts

Let $V$ be a real vector space of dimension $n$, and let $g$ be a symmetric bilinear form. Its quadratic form is $Q(v)=g(v,v)$. A suitable basis puts its matrix into the form

$$
\operatorname{diag}(\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{p},
\underbrace{-1,\ldots,-1}_{q},
\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{r}),\qquad p+q+r=n.
$$

The numbers $p,q,r$ are the inertia of the form. Sylvester's law of inertia says that these counts do not depend on the basis. In this article the **signature** of a nondegenerate form means the ordered pair $(p,q)$, with the positive count first. Some texts instead use $p-q$, so always state the convention.

To understand existence of the diagonal form, choose an auxiliary basis and apply the real spectral theorem to its symmetric matrix. An orthogonal basis change gives diagonal real eigenvalues. For each nonzero eigenvalue $\lambda_i$, replace its eigenbasis vector $e_i$ by $e_i/\sqrt{|\lambda_i|}$. The new self-pairing is $\lambda_i/|\lambda_i|$. Each nonzero diagonal entry then becomes $1$ or $-1$. Zero eigenvalues remain zero. This auxiliary Euclidean calculation is a way to classify the form, not an extra part of the metric.

The sign counts are intrinsic. For instance, after normalization every subspace of dimension greater than $p$ intersects the subspace spanned by the negative and zero directions. Thus the form cannot be strictly positive on such a subspace. The positive coordinate span attains dimension $p$, so $p$ is the largest possible dimension of a positive-definite subspace. The same argument determines $q$.

## Definite, indefinite, and degenerate

A Riemannian metric has signature $(n,0)$. A nondegenerate indefinite metric has $p>0$ and $q>0$. A **Lorentzian metric** in dimension $n\ge2$ has one sign direction and $n-1$ of the other: $(n-1,1)$ or $(1,n-1)$, depending on convention. No interpretation outside mathematics is needed for this terminology.

Degeneracy means $r>0$, or equivalently $\det G=0$. Indefiniteness instead concerns both positive and negative values of $Q$. These properties are different. A nonzero vector is **null** when $Q(v)=0$. In an indefinite nondegenerate form, the positive and negative terms can cancel even though no information is lost by the linear map $v\mapsto g(v,\cdot)$.

## Worked example: complete the square

Consider

$$
Q(x,y)=x^2+4xy+y^2=(x+2y)^2-3y^2.
$$

Set $u=x+2y$ and $v=\sqrt3\,y$. This is invertible because $y=v/\sqrt3$ and $x=u-2v/\sqrt3$. In these variables, $Q=u^2-v^2$, so its signature is $(1,1)$. The original matrix is $G=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}$ with determinant $-3\ne0$.

To find null directions, use $u=\pm v$. In the original coordinates this gives $x=(-2\pm\sqrt3)y$. Every nonzero point on these lines is null. None is in the kernel because $G$ is invertible. This calculation also shows why counting positive diagonal entries in an arbitrary matrix can fail: both original diagonal entries are positive, but the form is indefinite.

For a smooth nondegenerate metric, the signature is locally constant. Eigenvalues vary continuously with the matrix entries, and a sign cannot change without an eigenvalue passing through zero. Hence the signature is constant on each connected component of the manifold.

## Practice

1. Classify $G=\operatorname{diag}(2,3,-4,-5)$ and $H=\operatorname{diag}(2,3,-4,0)$ by inertia. Which is Lorentzian?
2. Find the signature of $Q(x,y)=4x^2+4xy+y^2$, and find a nonzero kernel vector.

## Worked answers

1. $G$ has inertia $(2,2,0)$ and is nondegenerate indefinite, but not Lorentzian. $H$ has inertia $(2,1,1)$ and is degenerate; it is not a pseudo-Riemannian metric on a four-dimensional tangent space. A Lorentzian form must be nondegenerate and have one sign count equal to one.
2. Factor $Q=(2x+y)^2$. Thus the inertia is $(1,0,1)$ and there is no nondegenerate signature pair. Its matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}$. Multiplication by $(1,-2)^T$ gives zero, so that vector spans the kernel.
