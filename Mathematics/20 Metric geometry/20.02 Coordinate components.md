title: "Coordinate components of a metric"
---

> [!TLDR]
> A line element records a symmetric metric matrix; a cross-term coefficient contains both off-diagonal entries.

## The coordinate basis and its dual

Let $(x^1,\ldots,x^n)$ be coordinates on an open part of a smooth manifold. The tangent basis is $\partial_i=\partial/\partial x^i$, and the dual covectors $dx^i$ satisfy $dx^i(\partial_j)=\delta^i{}_j$. A covector is a linear real-valued function on a tangent space. A metric is a smooth symmetric nondegenerate bilinear form on that space.

Define $g_{ij}=g(\partial_i,\partial_j)$. The tensor expression is

$$
g=\sum_{i,j}g_{ij}\,dx^i\otimes dx^j.
$$

The tensor product acts by $(dx^i\otimes dx^j)(v,w)=dx^i(v)dx^j(w)$. Therefore, for vectors with components $v^i,w^j$,

$$
g(v,w)=\sum_{i,j}g_{ij}v^i w^j.
$$

The basis vectors usually do not have unit length. They need not be orthogonal. The entries of $G=(g_{ij})$ give their pairings, not just numbers to place beside formal differentials.

## What the line element means

The conventional expression

$$
ds^2=g_{ij}\,dx^i dx^j
$$

records the quadratic form associated with $g$. It is shorthand for evaluating the tensor on the same tangent displacement twice. It is not an equation for a finite distance between arbitrary points. In two coordinates $(x,y)$, symmetry gives

$$
ds^2=g_{xx}\,dx^2+2g_{xy}\,dx\,dy+g_{yy}\,dy^2.
$$

The factor $2$ comes from $g_{xy}\,dx\otimes dy$ and $g_{yx}\,dy\otimes dx$. Both contribute the same product when the two arguments are equal. If one instead writes a symmetrized product $dx\odot dy=(dx\otimes dy+dy\otimes dx)/2$, then the tensor cross term is $2g_{xy}dx\odot dy$. State that convention before using it.

Because the form is symmetric over the real numbers, the quadratic function $Q(v)=g(v,v)$ determines the complete pairing. Expanding $Q(v+w)$ and canceling the self-pairings gives the polarization identity

$$
g(v,w)=\frac{Q(v+w)-Q(v)-Q(w)}{2}.
$$

Thus no information about a real symmetric metric is lost by recording its line element.

## Worked example: read and use a line element

Consider

$$
ds^2=4\,dx^2+6\,dx\,dy+5\,dy^2.
$$

The diagonal entries are $4$ and $5$. Since $2g_{xy}=6$, both off-diagonal entries equal $3$:

$$
G=\begin{pmatrix}4&3\\3&5\end{pmatrix}.
$$

For $v=(1,2)$, calculate $Gv=(10,13)^T$. Hence $g(v,v)=1\cdot10+2\cdot13=36$. The line-element calculation gives the same result: $4(1)^2+6(1)(2)+5(2)^2=36$.

For $w=(2,-1)$, first $Gw=(5,1)^T$, so $g(v,w)=1\cdot5+2\cdot1=7$. To check polarization, $v+w=(3,1)$ has $Q(v+w)=36+18+5=59$, while $Q(w)=16-12+5=9$. Therefore $(59-36-9)/2=7$.

The matrix is positive definite: its first leading principal minor is $4>0$ and its determinant is $20-9=11>0$. One can also complete the square:

$$
4a^2+6ab+5b^2=4\left(a+\frac34 b\right)^2+\frac{11}{4}b^2.
$$

## Common errors

Do not put $6$ into both off-diagonal entries in this example. That would create a cross term of $12\,dx\,dy$ and a different form. Do not read $g_{xy}$ as the angle itself: it is a pairing, from which an angle requires normalization by lengths. Finally, components can depend on the point, so evaluate $G$ at the curve point before computing speed; see [[20.07 Curves]].

## Practice

1. Find the matrix of $ds^2=2\,du^2-8\,du\,dv+10\,dv^2$ and calculate the self-pairing of $(1,1)$.
2. If $Q(a,b)=a^2+4ab$, recover $g((1,0),(0,1))$ and decide whether the form is nondegenerate.

## Worked answers

1. The matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}2&-4\\-4&10\end{pmatrix}$. Its self-pairing at $(1,1)$ is $2-8+10=4$. The determinant is $20-16=4$, so it is nondegenerate; completing the square gives $2(a-2b)^2+2b^2$, which is positive definite.
2. The vectors have individual quadratic values one and zero: $Q(1,0)=1$, $Q(0,1)=0$, while $Q(1,1)=5$. Polarization gives $(5-1-0)/2=2$. The matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&0\end{pmatrix}$ with determinant $-4\ne0$. The missing $b^2$ term does not by itself imply degeneracy.
