title: "Metric examples and curvature limits"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Variable metric components can describe flat Euclidean geometry; curvature requires a coordinate-invariant calculation or argument.

## Components depend on the coordinate basis

A metric $g$ assigns a bilinear pairing to tangent vectors. In a chart its entries $g_{ij}=g(\partial_i,\partial_j)$ depend on the coordinate basis. The basis can change from point to point even when the geometry is Euclidean. Thus a variable coefficient is not evidence of curvature.

On the Euclidean plane in Cartesian coordinates, $ds^2=dx^2+dy^2$. Introduce polar coordinates on a region with $r>0$ and a single smooth angular branch:

$$
x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta.
$$

Differentiate each equation:

$$
dx=\cos\theta\,dr-r\sin\theta\,d\theta,
\qquad dy=\sin\theta\,dr+r\cos\theta\,d\theta.
$$

Squaring and adding yields a radial coefficient $\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1$. The two cross terms cancel. The angular coefficient is $r^2(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta)=r^2$. Hence

$$
ds^2=dr^2+r^2d\theta^2.
$$

The angular basis vector gets longer as $r$ increases. That fact explains the variable coefficient. The transformation is an explicit local isometry to a Euclidean region, so this metric is flat wherever these coordinates are valid.

## Worked example: compute curvature despite nonzero connection terms

For a Riemannian metric, the Levi-Civita connection has coordinate coefficients

$$
\Gamma^i{}_{jk}=\frac12g^{i\ell}
\left(\partial_jg_{\ell k}+\partial_kg_{\ell j}-\partial_\ell g_{jk}\right),
$$

where $g^{i\ell}$ are entries of the inverse metric. For $G=\operatorname{diag}(1,r^2)$, the only nonzero metric derivative is $\partial_rg_{\theta\theta}=2r$. Substitution gives

$$
\Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta}=-r,\qquad
\Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta}=\Gamma^\theta{}_{\theta r}=\frac1r.
$$

These coefficients are nonzero because the polar basis changes with position. To check curvature, use the convention

$$
R^i{}_{jkl}=\partial_k\Gamma^i{}_{lj}
-\partial_l\Gamma^i{}_{kj}
+\Gamma^i{}_{km}\Gamma^m{}_{lj}
-\Gamma^i{}_{lm}\Gamma^m{}_{kj}.
$$

This corresponds to $R(\partial_k,\partial_l)\partial_j$. The relevant component is

$$
R^r{}_{\theta r\theta}
=\partial_r(-r)-0+0-(-r)(1/r)
=-1+1=0.
$$

In two dimensions the curvature symmetries leave one independent component. Therefore this calculation also confirms zero curvature. Neither variable metric coefficients nor nonzero Christoffel symbols alone establish nonzero curvature.

## A useful family: when the same pattern is curved

Consider $ds^2=dr^2+f(r)^2d\theta^2$ on a region where $f$ is smooth and strictly positive. The same coefficient formula gives $\Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta}=-ff'$ and $\Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta}=f'/f$. Therefore

$$
R^r{}_{\theta r\theta}
=-(f'^2+ff'')+f'^2=-ff''.
$$

For the convention above, the Gaussian curvature is

$$
K=\frac{g_{rr}R^r{}_{\theta r\theta}}
{g_{rr}g_{\theta\theta}-g_{r\theta}^2}
=-\frac{f''}{f}.
$$

For $f(r)=r$ this gives zero. For $f(r)=\sin r$ on $0<r<\pi$, it gives $K=1$. For $f(r)=e^r$, it gives $K=-1$. These examples show why the derivatives must be combined into curvature rather than judged separately.

## Coordinate singularities and global limits

At $r=0$, the polar expression has zero determinant, but the Cartesian metric remains the identity. All angular values represent the same origin, so the polar map is not an invertible chart there. This is a coordinate singularity, not a degenerate Euclidean metric.

Flatness is a local statement. A flat cylinder and a plane can have different global topology and different global distance behavior even though both have zero curvature. An explicit Euclidean coordinate change proves local flatness only on its valid chart domain. It does not automatically give a single global chart.

## Practice

1. On the first quadrant, set $x=e^u$, $y=e^v$. Find the Euclidean metric in $(u,v)$ and explain its curvature without calculating Christoffel symbols.
2. Find the Gaussian curvature of $ds^2=dr^2+\cosh^2r\,d\theta^2$. Then find that of $ds^2=dr^2+(2r+3)^2d\theta^2$ on $r>0$.

## Worked answers

1. Since $dx=e^udu$ and $dy=e^vdv$, the metric is $e^{2u}du^2+e^{2v}dv^2$. The map $(u,v)\mapsto(e^u,e^v)$ is a diffeomorphism onto the first quadrant and was used to pull back the Euclidean metric. It is therefore a local isometry, and the curvature is zero despite both coefficients varying.
2. For $f=\cosh r$, $f''=\cosh r$, so $K=-1$. For $f=2r+3$, the function is positive on the stated domain and $f''=0$, so $K=0$. The linear angular scale factor does not create curvature.
