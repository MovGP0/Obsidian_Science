> [!TLDR]
> A metric is a smooth, nondegenerate symmetric bilinear pairing on each tangent space; positive definiteness gives a Riemannian metric.

## From one vector space to a manifold

Let $M$ be a smooth real manifold of dimension $n$. Its tangent space $T_pM$ at a point $p$ is an $n$-dimensional real vector space. A metric assigns a real number $g_p(v,w)$ to each pair $v,w\in T_pM$. The two vectors must belong to the same tangent space. Comparing vectors at different points requires additional structure.

For each fixed $p$, the map $g_p:T_pM\times T_pM\to\mathbb R$ must be bilinear and symmetric. Bilinearity means, for real numbers $a,b$,

$$
g_p(av_1+bv_2,w)=a g_p(v_1,w)+b g_p(v_2,w),
$$

with the same rule in the second argument. Symmetry means $g_p(v,w)=g_p(w,v)$. These properties allow a component calculation to determine the pairing of any two vectors from its values on a basis.

The essential invertibility condition is **nondegeneracy**:

$$
g_p(v,w)=0\text{ for every }w\in T_pM\quad\Longrightarrow\quad v=0.
$$

This condition does not say that $g_p(v,v)=0$ implies $v=0$. That stronger statement holds for positive-definite metrics. An indefinite nondegenerate form can have nonzero vectors with zero self-pairing.

## Smoothness and local components

In coordinates $(x^1,\ldots,x^n)$, write $\partial_i=\partial/\partial x^i$ and $g_{ij}(p)=g_p(\partial_i,\partial_j)$. The matrix $G=(g_{ij})$ is symmetric. The metric is smooth when every function $g_{ij}$ is smooth in each chart. Equivalently, $g$ is a smooth section of the symmetric covariant two-tensor bundle. That language records the same pointwise pairing and its smooth dependence on $p$.

For $v=v^i\partial_i$ and $w=w^j\partial_j$, bilinearity gives

$$
g_p(v,w)=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n g_{ij}(p)v^i w^j=v^T G(p)w.
$$

Here the repeated-index convention abbreviates these finite sums. Nondegeneracy is equivalent to $\det G(p)\ne0$. If the matrix were singular, a nonzero vector in its kernel would pair to zero with every vector.

A **Riemannian metric** also satisfies $g_p(v,v)>0$ whenever $v\ne0$. A **pseudo-Riemannian metric** requires symmetry and nondegeneracy but can have both positive and negative directions. Some authors use “pseudo-Riemannian” to include the positive-definite case; here “indefinite” explicitly means that both signs occur. Signature counts these signs; see [[20.05 Signature]].

## Worked example: a smooth metric with cross terms

On $\mathbb R^2$ with coordinates $(x,y)$, set

$$
G(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}1&x\\x&1+x^2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The entries are smooth and the matrix is symmetric. Its determinant is $1(1+x^2)-x^2=1$, so it is nondegenerate at every point. For a tangent vector with components $(a,b)$,

$$
g(v,v)=a^2+2xab+(1+x^2)b^2=(a+xb)^2+b^2.
$$

The last expression is positive unless $b=0$ and $a+xb=0$, which together imply $a=b=0$. Thus this is a Riemannian metric. At a point with $x=2$, choose $v=(1,-1)$ and $w=(0,1)$. First $G w=(2,5)^T$; then $g(v,w)=2-5=-3$. A positive-definite metric can give a negative pairing between two different vectors. Positivity concerns self-pairings only.

For comparison, $H=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$ is nondegenerate because its determinant is $-1$. The vector $z=(1,1)$ has $z^THz=0$, but $z^TH(1,0)=1$. Thus $z$ is null, not a kernel vector.

## Limits and common errors

A tensor metric and a distance function are different objects. A Riemannian metric defines lengths of curves, from which one can construct distance within a connected component. An indefinite tensor does not give an ordinary distance by taking the square root of every self-pairing. Also, smoothness alone does not prevent degeneracy: $\operatorname{diag}(1,x^2)$ is smooth but singular on $x=0$.

## Practice

1. Is $G=\operatorname{diag}(e^{2x},e^{-2x})$ a Riemannian metric on $\mathbb R^2$?
2. For $H=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, show that $(1,0)$ is null but is not in the kernel. Is $H$ positive definite?

## Worked answers

1. Both diagonal entries are smooth and strictly positive. For $(a,b)\ne(0,0)$, the self-pairing is $e^{2x}a^2+e^{-2x}b^2>0$. The determinant is $1$, so all conditions hold globally.
2. The self-pairing is zero, but its pairing with $(0,1)$ is $1$. Also, $H(1,0)^T=(0,1)^T\ne0$, and $\det H=-1$. The vectors $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$ have self-pairings $2$ and $-2$, so the metric is indefinite.
