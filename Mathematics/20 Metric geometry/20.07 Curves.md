---
title: "Metric lengths of curves"
---

> [!TLDR]
> A curve length is the integral of its metric speed; the local line element becomes a finite length only after evaluation and integration.

## A curve and its tangent

Let $(M,g)$ be a Riemannian manifold, so $g$ is a smooth positive-definite metric. Let $\gamma:[a,b]\to M$ be a piecewise continuously differentiable curve. In coordinates write $x^i(t)=x^i(\gamma(t))$. Its tangent vector is

$$
\dot\gamma(t)=\frac{dx^i}{dt}\partial_i\big|_{\gamma(t)}.
$$

The dot denotes a derivative with respect to the chosen parameter $t$. It does not assign any special meaning to the parameter. The metric speed is the nonnegative scalar

$$
\lVert\dot\gamma(t)\rVert_g
=\sqrt{g_{ij}(x(t))\frac{dx^i}{dt}\frac{dx^j}{dt}}.
$$

Evaluate the component functions at the point on the curve. A coordinate-dependent metric cannot be replaced by one fixed matrix throughout the integral.

## From a line element to a finite integral

The length is

$$
L_g(\gamma)=\int_a^b\lVert\dot\gamma(t)\rVert_g\,dt.
$$

The notation $ds^2=g_{ij}dx^idx^j$ describes the local quadratic form. Along the curve, $dx^i=\dot x^i dt$, so the formal expression becomes $ds^2=g_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^jdt^2$. The nonnegative length increment is $ds=\lVert\dot\gamma\rVert_g|dt|$. One must then integrate to get a finite length. Substituting finite coordinate differences directly into $ds^2$ generally gives neither curve length nor shortest distance.

A regular curve has $\dot\gamma(t)\ne0$, so its accumulated length $s(t)=\int_a^t\lVert\dot\gamma(\tau)\rVert_gd\tau$ increases strictly. Where the inverse parameter exists, differentiation shows $\lVert d\gamma/ds\rVert_g=1$. This is an arc-length parameter. Piecewise smooth curves can still have well-defined length when the derivative vanishes at isolated points.

## Why a change of parameter preserves length

Let $t=\phi(u)$ be a smooth increasing bijection from $[c,d]$ to $[a,b]$ with positive derivative. The chain rule gives $d(\gamma\circ\phi)/du=\dot\gamma(\phi(u))\phi'(u)$. The norm is therefore $\lVert\dot\gamma(\phi(u))\rVert_g\phi'(u)$. Changing variables in the integral recovers the original length. A decreasing bijection contributes $|\phi'|$ and also preserves length. A map that traverses the curve twice is not a one-to-one reparameterization and counts both traversals.

## Worked example: a spiral in polar coordinates

On the punctured Euclidean plane, polar coordinates $(r,\theta)$ have metric

$$
ds^2=dr^2+r^2d\theta^2.
$$

Consider $r(t)=e^t$, $\theta(t)=t$, for $0\le t\le\log2$. The radius stays positive, so the chart is valid along this curve after a suitable angular branch is chosen. Differentiate: $\dot r=e^t$ and $\dot\theta=1$. Then

$$
g(\dot\gamma,\dot\gamma)
=\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2
=e^{2t}+e^{2t}=2e^{2t}.
$$

Its speed is $\sqrt2 e^t$, and

$$
L=\sqrt2\int_0^{\log2}e^t\,dt
=\sqrt2(2-1)=\sqrt2.
$$

As a check, the Cartesian coordinates are $x=e^t\cos t$, $y=e^t\sin t$. Their derivatives are $e^t(\cos t-\sin t)$ and $e^t(\sin t+\cos t)$. Squaring and adding cancels the mixed terms and gives $2e^{2t}$ again. The result describes this spiral segment, not necessarily the shortest path between its endpoints.

## Distance and indefinite limitations

Within a connected Riemannian manifold, distance is the infimum of lengths of piecewise smooth curves joining two points. Finding one curve length gives an upper bound for that distance; it does not prove minimality.

For an indefinite metric, $g(\dot\gamma,\dot\gamma)$ can be negative or zero for a nonzero tangent. On a curve segment of fixed sign one can integrate $\sqrt{|g(\dot\gamma,\dot\gamma)|}$ as a sign-dependent length functional. It does not construct an ordinary distance on all pairs of points. A nonconstant null curve can have zero such integral.

## Practice

1. In the polar Euclidean metric, calculate the length of $r=3$, $\theta=t$, $0\le t\le\pi/2$.
2. On $\mathbb R$ with metric $ds^2=e^{2x}dx^2$, calculate the length of the curve $x(t)=t$ from $t=0$ to $t=\log3$. Then verify the result with the parameter $x=\log u$, $1\le u\le3$.

## Worked answers

1. The radial derivative is zero and the angular derivative is one. Thus the speed is $\sqrt{0+9}=3$, and $L=\int_0^{\pi/2}3dt=3\pi/2$.
2. The first parameter gives speed $e^t$ and length $\int_0^{\log3}e^tdt=2$. In the second parameter, $dx/du=1/u$ and $e^{2x}=u^2$. The squared speed is $u^2(1/u)^2=1$, so $L=\int_1^3du=2$. The different speed functions describe the same length.
