---
title: "Lengths and angles from a metric"
---

> [!TLDR]
> A positive-definite metric defines vector norms and ordinary angles; indefinite metrics require different sign and null-direction rules.

## Norms at one point

Fix a point $p$ on a Riemannian manifold. Its metric $g_p$ is a positive-definite symmetric bilinear form on the real tangent space $T_pM$. For a vector $v$, define

$$
\lVert v\rVert_g=\sqrt{g_p(v,v)}.
$$

Positive definiteness makes the square root real, makes the norm positive for nonzero vectors, and makes the norm zero only at $v=0$. Bilinearity gives $\lVert av\rVert_g=|a|\lVert v\rVert_g$ for a real scalar $a$.

The metric is evaluated at a single point throughout this calculation. Coordinate vectors based at different points do not automatically belong to the same vector space. A norm of a tangent vector is also different from the distance between two manifold points; the latter involves complete curves.

## Cauchy–Schwarz and angles

For a nonzero vector $w$, positivity applied to $v-tw$ gives

$$
0\le g(v-tw,v-tw)=g(v,v)-2t g(v,w)+t^2g(w,w).
$$

The right-hand side has its minimum at $t=g(v,w)/g(w,w)$. Substitution gives

$$
0\le g(v,v)-\frac{g(v,w)^2}{g(w,w)},
\qquad |g(v,w)|\le\lVert v\rVert_g\lVert w\rVert_g.
$$

This is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. It ensures that the normalized pairing lies in $[-1,1]$. Thus for nonzero $v,w$, their angle is the unique $\theta\in[0,\pi]$ with

$$
\cos\theta=\frac{g(v,w)}{\lVert v\rVert_g\lVert w\rVert_g}.
$$

Vectors are orthogonal if their pairing is zero. The same inequality proves the triangle inequality: expand $\lVert v+w\rVert_g^2$ and bound the cross term by $2\lVert v\rVert_g\lVert w\rVert_g$. Taking nonnegative square roots gives $\lVert v+w\rVert_g\le\lVert v\rVert_g+\lVert w\rVert_g$.

## Worked example: nonorthogonal coordinate directions

At a point, use the matrix

$$
G=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The quadratic form is $4a^2+2ab+b^2=3a^2+(a+b)^2$, so it is positive definite. Let $e_1=(1,0)$ and $e_2=(0,1)$. Their squared lengths are $4$ and $1$, and their pairing is $1$. Therefore

$$
\lVert e_1\rVert_g=2,\qquad\lVert e_2\rVert_g=1,
\qquad \cos\theta=\frac12,\qquad\theta=\frac\pi3.
$$

The coordinate directions meet at $60$ degrees according to this metric. The component dot product $e_1^Te_2=0$ would give the wrong answer because it silently replaces $G$ by the identity matrix.

To find a vector orthogonal to $e_1$, write $z=(a,b)$. Then $g(e_1,z)=4a+b=0$. Choosing $a=1$ gives $z=(1,-4)$. Its squared length is $4-8+16=12$. Hence $E_1=e_1/2$ and $E_2=z/(2\sqrt3)$ form an orthonormal basis: both have norm one and their pairing is zero.

## Why indefinite metrics need limits

For $h(v,w)=v_1w_1-v_2w_2$, the nonzero vector $(1,1)$ is null. Its self-pairing is zero, so it cannot be assigned an ordinary positive norm from $h(v,v)$. Another vector, $(0,1)$, has negative self-pairing, so its square root is not real.

Replacing the formula by $\sqrt{|h(v,v)|}$ still does not produce a norm: a nonzero null vector has value zero. The ordinary angle formula also fails. For $v=(1,0)$ and $w=(\cosh t,\sinh t)$ with $t\ne0$, both self-pairings are $1$, but $h(v,w)=\cosh t>1$. No real Euclidean angle has that cosine. Hyperbolic parameters can describe certain indefinite pairings, but their sign assumptions must be stated separately. See [[20.05 Signature]] for the algebra of null directions.

## Practice

1. For $G=\operatorname{diag}(9,4)$, find the angle between $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$.
2. For $h=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$, find the orthogonal complement of $n=(1,1)$. Explain why this cannot occur for a nonzero vector in a positive-definite space.

## Worked answers

1. Each squared length is $9+4=13$. Their pairing is $9-4=5$, so $\theta=\arccos(5/13)$. The unequal diagonal coefficients prevent the component-based right-angle conclusion.
2. The equation $h(n,(a,b))=a-b=0$ gives $b=a$, so $n^\perp=\operatorname{span}(n)$. The null vector belongs to its own orthogonal complement. In a positive-definite space, $g(n,n)>0$ for $n\ne0$, which excludes this possibility.
