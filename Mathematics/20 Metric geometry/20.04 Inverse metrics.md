---
title: "Inverse metrics and index changes"
---

> [!TLDR]
> A nondegenerate metric identifies vectors with covectors; its inverse reverses that map and raises indices.

## Two different spaces

At a point $p$ of an $n$-dimensional manifold, a tangent vector belongs to $T_pM$. A covector belongs to the dual space $T_p^*M$ and maps vectors linearly to real numbers. In a coordinate basis, write

$$
v=v^i\partial_i,\qquad \alpha=\alpha_i dx^i,
\qquad \alpha(v)=\alpha_i v^i.
$$

Upper and lower indices distinguish the transformation rules of the two types of components. They do not indicate powers. There is a natural evaluation pairing between covectors and vectors, but no preferred conversion between these spaces before a suitable bilinear form is chosen.

Let $g$ be a symmetric nondegenerate metric with matrix $G=(g_{ij})$. It defines the **lowering map**, also called the flat map,

$$
\flat:T_pM\longrightarrow T_p^*M,\qquad
v^\flat(w)=g(v,w).
$$

Its components follow from evaluating on $\partial_j$:

$$
(v^\flat)_j=g_{ij}v^i=g_{ji}v^i.
$$

We commonly write the lowered components as $v_j=g_{ji}v^i$. The object $v^\flat$ is a covector, even when the notation reuses the letter $v$.

## Why nondegeneracy is necessary

The kernel of $\flat$ consists of vectors that pair to zero with every vector. Nondegeneracy says that this kernel is zero. Since the domain and codomain have the same finite dimension, injectivity implies bijectivity. The inverse is the **raising map**, or sharp map.

Define the inverse metric components $g^{ij}$ by

$$
g^{ik}g_{kj}=\delta^i{}_j.
$$

Then a covector $\alpha$ raises to the vector

$$
\alpha^\sharp=\alpha^i\partial_i,\qquad
\alpha^i=g^{ij}\alpha_j.
$$

Raising after lowering returns the original vector because $g^{ij}g_{jk}v^k=\delta^i{}_k v^k=v^i$. The inverse matrix entries are not generally the reciprocals of the individual entries of $G$. One must invert the full matrix.

## Worked example with a nondiagonal metric

Take a constant metric on $\mathbb R^2$ with

$$
G=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
G^{-1}=\frac15\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The determinant is $2\cdot3-1=5$. Multiplying the displayed matrices gives the identity, so this inverse is valid. For $v=(1,2)$,

$$
v^\flat=4\,dx+7\,dy.
$$

Raising its components gives $(3\cdot4-7,-4+2\cdot7)/5=(1,2)$. For the independent covector $\alpha=2dx-dy$, raising gives

$$
\alpha^\sharp=\frac75\partial_x-\frac45\partial_y.
$$

The defining property is $g(\alpha^\sharp,w)=\alpha(w)$ for every $w$. Check it on $w=(3,1)$. Multiplication gives $G\alpha^\sharp=(2,-1)$, so $g(\alpha^\sharp,w)=2\cdot3-1=5$, exactly $\alpha(w)$.

## Contractions and the inverse pairing

The metric also gives a pairing of covectors: $g^{-1}(\alpha,\beta)=g^{ij}\alpha_i\beta_j$. In particular,

$$
g(v,w)=v_i w^i=v^i w_i,
\qquad g^{-1}(v^\flat,w^\flat)=g(v,w).
$$

A repeated lower index alone is not a valid coordinate-invariant contraction. For example, $v_iw_i$ uses a Euclidean-looking summation with no reason to be invariant under a general coordinate change. Each contracted pair must include one upper and one lower index, or an explicit inverse metric must supply the missing conversion.

If $G=\operatorname{diag}(1,0)$, vectors $(a,b)$ all lower to $a\,dx$. Information about $b$ is lost. Thus the inverse map cannot exist. In contrast, the indefinite matrix $\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$ is invertible and permits index changes without difficulty. Zero self-pairing is not the obstruction; a nontrivial kernel is.

## Practice

1. With $G=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$, lower $v=(2,3)$ and verify $g(v,v)$ by contraction.
2. For $G=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&5\end{pmatrix}$, raise $\alpha=3dx+4dy$ and check the result by lowering it again.

## Worked answers

1. The covector components are $(2,-3)$. Therefore $v_i v^i=2\cdot2+(-3)\cdot3=-5$. This equals $2^2-3^2$. A negative result is permitted for an indefinite metric.
2. The determinant is $1$, so $G^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}5&-2\\-2&1\end{pmatrix}$. Multiplication gives $\alpha^\sharp=(15-8,-6+4)=(7,-2)$. Lowering gives $(7-4,14-10)=(3,4)$, as required.
