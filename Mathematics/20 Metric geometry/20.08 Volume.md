title: "Metric volume and orientation"

> [!TLDR]
> The factor √|det G| gives an invariant volume density; an oriented volume form also records coordinate orientation.

## A determinant measures a local cell

Let $G=(g_{ij})$ be the component matrix of a smooth nondegenerate metric on an $n$-dimensional manifold. In a Riemannian tangent space, $G$ is the Gram matrix of the coordinate basis. The squared volume of its basis parallelepiped equals $\det G$. One way to see this is to choose an orthonormal frame, write the coordinate basis as the columns of a matrix $B$, and use $G=B^TB$. Then $\det G=(\det B)^2$, while the Euclidean volume of that cell is $|\det B|$.

The local **metric volume density** is

$$
d\mu_g=\sqrt{|\det G|}\,|dx^1\cdots dx^n|.
$$

The bars on the coordinate product denote a density: coordinate changes use the absolute Jacobian determinant. They are not an instruction to take separate absolute values of infinitesimal real numbers. A density can be integrated without choosing an orientation.

For a positive-definite metric, $\det G>0$. For a pseudo-Riemannian metric with $q$ negative directions, $\det G$ has sign $(-1)^q$. Its absolute value is therefore needed to produce a real positive density. This construction measures volume even though indefinite tangent-vector self-pairings do not define norms.

## Derive coordinate invariance

Suppose old coordinates $x$ are functions of new coordinates $y$, and let $J^i{}_a=\partial x^i/\partial y^a$. The component rule from [[20.03 Coordinate changes]] gives

$$
G_y=J^T G_xJ,\qquad
\det G_y=(\det J)^2\det G_x.
$$

Take absolute values and square roots:

$$
\sqrt{|\det G_y|}=|\det J|\sqrt{|\det G_x|}.
$$

This is exactly the factor required by the change-of-variables formula. Thus $\sqrt{|\det G_y|}\,|dy^1\cdots dy^n|$ represents the same density as $\sqrt{|\det G_x|}\,|dx^1\cdots dx^n|$.

## Orientation and differential forms

If the manifold has a chosen orientation, the metric also gives an $n$-form $\operatorname{vol}_g$. In every positively oriented chart,

$$
\operatorname{vol}_g=\sqrt{|\det G|}\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n.
$$

The wedge product is alternating, so swapping two coordinate covectors changes its sign. In a negatively oriented chart, the displayed positive coefficient must be replaced by its negative to describe the same chosen volume form. A positive square root in every possible chart would not define one global form. On a nonorientable manifold the metric still gives a global density, but it does not supply a global nonvanishing volume form.

## Worked example: polar area

For $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$ with $r>0$, the Euclidean metric has matrix $G=\operatorname{diag}(1,r^2)$. Its determinant is $r^2$, so the area density is $r\,|dr\,d\theta|$. This agrees with the polar coordinate Jacobian, whose determinant is $r$.

![[math-20-polar-cell.svg|**Fig. 20.2** A polar cell has radial side Δr and angular side approximately r Δθ. The coordinate tangent directions are orthogonal, but the angular basis vector has length r.]]

Integrate over the annular sector $1\le r\le2$, $0\le\theta\le\pi/3$:

$$
\operatorname{Area}=\int_0^{\pi/3}\int_1^2r\,dr\,d\theta
=\int_0^{\pi/3}\frac{4-1}{2}\,d\theta
=\frac\pi2.
$$

The exact cell area is $\tfrac12((r+\Delta r)^2-r^2)\Delta\theta=r\Delta r\Delta\theta+\tfrac12(\Delta r)^2\Delta\theta$. The leading term explains the density shown by the figure. The higher-order term does not survive in the area integral limit.

## Common errors

Do not integrate $\det G$ instead of its square root. Do not omit the absolute value for an indefinite metric. A zero determinant at $r=0$ in polar notation is not a singularity of the Euclidean metric: polar coordinates fail to be a chart there. Finally, orientation reversal changes the coordinate expression for a form, but a positive area density remains positive.

## Practice

1. On $\mathbb R^2$, let $G=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$. Find the metric area of the coordinate rectangle $0\le x\le2$, $0\le y\le3$.
2. For the indefinite metric $ds^2=du^2-4dv^2$, find the volume density. Under $a=u$, $b=-v$, state how the oriented form $2\,du\wedge dv$ is written.

## Worked answers

1. The determinant is $4-1=3$, so the density coefficient is $\sqrt3$. It is constant. The integral is $\sqrt3\int_0^2dx\int_0^3dy=6\sqrt3$.
2. The determinant is $-4$, so $d\mu_g=2|du\,dv|=2|da\,db|$. But $du=da$ and $dv=-db$, hence the same oriented form becomes $-2\,da\wedge db$. The negative sign records that the new chart reverses the selected orientation.
