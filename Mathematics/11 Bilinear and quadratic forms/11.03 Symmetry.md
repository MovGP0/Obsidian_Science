---
title: "Symmetry of bilinear forms"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Every real bilinear form has unique symmetric and antisymmetric parts; the quadratic form retains only the symmetric part.

## Exchange the inputs

Let $B:V\times V\to\mathbb R$ be bilinear. It is **symmetric** if $B(x,y)=B(y,x)$ and **antisymmetric**, or skew-symmetric, if $B(x,y)=-B(y,x)$. The terms refer to exchange of the complete inputs. They do not require any individual coordinate to change sign.

Define two new forms by

$$
B_{\mathrm s}(x,y)=\frac{B(x,y)+B(y,x)}2,\qquad
B_{\mathrm a}(x,y)=\frac{B(x,y)-B(y,x)}2.
$$

Each is bilinear because sums and scalar multiples preserve linearity. Exchanging $x$ and $y$ leaves the first expression unchanged and changes the sign of the second. Adding the formulas gives $B=B_{\mathrm s}+B_{\mathrm a}$.

The decomposition is unique. Indeed, a form that is both symmetric and antisymmetric satisfies $C(x,y)=-C(x,y)$, hence $2C(x,y)=0$, so $C=0$ over $\mathbb R$. Subtracting any two proposed decompositions produces exactly such a form.

## Matrix decomposition

If $B(x,y)=x^{\mathsf T}Ay$, then $B(y,x)=x^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}y$. Consequently,

$$
A=S+K,\qquad S=\frac{A+A^{\mathsf T}}2,\qquad K=\frac{A-A^{\mathsf T}}2.
$$

We have $S^{\mathsf T}=S$ and $K^{\mathsf T}=-K$. This decomposition concerns transpose, not inverse. It applies to square matrices because both inputs belong to the same coordinate space. As with polarization, division by two must be valid in the scalar field.

## Why the antisymmetric part disappears

An antisymmetric form satisfies $B_{\mathrm a}(x,x)=-B_{\mathrm a}(x,x)$, so $B_{\mathrm a}(x,x)=0$. In matrix terms, the scalar $r=x^{\mathsf T}Kx$ equals its transpose. Taking its transpose also gives

$$
r=r^{\mathsf T}=x^{\mathsf T}K^{\mathsf T}x=-x^{\mathsf T}Kx=-r.
$$

Thus $r=0$ and $x^{\mathsf T}Ax=x^{\mathsf T}Sx$. A quadratic form cannot detect $K$. Do not infer that $K$ itself must be zero: it can give nonzero values on different vectors. Over $\mathbb R$, an antisymmetric form is also called **alternating**, because its diagonal values vanish. In characteristic two, skew-symmetry and alternation require separate care.

## Worked example: separate two different effects

Take

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}2&5\\1&-3\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The average and half-difference with its transpose give

$$
S=\begin{pmatrix}2&3\\3&-3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
K=\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\-2&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

For $u=(1,2)$ and $v=(3,-1)$, calculate $Sv=(3,12)$ and $Kv=(-2,-6)$. Therefore $B_{\mathrm s}(u,v)=3+24=27$ and $B_{\mathrm a}(u,v)=-2-12=-14$. Their sum is $13$. Direct multiplication gives $Av=(1,6)$ and $u^{\mathsf T}Av=13$ as required.

On a repeated vector $x=(a,b)$, the antisymmetric contribution is $2ab-2ba=0$, and the remaining quadratic form is

$$
q(a,b)=2a^2+6ab-3b^2.
$$

The antisymmetric part has another mathematical meaning: $B_{\mathrm a}(u,v)=2(u_1v_2-u_2v_1)$ is twice the signed area of the parallelogram spanned by $u,v$. Reversing their order reverses that sign. Repeating the same vector gives a parallelogram of area zero. This is a useful interpretation of vanishing diagonal values without vanishing pairings.

The area interpretation also explains why a diagonal-only test cannot identify every bilinear form. To determine a general form, evaluate it on ordered pairs of basis vectors. For a symmetric form, the order does not matter, and diagonal values together with sums of basis vectors suffice through polarization. For an antisymmetric form, every repeated-input test gives zero, so distinct inputs are necessary.

## Practice

1. Decompose $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&4\\-2&0\end{pmatrix}$ and find its quadratic form.
2. If $C$ is symmetric and $C(x,x)=0$ for every real $x$, prove $C=0$. Does the conclusion hold without symmetry?

## Worked answers

1. The off-diagonal average is $(4-2)/2=1$, and the half-difference is $(4+2)/2=3$. Hence $S=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $K=\begin{pmatrix}0&3\\-3&0\end{pmatrix}$. The diagonal expression is $q(x_1,x_2)=x_1^2+2x_1x_2$.
2. Expand $0=C(x+y,x+y)=C(x,x)+2C(x,y)+C(y,y)=2C(x,y)$. Thus every pairing is zero. Without symmetry, $C(x,y)=x_1y_2-x_2y_1$ is a counterexample: $C(x,x)=0$ for all $x$ but $C((1,0),(0,1))=1$.
