---
title: "Basis changes for bilinear forms"
---

> [!TLDR]
> A bilinear form changes by congruence because both inputs change coordinates; a linear operator changes by similarity because its output also has coordinates.

## State the coordinate convention first

Let $E=(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$ be an old basis and $F=(f_1,\ldots,f_n)$ a new basis of a real vector space. Let $P$ be the matrix whose $j$th column is the old coordinate vector of $f_j$. It is invertible because the new vectors form a basis. If $x$ and $x'$ are the old and new coordinate columns of the same abstract vector, then

$$
x=Px',\qquad x'=P^{-1}x.
$$

This convention determines the transformation formulas. A formula with $P^{-1}$ in another position can be correct under the opposite definition of $P$, but the two conventions must not be mixed.

## Derive congruence

Suppose a bilinear form has old matrix $A$, so $B(u,v)=x^{\mathsf T}Ay$ for old coordinate columns $x,y$. Substitute $x=Px'$ and $y=Py'$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
B(u,v)&=(Px')^{\mathsf T}A(Py')\\
&=x'^{\mathsf T}(P^{\mathsf T}AP)y'.
\end{aligned}
$$

The new matrix is therefore

$$
A'=P^{\mathsf T}AP.
$$

This relation is called **congruence**. It transforms both argument slots. Its entries also follow from $A'_{ij}=B(f_i,f_j)$. For a form on two different spaces with changes $P$ and $Q$, the formula becomes $A'=P^{\mathsf T}AQ$.

Symmetry is preserved because $(A')^{\mathsf T}=P^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}P$. Rank is preserved under multiplication by invertible matrices. The determinant obeys $\det A'=(\det P)^2\det A$, so its numerical value generally changes, although vanishing and its real sign do not.

## Why an operator uses similarity

Let $T:V\to V$ be a linear operator with old matrix $M$. The old coordinates of its output are $Mx=MPx'$. Convert that output to new coordinates by multiplying by $P^{-1}$:

$$
[T(u)]_F=P^{-1}MPx',\qquad M'=P^{-1}MP.
$$

This is **similarity**. A form has a scalar output, so there is no output coordinate conversion. That is why its left factor is $P^{\mathsf T}$ instead. If $P$ is orthogonal, $P^{-1}=P^{\mathsf T}$, and the formulas coincide numerically. That special case does not make the two concepts identical.

## Worked example: change a nonorthogonal basis

Take

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The new basis is $f_1=e_1$, $f_2=e_1+e_2$. Calculate

$$
AP=\begin{pmatrix}2&3\\1&4\end{pmatrix},\qquad
A'=P^{\mathsf T}AP=\begin{pmatrix}2&3\\3&7\end{pmatrix}.
$$

For $x'=(1,2)$, the old coordinates are $x=Px'=(3,2)$. The old quadratic expression gives $2(3)^2+2(3)(2)+3(2)^2=42$. The new expression gives $2(1)^2+6(1)(2)+7(2)^2=42$. The scalar value is unchanged, although the coefficients change.

If we treat the same array $A$ as an operator matrix, similarity instead gives

$$
P^{-1}AP=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\1&4\end{pmatrix}.
$$

It is not symmetric, which is permitted for an operator matrix in a nonorthonormal basis. Congruence changes eigenvalues in general: $I$ becomes $\operatorname{diag}(4,1)$ under $P=\operatorname{diag}(2,1)$. Similarity preserves the characteristic polynomial. For real symmetric forms, the sign counts of [[11.07 Classification]] replace eigenvalues as the main congruence invariants.

## Practice

1. For $A=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$ and $P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$, find $A'$.
2. Let $A=I$ and $P=\operatorname{diag}(3,2)$. Compare the form transformation with the operator transformation.

## Worked answers

1. First $AP=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}$. Left multiplication by $P^{\mathsf T}$ gives $A'=\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\2&0\end{pmatrix}$. Each new basis vector is null, but their mutual pairing is $2$, so the matrix is still invertible.
2. Congruence gives $P^{\mathsf T}IP=\operatorname{diag}(9,4)$, since the new coordinate basis vectors have squared lengths $9$ and $4$. Similarity gives $P^{-1}IP=I$, since the identity operator sends every vector to itself in every basis.
