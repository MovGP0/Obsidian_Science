> [!TLDR]
> A real quadratic form has a unique symmetric matrix, and polarization recovers its symmetric bilinear form from diagonal values.

## From a polynomial to a matrix

A **quadratic form** on a real vector space is a function $q$ of the form $q(x)=B(x,x)$ for a bilinear form $B$. In coordinates it is a homogeneous polynomial of degree two: each term contains exactly two coordinate factors. Thus $q(tx)=t^2q(x)$ for every real $t$. An expression with linear or constant terms is a quadratic polynomial, but it is not a quadratic form.

If $q(x)=x^{\mathsf T}Ax$, replacing $A$ by its symmetric part leaves $q$ unchanged. We therefore use the unique symmetric matrix $S=S^{\mathsf T}$ that represents $q$ in a fixed basis. Expanding the product gives

$$
q(x)=\sum_i S_{ii}x_i^2+2\sum_{i<j}S_{ij}x_i x_j.
$$

A square coefficient goes directly on the matrix diagonal. A mixed coefficient is split equally between two symmetric entries. These factors of two are essential: matrix multiplication includes both $S_{ij}x_i x_j$ and $S_{ji}x_j x_i$.

## Recover the symmetric bilinear form

Define $b(x,y)=x^{\mathsf T}Sy$. Symmetry gives $b(x,y)=b(y,x)$. Expand the diagonal value at a sum:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q(x+y)&=b(x+y,x+y)\\
&=q(x)+2b(x,y)+q(y).
\end{aligned}
$$

Solving for the mixed term gives the **polarization identity**,

$$
b(x,y)=\frac{q(x+y)-q(x)-q(y)}{2}
=\frac{q(x+y)-q(x-y)}{4}.
$$

The second version follows by subtracting the two expansions. It proves uniqueness: every symmetric bilinear form with diagonal $q$ must have these same values. The argument uses division by two, so it applies over fields of characteristic different from two. Over a field of characteristic two, diagonal values do not determine a symmetric bilinear form by this formula. Here all further calculations use $\mathbb R$.

Homogeneity by itself is not a complete definition of a quadratic form. For example, a homogeneous degree-two function can have a non-polynomial dependence on direction. The bilinear origin, or an equivalent polynomial definition in finite dimensions, supplies the needed structure.

## Worked example: matrix and polarization

Consider

$$
q(x_1,x_2)=3x_1^2+4x_1x_2+2x_2^2.
$$

Its symmetric matrix and pairing are

$$
S=\begin{pmatrix}3&2\\2&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
b(x,y)=3x_1y_1+2x_1y_2+2x_2y_1+2x_2y_2.
$$

Take $u=(1,0)$ and $v=(1,-1)$. Directly, $b(u,v)=3-2=1$. Polarization gives the same result: $q(u)=3$, $q(v)=3-4+2=1$, and $q(u+v)=q(2,-1)=12-8+2=6$. Hence $(6-3-1)/2=1$.

Completing the square gives another useful expression,

$$
q(x_1,x_2)=3\left(x_1+\frac23x_2\right)^2+\frac23x_2^2.
$$

Both terms are nonnegative, and both vanish only at the zero vector. Therefore this form is positive definite. This conclusion follows from the entire expression, not merely from its positive diagonal entries. [[11.07 Classification]] develops this test more generally.

## Information that cannot be recovered

Polarization recovers the symmetric pairing, not an arbitrary original bilinear form. If $K^{\mathsf T}=-K$, then $x^{\mathsf T}Kx=0$ for all real $x$. Both $S$ and $S+K$ therefore produce the same $q$. See [[11.03 Symmetry]] for the calculation and its relation to oriented area.

## Practice

1. Find the symmetric matrix for $q(x,y,z)=x^2+6xy-4xz+5z^2$.
2. For $q(x,y)=x^2-2xy+3y^2$, use polarization to find $b((1,1),(2,0))$.

## Worked answers

1. The diagonal is $(1,0,5)$. Half the $xy$ coefficient is $3$, half the $xz$ coefficient is $-2$, and there is no $yz$ term. Thus $S=\begin{pmatrix}1&3&-2\\3&0&0\\-2&0&5\end{pmatrix}$. Expanding $[x\ y\ z]S[x\ y\ z]^{\mathsf T}$ recovers the stated expression.
2. Put $u=(1,1)$ and $v=(2,0)$. We have $q(u)=2$, $q(v)=4$, and $q(u+v)=q(3,1)=9-6+3=6$. Therefore $b(u,v)=(6-2-4)/2=0$. The vectors are orthogonal for this pairing even though their ordinary coordinate dot product is $2$.
