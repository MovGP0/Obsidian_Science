title: "Classification of real symmetric forms"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Real symmetric forms are classified under basis changes by their numbers of positive, negative, and zero squares.

## Diagonal form and inertia

Let $B(x,y)=x^{\mathsf T}Ay$ on $\mathbb R^n$, where $A=A^{\mathsf T}$. The real spectral theorem gives an orthogonal matrix $Q$ with $Q^{\mathsf T}AQ=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)$. In coordinates $x=Qz$,

$$
q(x)=B(x,x)=\lambda_1z_1^2+\cdots+\lambda_nz_n^2.
$$

For each nonzero $\lambda_i$, rescale the corresponding basis vector by $1/\sqrt{|\lambda_i|}$. This is an invertible basis change and makes its diagonal coefficient $+1$ or $-1$. Reorder the basis to obtain

$$
\operatorname{diag}(I_p,-I_m,0_r),\qquad p+m+r=n.
$$

The triple $(p,m,r)$ is the **inertia**. Some authors call $(p,m)$ the signature; others call the difference $p-m$ the signature. This article uses the ordered pair $(p,m)$ and states the nullity $r$ separately. The eigenvalues themselves depend on a general basis change, but these counts do not.

## Why the counts are invariant

In the displayed canonical coordinates, the first $p$ coordinate directions form a subspace on which $q$ is positive definite. No larger subspace has that property. To see this, let $U$ have dimension greater than $p$, and let $N$ be the span of the negative and zero coordinate directions, of dimension $n-p$. The dimension inequality gives

$$
\dim(U\cap N)\ge\dim U+\dim N-n>0.
$$

Thus $U$ contains a nonzero vector in $N$, where $q\le0$, so $q$ cannot be positive definite on $U$. Therefore $p$ is the largest possible dimension of a positive-definite subspace. This description does not depend on coordinates. The same argument applied to $-q$ proves invariance of $m$, and then $r=n-p-m$. This result is Sylvester's law of inertia.

## Degeneracy and sign classes

The **radical** is $\operatorname{rad}B=\{x:B(x,y)=0\text{ for all }y\}$. For a symmetric matrix it equals $\ker A$. The form is **nondegenerate** if this radical is zero, equivalently $A$ is invertible. In canonical coordinates the radical is the $r$-dimensional zero-coordinate subspace.

The form is positive definite when $p=n$, negative definite when $m=n$, and indefinite when $p,m>0$. It is positive semidefinite when $m=0$ and negative semidefinite when $p=0$; these definitions include definite forms. A nonzero vector with $q(x)=0$ need not belong to the radical. Indefinite nondegenerate forms have such vectors, as [[11.08 Indefinite forms]] shows.

## Worked example by completing squares

Consider

$$
q(x,y,z)=2x^2+4xy+y^2-2yz-z^2.
$$

Its symmetric matrix is $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&2&0\\2&1&-1\\0&-1&-1\end{pmatrix}$. Complete the square in $x$ first:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q&=2(x+y)^2-y^2-2yz-z^2\\
&=2(x+y)^2-(y+z)^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Set $u=x+y$, $v=y+z$, and $w=z$. This coordinate transformation is invertible: $z=w$, $y=v-w$, and $x=u-v+w$. Thus the diagonal form really uses a basis and has inertia $(1,1,1)$. Rescaling $u$ gives one positive square, one negative square, and one zero direction.

The radical solves $u=v=0$. In old coordinates it consists of $(t,-t,t)$. Indeed, multiplying $A(1,-1,1)^{\mathsf T}$ gives zero. By contrast, $q(1,0,\sqrt2)=0$, but this vector is not in the radical: its pairing with $(1,0,0)$ is $2$. These are two distinct reasons for a zero diagonal value.

## Practice

1. Classify $q(x,y)=x^2+4xy+3y^2$ and state whether it is degenerate.
2. Find the inertia and radical of $q(x,y,z)=(x-y)^2+z^2$.

## Worked answers

1. Completing the square gives $(x+2y)^2-y^2$. The change $(x,y)\mapsto(x+2y,y)$ is invertible, so the inertia is $(1,1,0)$ and signature $(1,1)$. Its matrix has determinant $3-4=-1\ne0$, so the form is nondegenerate despite its null vectors.
2. Use $u=x-y$, $v=z$, and $w=y$. These are invertible coordinates, and $q=u^2+v^2$. Thus the inertia is $(2,0,1)$, and the form is positive semidefinite and degenerate. Its radical has $u=v=0$, so it is $\{(t,t,0):t\in\mathbb R\}$.
