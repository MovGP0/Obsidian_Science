title: "Orthonormal bases and Gram–Schmidt"

> [!TLDR]
> Gram–Schmidt removes previously used orthogonal directions and normalizes each remaining vector in a positive-definite inner product.

## Orthogonal and normalized

Let $V$ be a real finite-dimensional inner product space. Vectors $e_1,\ldots,e_k$ are **orthonormal** if $\langle e_i,e_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}$, where $\delta_{ij}$ is one for $i=j$ and zero otherwise. They are automatically independent: pairing $\sum_j c_j e_j=0$ with $e_i$ gives $c_i=0$. If their number is $\dim V$, they form an orthonormal basis.

In such a basis, the coordinates of $x$ are $c_j=\langle x,e_j\rangle$, because pairing $x=\sum_jc_j e_j$ with $e_i$ removes every term except $c_i$. Consequently,

$$
x=\sum_j\langle x,e_j\rangle e_j,\qquad
\|x\|^2=\sum_j\langle x,e_j\rangle^2.
$$

The second identity is the finite-dimensional Parseval identity. It explains why orthonormal coordinates simplify lengths as well as projections.

## The construction

Start with independent vectors $v_1,\ldots,v_k$. Set $w_1=v_1$ and $e_1=w_1/\|w_1\|$. At step $j$, define

$$
w_j=v_j-\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\langle v_j,e_i\rangle e_i,\qquad
e_j=\frac{w_j}{\|w_j\|}.
$$

The sum is the orthogonal projection onto the preceding span. For $\ell<j$, take its pairing with $e_\ell$:

$$
\langle w_j,e_\ell\rangle
=\langle v_j,e_\ell\rangle-
\sum_{i<j}\langle v_j,e_i\rangle\delta_{i\ell}=0.
$$

Also, $w_j$ cannot be zero: that would express $v_j$ in the span of the previous vectors, contrary to independence. Positive definiteness then makes $\|w_j\|>0$, so normalization is valid. At each step the old and new partial spans agree, since each $w_j$ differs from $v_j$ by a combination of previous vectors. The final orthonormal list spans the same subspace as the original list.

## Worked example in three coordinates

Use the standard product and start with

$$
v_1=(1,1,0),\quad v_2=(1,0,1),\quad v_3=(0,1,1).
$$

First $e_1=(1,1,0)/\sqrt2$. Since $\langle v_2,e_1\rangle=1/\sqrt2$,

$$
w_2=(1,0,1)-\tfrac12(1,1,0)
=(\tfrac12,-\tfrac12,1),\qquad
e_2=\frac{(1,-1,2)}{\sqrt6}.
$$

For the third vector, the two projection coefficients are $\langle v_3,e_1\rangle=1/\sqrt2$ and $\langle v_3,e_2\rangle=1/\sqrt6$. Hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
w_3&=(0,1,1)-\tfrac12(1,1,0)-\tfrac16(1,-1,2)\\
&=(-\tfrac23,\tfrac23,\tfrac23),\\
e_3&=\frac{(-1,1,1)}{\sqrt3}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The numerators have squared lengths $2,6,3$. Their pairwise dot products are $1-1=0$, $-1+1=0$, and $-1-1+2=0$. This checks both orthogonality and normalization independently of the construction.

## Conditions and limits

The procedure uses the actual inner product, not necessarily the coordinate dot product. For a weighted product $x^{\mathsf T}Gy$, all coefficients and norms must use $G$. If an input list is dependent, a residual becomes zero in exact arithmetic; discard that redundant input rather than divide by zero.

For indefinite forms, independence alone does not prevent a nonzero residual from having zero self-pairing. Ordinary positive-definite Gram–Schmidt therefore does not apply. Numerical arithmetic adds another issue: nearly dependent inputs can produce very small residuals and lose accuracy. Modified Gram–Schmidt updates a residual after each projection, while Householder methods are often preferable for numerical matrix factorization. These are numerical refinements, not changes to the exact formulas above.

## Practice

1. Orthonormalize $v_1=(1,0)$ and $v_2=(1,2)$ in the standard product.
2. Use $\langle x,y\rangle=2x_1y_1+x_2y_2$ to orthonormalize $(1,0),(1,1)$.

## Worked answers

1. The first vector already has unit length, so $e_1=(1,0)$. Subtract $\langle(1,2),e_1\rangle e_1=(1,0)$ to obtain $w_2=(0,2)$. Its norm is $2$, so $e_2=(0,1)$.
2. The first norm is $\sqrt2$, giving $e_1=(1/\sqrt2,0)$. The next projection coefficient is $2/\sqrt2=\sqrt2$. Its projected vector is $(1,0)$, so the residual is $(0,1)$ with weighted norm one. Thus $e_2=(0,1)$. The coordinate matrix of this basis is not an ordinary orthogonal matrix, but its columns are orthonormal for the specified product.
