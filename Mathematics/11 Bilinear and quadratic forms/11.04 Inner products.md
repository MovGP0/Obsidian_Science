> [!TLDR]
> A positive-definite symmetric bilinear form defines lengths, angles, and unique orthogonal projections onto finite-dimensional subspaces.

## Positivity adds geometry

A real **inner product** on a real vector space $V$ is a symmetric bilinear form $\langle x,y\rangle$ that satisfies $\langle x,x\rangle>0$ for every $x\ne0$. Bilinearity then gives $\langle0,0\rangle=0$. The associated norm and distance are

$$
\|x\|=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle},\qquad d(x,y)=\|x-y\|.
$$

In coordinates, $\langle x,y\rangle=x^{\mathsf T}Gy$, where $G$ is real, symmetric, and positive definite. Ordinary Euclidean geometry uses $G=I$, but other positive-definite matrices give valid inner products. Orthogonality means $\langle x,y\rangle=0$ for this chosen product. It need not agree with the ordinary coordinate dot product.

For $y\ne0$, positivity applied to $x-ty$, with $t=\langle x,y\rangle/\|y\|^2$, gives

$$
0\le\left\|x-\frac{\langle x,y\rangle}{\|y\|^2}y\right\|^2
=\|x\|^2-\frac{\langle x,y\rangle^2}{\|y\|^2}.
$$

Rearranging proves the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $|\langle x,y\rangle|\le\|x\|\|y\|$. For nonzero vectors it makes the angle definition

$$
\cos\theta=\frac{\langle x,y\rangle}{\|x\|\|y\|},\qquad 0\le\theta\le\pi
$$

valid. It also proves the triangle inequality: expand $\|x+y\|^2$ and bound the mixed term by $2\|x\|\|y\|$.

## Orthogonal projection onto a line

For a nonzero $u$, seek a multiple $p=cu$ such that the residual $r=x-p$ is orthogonal to $u$. The equation $\langle x-cu,u\rangle=0$ gives

$$
p=\frac{\langle x,u\rangle}{\langle u,u\rangle}u.
$$

The denominator is positive, so this projection exists uniquely. For any other multiple $au$, the vectors $r$ and $p-au$ are orthogonal. Expansion therefore gives

$$
\|x-au\|^2=\|r\|^2+\|p-au\|^2\ge\|r\|^2.
$$

Thus the orthogonal projection is the unique nearest point on the line. The same argument works for any finite-dimensional subspace once its orthogonal projection has been constructed.

## Worked example: a weighted inner product

Use $G=\operatorname{diag}(2,1)$, so $\langle(a,b),(c,d)\rangle=2ac+bd$. Let $x=(2,0)$ and $u=(1,1)$. Then $\langle x,u\rangle=4$ and $\langle u,u\rangle=3$, so

$$
p=\left(\frac43,\frac43\right),\qquad r=x-p=\left(\frac23,-\frac43\right).
$$

Check orthogonality: $\langle r,u\rangle=2(2/3)-4/3=0$. The squared lengths are $\|x\|^2=8$, $\|p\|^2=16/3$, and $\|r\|^2=8/3$. They satisfy $8=16/3+8/3$, the Pythagorean identity for this product.

The ordinary dot product gives $r\cdot u=-2/3$, so a visual Euclidean perpendicularity test would give the wrong conclusion. Always state the inner product before calculating an angle or projection.

## Projection onto several directions

If $e_1,\ldots,e_k$ form an orthonormal basis of a subspace $W$, then

$$
P_Wx=\sum_{j=1}^k\langle x,e_j\rangle e_j.
$$

Pairing the residual with each $e_i$ gives zero, so it is orthogonal to every vector in $W$. [[11.05 Orthonormal bases]] constructs such bases. Positivity is essential: [[11.08 Indefinite forms]] contains nonzero vectors whose self-pairing is zero, for which the line formula can fail.

## Practice

1. In the standard product, project $x=(3,1)$ onto $u=(1,1)$ and find the distance to the line.
2. For $G=\operatorname{diag}(4,1)$, find the norm of $(1,2)$ and its angle with $(1,0)$.

## Worked answers

1. The coefficient is $(3+1)/(1+1)=2$. Thus $p=(2,2)$, $r=(1,-1)$, and the distance is $\sqrt{1+1}=\sqrt2$. Since $r\cdot u=0$, the nearest-point condition holds.
2. The squared norm is $4+4=8$, so the norm is $2\sqrt2$. The other vector has norm $2$ and the pairing is $4$. Therefore $\cos\theta=4/(4\sqrt2)=1/\sqrt2$, which gives $\theta=\pi/4$.
