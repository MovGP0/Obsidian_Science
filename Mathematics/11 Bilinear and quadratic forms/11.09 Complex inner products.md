title: "Complex inner products"

> [!TLDR]
> A complex inner product uses conjugation in one input so that its diagonal values are real and positive; complex bilinear forms have different behavior.

## Choose a convention

Let $V$ be a complex vector space. This article uses the convention that $\langle x,y\rangle$ is **conjugate-linear in the first input** and linear in the second:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle ax+bz,y\rangle&=\overline a\langle x,y\rangle+\overline b\langle z,y\rangle,\\
\langle x,ay+bz\rangle&=a\langle x,y\rangle+b\langle x,z\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$

Such a form is **sesquilinear**. Some texts reverse the two conventions; their projection and polarization formulas must change accordingly. A complex inner product also has **Hermitian symmetry**, $\langle y,x\rangle=\overline{\langle x,y\rangle}$, and positive definiteness, $\langle x,x\rangle>0$ for $x\ne0$.

Hermitian symmetry makes a diagonal value equal to its complex conjugate, hence real. The norm is $\|x\|=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle}$. In coordinates, the standard product is

$$
\langle x,y\rangle=x^*y=\sum_j\overline{x_j}y_j,
$$

where $x^*=\overline{x}^{\mathsf T}$ is the conjugate transpose. More generally $x^*Hy$ is an inner product when $H=H^*$ is Hermitian and $x^*Hx>0$ for nonzero $x$. A basis change $x=Px'$ gives $H'=P^*HP$.

## Why complex bilinearity is different

A complex bilinear form uses no conjugation, for example $b(x,y)=\sum_jx_jy_j$. It cannot be positive definite on a nonzero complex vector space. Bilinearity would give $b(ix,ix)=i^2b(x,x)=-b(x,x)$, so a positive diagonal value would become negative after multiplication by $i$.

For $z=(1,i)$, the bilinear diagonal is $b(z,z)=1+i^2=0$, although $z\ne0$. The standard inner product instead gives $\langle z,z\rangle=1+\overline i i=2$. Thus replacing the conjugate transpose by an ordinary transpose changes the structure, not merely the notation.

## Worked example: pairings and projection

Use the standard product on $\mathbb C^2$, with $u=(1,i)$ and $v=(i,1)$. Then

$$
\langle u,v\rangle=1\cdot i+(-i)\cdot1=0,\qquad
\|u\|^2=\|v\|^2=2.
$$

The vectors are orthogonal. In contrast, the bilinear pairing is $b(u,v)=i+i=2i$.

To project $x=(1,0)$ onto the complex line spanned by $u$, write $p=cu$ and impose $\langle u,x-p\rangle=0$. Linearity in the second input gives

$$
c=\frac{\langle u,x\rangle}{\langle u,u\rangle}=\frac12,
\quad p=(\tfrac12,\tfrac i2),\quad r=(\tfrac12,-\tfrac i2).
$$

Check $\langle u,r\rangle=1/2+(-i)(-i/2)=0$. The order of the numerator matters: it is $\langle u,x\rangle$ under the convention used here. For a complex coefficient the reversed pairing would conjugate the coefficient and generally give the wrong projection.

## Recovering a pairing from norms

Put $q(x)=\langle x,x\rangle$. Expanding $q(x+y)$ and $q(x-y)$ gives their difference as $4\operatorname{Re}\langle x,y\rangle$. Expanding with $iy$ gives

$$
q(x+iy)-q(x-iy)=-4\operatorname{Im}\langle x,y\rangle.
$$

Combining the real and imaginary parts yields the complex polarization identity for our convention:

$$
\langle x,y\rangle=\frac14\left(q(x+y)-q(x-y)-i q(x+iy)+i q(x-iy)\right).
$$

As a sign check, in $\mathbb C$ take $x=1,y=i$. The real difference is zero, while $q(1+i^2)-q(1-i^2)=0-4=-4$. The formula returns $i$, the correct product $\overline1 i$.

There is no ordinary real angle obtained directly from an arbitrary complex pairing. Its real part defines an inner product on the underlying real vector space, while $|\langle x,y\rangle|/(\|x\|\|y\|)$ gives a phase-independent comparison of complex lines. Cauchy–Schwarz uses the modulus: $|\langle x,y\rangle|\le\|x\|\|y\|$.

## Practice

1. Compute the standard pairing and norms for $x=(1+i,2)$ and $y=(i,-1)$.
2. Project $x=(i,0)$ onto the complex line spanned by $u=(1,i)$.

## Worked answers

1. Conjugate only the coordinates of the first input: $\langle x,y\rangle=(1-i)i+2(-1)=1+i-2=-1+i$. The squared norms are $|1+i|^2+4=6$ and $|i|^2+1=2$. The bound holds because $|-1+i|=\sqrt2\le\sqrt{12}$.
2. The coefficient is $\langle u,x\rangle/2=i/2$. Thus $p=(i/2,-1/2)$ and $r=(i/2,1/2)$. Checking $\langle u,r\rangle=i/2-i/2=0$ confirms the result. Reversing the numerator would give $-i/2$ and would not make the residual orthogonal.
