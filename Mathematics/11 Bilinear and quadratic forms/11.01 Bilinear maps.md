title: "Bilinear maps"

> [!TLDR]
> A bilinear map is linear in each input when the other input is fixed; its values on pairs of basis vectors determine the complete map.

## Two separate linearity conditions

Let $U,V,W$ be vector spaces over a field $F$. A map $B:U\times V\to W$ is **bilinear** if, for all permitted vectors and scalars,

$$
\begin{aligned}
B(au_1+bu_2,v)&=aB(u_1,v)+bB(u_2,v),\\
B(u,av_1+bv_2)&=aB(u,v_1)+bB(u,v_2).
\end{aligned}
$$

The first equation fixes $v$; the second fixes $u$. In particular, $B(0,v)=B(u,0)=0$. Applying both conditions gives $B(au,bv)=abB(u,v)$. A **bilinear form** is a scalar-valued bilinear map, usually $B:V\times V\to F$. The inputs need not have the same dimension for a general bilinear map.

Bilinearity does not mean that $B$ is linear as a function on the product vector space $U\times V$. For the real map $B(x,y)=xy$, we have $B(2x,2y)=4xy$, whereas linearity on the product would require $2xy$. This distinction explains why quadratic expressions appear when both inputs change together.

## Build a form from basis coefficients

Suppose $U$ has basis $e_1,\ldots,e_m$ and $V$ has basis $f_1,\ldots,f_n$. Write $u=\sum_i x_i e_i$ and $v=\sum_j y_j f_j$. Repeated use of linearity gives

$$
B(u,v)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_i y_j B(e_i,f_j).
$$

For a scalar-valued map, set $A_{ij}=B(e_i,f_j)$. With column coordinate vectors $x$ and $y$, the same expression is

$$
B(u,v)=x^{\mathsf T}Ay.
$$

Here $A$ has $m$ rows and $n$ columns. Conversely, any such matrix defines a bilinear map: matrix multiplication distributes over sums in either input. Thus the $mn$ basis values determine the form uniquely, and there are no further restrictions on those coefficients. For a $W$-valued map, each coefficient is a vector in $W$. Choosing a basis in $W$ gives one scalar coefficient array for each output coordinate.

## Worked example: a nonsymmetric pairing

On $\mathbb R^2$, take

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\3&4\end{pmatrix},\qquad
u=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
v=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\3\end{pmatrix}.
$$

First calculate $Av=(-5,9)^{\mathsf T}$. Then $B(u,v)=u^{\mathsf T}Av=-5+18=13$. In the opposite order, $Au=(0,11)^{\mathsf T}$ and $B(v,u)=33$. A bilinear form need not be symmetric.

We can also recover the calculation directly from the four basis values:

$$
B(u,v)=2(1)(-1)-(1)(3)+3(2)(-1)+4(2)(3)=13.
$$

Each term contains one coordinate from each input. A term such as $x_1^2y_2$ would fail linearity in the first input. A nonzero constant term would fail $B(0,v)=0$.

## What the diagonal remembers

Setting the inputs equal produces $q(x)=B(x,x)$, a [[11.02 Quadratic forms|quadratic form]]. The two off-diagonal terms combine, so this operation loses some information. In the example,

$$
q(x)=2x_1^2+2x_1x_2+4x_2^2.
$$

The separate values $A_{12}=-1$ and $A_{21}=3$ cannot be recovered from their sum alone. [[11.03 Symmetry]] explains precisely which information is lost.

## Practice

1. Let $B(x,y)=x_1y_2-2x_2y_1$ on $\mathbb R^2$. Find its matrix and compute $B((2,1),(3,-1))$.
2. Is $C(x,y)=x_1y_1+x_2+y_2$ bilinear? Give an explicit failed condition.

## Worked answers

1. The coefficient of $x_i y_j$ is the entry in row $i$, column $j$. Therefore $A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-2&0\end{pmatrix}$. The value is $2(-1)-2(1)(3)=-8$. The entries on the diagonal are zero because no terms $x_1y_1$ or $x_2y_2$ occur.
2. Set $x=(0,0)$ and $y=(0,1)$. Then $C(0,y)=1$, but linearity in the first input requires $C(0,y)=0$. One failed condition is enough; there is no need to test all scalar and vector combinations.
