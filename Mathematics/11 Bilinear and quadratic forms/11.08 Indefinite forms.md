---
title: "Indefinite forms and null vectors"
---

> [!TLDR]
> An indefinite form takes both signs and can give zero on nonzero vectors, so its diagonal values cannot serve as ordinary squared lengths.

## Three kinds of vector

A real symmetric bilinear form $B$ is **indefinite** if its quadratic form $q(x)=B(x,x)$ takes both positive and negative values. A nonzero vector $n$ is **null**, or isotropic, if $q(n)=0$. The zero vector also has diagonal value zero, but calling a vector null usually means that it is nonzero.

The simplest example is the nondegenerate form

$$
B((x,y),(a,b))=xa-yb,\qquad q(x,y)=x^2-y^2.
$$

The vectors $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ have diagonal values $+1$ and $-1$. Solving $q(x,y)=0$ gives $(x-y)(x+y)=0$, so the null set is the union of the lines $y=x$ and $y=-x$. The sign is positive in the regions $|x|>|y|$ and negative when $|y|>|x|$.

These level sets differ from the ellipses that describe a positive-definite form. A zero value marks a direction, not zero coordinates. The picture uses ordinary coordinate axes to display a different algebraic pairing.

## Null does not mean radical

The radical requires $B(n,v)=0$ for **every** $v$. Nullity only requires $B(n,n)=0$. For $n=(1,1)$, we have $q(n)=0$, but $B(n,(1,0))=1$. Therefore $n$ is not in the radical. In fact, $\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$ is invertible, so the entire form is nondegenerate.

Every indefinite real symmetric form has nonzero null vectors. From its canonical form, choose a positive direction $e_+$ with square $+1$ and a negative direction $e_-$ with square $-1$, orthogonal to each other. Then $q(e_++e_-)=1-1=0$. This does not imply that null vectors form a vector subspace. For example, $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$ are null, but their sum $(2,0)$ has square $4$.

## Why length and projection rules fail

The expression $\sqrt{q(x)}$ is not a real norm: it is not real for negative $q$ and vanishes for some nonzero vectors. Replacing it by $\sqrt{|q(x)|}$ does not fix this. It still vanishes at nonzero null vectors and can fail the triangle inequality. Take $u=(1,1)$ and $v=(1,-1)$: both proposed lengths are zero, while the proposed length of $u+v=(2,0)$ is $2$.

Cauchy–Schwarz also fails. For the same two null vectors, $B(u,v)=2$, even though $q(u)q(v)=0$. Orthogonality remains the algebraic condition $B(x,y)=0$, but self-orthogonality is now possible.

## Worked example: projection onto a null line

Let $n=(1,1)$ and try to decompose $x=cn+r$ with $B(r,n)=0$. Pairing with $n$ gives

$$
0=B(x-cn,n)=B(x,n)-cB(n,n)=B(x,n).
$$

For $x=(1,0)$, the right side is $1$, so no coefficient $c$ works. For $x=(2,2)$, the right side is zero for every $c$, so infinitely many coefficients work. The ordinary projection formula divides by $B(n,n)=0$ and has no unique result in either case.

For this form, the orthogonal complement of the null line $L=\operatorname{span}(n)$ is $L$ itself: $B((a,b),n)=a-b=0$ requires $a=b$. Hence $V$ is not the direct sum $L\oplus L^\perp$. For a non-null vector $u$, the algebraic projection $B(x,u)u/B(u,u)$ is well-defined, but it does not imply nearest-point geometry because no positive norm has been supplied.

## Practice

1. Find all null vectors for $q(x,y)=4x^2-y^2$ and determine whether its associated form is degenerate.
2. In $B(x,y)=x_1y_1+x_2y_2-x_3y_3$, show that $n=(1,0,1)$ is null and find $n^\perp$.

## Worked answers

1. Factor $q=(2x-y)(2x+y)$. The nonzero vectors on $y=2x$ or $y=-2x$ are null. The matrix is $\operatorname{diag}(4,-1)$ with determinant $-4$, so the radical is zero and the form is nondegenerate.
2. We have $B(n,n)=1-1=0$. For $v=(a,b,c)$, $B(v,n)=a-c$, so $n^\perp=\{(a,b,a):a,b\in\mathbb R\}$. This plane contains $n$ but is larger than its span. Its restricted quadratic form is $b^2$, so the restriction is degenerate even though the form on the full three-dimensional space is nondegenerate.
