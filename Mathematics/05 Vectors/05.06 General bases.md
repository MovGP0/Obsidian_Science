> [!TLDR]
> In a general basis, the Gram matrix records the lengths and angles of basis vectors and supplies the correct component dot product.

## Components do not specify basis geometry

Let $\mathcal B=(\mathbf b_1,\ldots,\mathbf b_n)$ be a basis of a real Euclidean vector space. Write two vectors as

$$
\mathbf u=\sum_i c_i\mathbf b_i,
\qquad
\mathbf v=\sum_j d_j\mathbf b_j.
$$

The numbers $c_i$ and $d_j$ say how many copies of each basis vector to add. They do not state the lengths of those basis vectors or the angles between them. Therefore the formula $\sum_i c_i d_i$ cannot in general give the geometric dot product.

For example, if $\mathbf b_1=(2,0)$ in standard coordinates, its components in $\mathcal B$ start with $(1,0,\ldots)$. Squaring and adding those components gives $1$, but its true squared Euclidean length is $4$. Even perpendicular basis directions need scale factors unless they are unit vectors.

## Derive the Gram matrix formula

Use the linearity of the dot product in each argument to expand the sums:

$$
\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v
=\sum_i\sum_j c_i d_j(\mathbf b_i\cdot\mathbf b_j).
$$

Define the **Gram matrix** $G$ by $G_{ij}=\mathbf b_i\cdot\mathbf b_j$. Its diagonal entries are squared basis lengths. Its off-diagonal entries describe the angles between different basis directions. With the component columns $c=[\mathbf u]_{\mathcal B}$ and $d=[\mathbf v]_{\mathcal B}$, the expansion becomes

$$
\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=c^{\mathsf T}Gd,
\qquad
\|\mathbf u\|^2=c^{\mathsf T}Gc.
$$

The matrix is symmetric because $\mathbf b_i\cdot\mathbf b_j=\mathbf b_j\cdot\mathbf b_i$. It is also positive definite: if $c\neq0$, independence of the basis implies $\sum_i c_i\mathbf b_i\neq\mathbf0$, so $c^{\mathsf T}Gc>0$. This ensures that the length formula gives a positive squared length for every nonzero vector.

If the basis is orthonormal, $G_{ii}=1$ and $G_{ij}=0$ for $i\neq j$. Then $G$ is the identity matrix, and the familiar component dot product follows. This is the precise hypothesis behind the simpler rule.

## Worked example: an oblique basis

Use the basis from [[05.03 Components and bases]]: $\mathbf b_1=(2,0)$ and $\mathbf b_2=(1,1)$. The required dot products are $4$, $2$, and $2$, giving

$$
G=\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\2&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Let $c=(1,2)^{\mathsf T}$ and $d=(2,-1)^{\mathsf T}$. First multiply

$$
Gd=\begin{pmatrix}4(2)+2(-1)\\2(2)+2(-1)\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}6\\2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Therefore $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=c^{\mathsf T}Gd=1(6)+2(2)=10$. Direct reconstruction checks the result: $\mathbf u=(4,2)$ and $\mathbf v=(3,-1)$, whose standard dot product is $12-2=10$. Multiplying only the basis components would give $1(2)+2(-1)=0$, which would falsely indicate perpendicularity.

For $c=(1,2)^{\mathsf T}$, the length formula gives $4+2(2)(1)(2)+2(2^2)=20$. Thus $\|\mathbf u\|=\sqrt{20}$, which agrees with $\sqrt{4^2+2^2}$.

## Use the same correction for angles and projections

In these components, the cosine of the angle is

$$
\cos\theta=\frac{c^{\mathsf T}Gd}{\sqrt{c^{\mathsf T}Gc}\sqrt{d^{\mathsf T}Gd}}.
$$

Both vectors must be nonzero. To project the vector with components $c$ onto the nonzero direction with components $d$, the projection components are $(c^{\mathsf T}Gd)/(d^{\mathsf T}Gd)$ times $d$. The geometry from [[05.05 Dot products]] is unchanged; only its component calculation changes.

## Practice

1. For $\mathbf b_1=(1,0)$ and $\mathbf b_2=(1,1)$, find $G$ and the length of the vector with basis components $(1,-1)$.
2. In that basis, find a nonzero component pair orthogonal to the vector whose components are $(1,0)$.

## Worked answers

1. The entries are $G_{11}=1$, $G_{12}=G_{21}=1$, and $G_{22}=2$. Thus $G=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$. For $c=(1,-1)^{\mathsf T}$, $c^{\mathsf T}Gc=1-2+2=1$. The length is $1$. Reconstruction gives $(1,0)-(1,1)=(0,-1)$, confirming the result.
2. Let $d=(r,s)^{\mathsf T}$. Orthogonality requires $(1,0)Gd=r+s=0$. Choose $r=1$, $s=-1$. Its vector is $(0,-1)$, which is perpendicular to $\mathbf b_1=(1,0)$. Notice that its component pair is not perpendicular to $(1,0)$ under the naive component rule.
