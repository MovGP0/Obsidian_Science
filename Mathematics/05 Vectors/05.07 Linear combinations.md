> [!TLDR]
> A span contains all linear combinations of selected vectors; independence makes their coefficients unique.

## Combinations and span

A **linear combination** of vectors $\mathbf v_1,\ldots,\mathbf v_k$ is an expression

$$
a_1\mathbf v_1+\cdots+a_k\mathbf v_k,
\qquad a_1,\ldots,a_k\in\mathbb R.
$$

The numbers $a_i$ are coefficients. Each coefficient can be positive, negative, or zero. The **span** of the vectors is the set of every vector you can obtain in this way:

$$
\operatorname{span}(\mathbf v_1,\ldots,\mathbf v_k)
=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_i\mathbf v_i:a_i\in\mathbb R\right\}.
$$

A single nonzero vector spans a line through the origin. Two nonparallel vectors span a plane through the origin. Two parallel vectors still span only one line. The span always contains the zero vector because all coefficients can be zero. It is closed under addition and scalar multiplication: adding two combinations or scaling one only changes their coefficients.

## Dependence and independence

The vectors are **linearly independent** if the equation

$$
a_1\mathbf v_1+\cdots+a_k\mathbf v_k=\mathbf0
$$

forces every coefficient to be zero. They are **linearly dependent** if at least one nonzero coefficient choice gives zero. The word nonzero refers to the coefficient list; some entries in that list can still be zero.

Dependence means at least one vector can be made from the others. If $a_j\neq0$ in a zero combination, rearrange it:

$$
\mathbf v_j=-\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{a_i}{a_j}\mathbf v_i.
$$

Therefore removing that vector does not reduce the span. Any set containing the zero vector is dependent, since multiplying that vector by $1$ and the others by $0$ gives a nontrivial zero combination.

## Why independence gives unique decomposition

Suppose a vector has two representations along independent directions:

$$
\mathbf w=\sum_i a_i\mathbf v_i=\sum_i b_i\mathbf v_i.
$$

Subtracting gives $\sum_i(a_i-b_i)\mathbf v_i=\mathbf0$. Independence forces $a_i-b_i=0$ for every $i$. Thus the representations agree coefficient by coefficient. Conversely, dependence allows you to add a nontrivial zero combination to a representation and obtain a different coefficient list for the same vector.

A basis combines two requirements: its vectors span the space and are independent. Spanning ensures existence of a decomposition; independence ensures uniqueness. See [[05.03 Components and bases]] for the use of those coefficients as components.

## Worked example: decompose along chosen directions

Let $\mathbf a=(1,1,0)$ and $\mathbf b=(0,1,1)$. To determine whether $\mathbf w=(2,5,3)$ lies in their span, seek $s$ and $t$ such that

$$
s\mathbf a+t\mathbf b=(s,s+t,t)=(2,5,3).
$$

The first coordinate gives $s=2$; the third gives $t=3$. The middle coordinate then requires $2+3=5$, which holds. Thus $\mathbf w=2\mathbf a+3\mathbf b$.

For $\mathbf z=(2,6,3)$, the first and third coordinates still force $s=2$, $t=3$, but the middle coordinate would be $5$, not $6$. Therefore $\mathbf z$ is outside the span. In fact, the span is exactly the plane of vectors $(x,y,z)$ with $y=x+z$.

The pair is independent: a zero combination $(s,s+t,t)=(0,0,0)$ forces $s=0$ and $t=0$. Therefore the decomposition of $\mathbf w$ is unique. The larger set $\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf a+\mathbf b$ is dependent, because $\mathbf a+\mathbf b-(\mathbf a+\mathbf b)=\mathbf0$ uses coefficients $(1,1,-1)$.

## Common errors

The number of vectors alone does not determine their span. Check their relationships. In particular, nonparallel pairs are independent, but checking that every pair in a larger set is nonparallel is not enough. Three vectors in a plane can be pairwise nonparallel and still be dependent. Also, solving some component equations is insufficient: every component equation must hold.

## Practice

1. Are $(1,2)$ and $(3,6)$ independent? Find their span and two representations of $(6,12)$.
2. For $\mathbf a=(1,0,1)$ and $\mathbf b=(0,1,1)$, state a condition for $(x,y,z)$ to lie in their span, then test $(2,-1,1)$.

## Worked answers

1. Since $(3,6)=3(1,2)$, the combination $3(1,2)-(3,6)=\mathbf0$ proves dependence. Their span is $\{(t,2t):t\in\mathbb R\}$. Both $6(1,2)+0(3,6)$ and $0(1,2)+2(3,6)$ give $(6,12)$.
2. A combination is $s\mathbf a+t\mathbf b=(s,t,s+t)$. Thus the necessary and sufficient condition is $z=x+y$. For $(2,-1,1)$, $2+(-1)=1$, so it lies in the span and equals $2\mathbf a-\mathbf b$.
