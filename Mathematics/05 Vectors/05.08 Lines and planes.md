> [!TLDR]
> A line uses one direction vector and a plane uses two independent directions; intersections are the shared solutions of their equations.

## Vector equation of a line

Choose an origin so that points can be represented by position vectors. A line through a point with position vector $\mathbf p$ and nonzero direction $\mathbf d$ is

$$
\mathbf r=\mathbf p+t\mathbf d,\qquad t\in\mathbb R.
$$

Here $\mathbf r$ represents any point on the line. The parameter $t$ is a real number, not a component of $\mathbf r$. For $t=0$, the point is $\mathbf p$; increasing or decreasing $t$ moves along the two directions of the line. If $\mathbf d=\mathbf0$, the formula describes only one point, so that case is excluded.

For example, $\mathbf p=(1,2,0)$ and $\mathbf d=(2,-1,3)$ give the component equations $x=1+2t$, $y=2-t$, and $z=3t$. A segment from $P$ to $Q$ has the same form with $\mathbf d=\mathbf q-\mathbf p$ and $0\leq t\leq1$. Restricting the parameter changes the set of points.

## Vector equation of a plane

A plane through $\mathbf p$ with independent direction vectors $\mathbf a$ and $\mathbf b$ has equation

$$
\mathbf r=\mathbf p+s\mathbf a+t\mathbf b,
\qquad s,t\in\mathbb R.
$$

Independence is essential. Parallel directions would give only a line. The set $\operatorname{span}(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)$ gives the plane's directions, while $\mathbf p$ locates it. See [[05.07 Linear combinations]] for the difference between a span through the origin and its translated copy.

In three-dimensional Euclidean space, a nonzero **normal vector** $\mathbf n$ is perpendicular to both plane directions. Every point on the plane then satisfies

$$
\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf p)=0.
$$

For $\mathbf n=(A,B,C)$ this becomes $Ax+By+Cz=D$, with $D=\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf p$. For example, the plane through $(1,0,0)$ with directions $(1,1,0)$ and $(0,1,1)$ has normal $(1,-1,1)$, since its dot product with each direction is zero. Its equation is $x-y+z=1$.

## Worked example: line meets plane

Find the intersection of

$$
\mathbf r=(1,2,0)+t(2,-1,3)
\quad\text{and}\quad x+y+z=7.
$$

Substitute all three line coordinates into the plane equation:

$$
(1+2t)+(2-t)+3t=7
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad3+4t=7.
$$

Thus $t=1$, and substitution in the line gives the point $(3,1,3)$. Its coordinate sum is $7$, which checks membership in the plane. Its difference from $(1,2,0)$ is $(2,-1,3)$, which checks membership in the line.

More generally, a plane with equation $\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf q)=0$ and a line $\mathbf r=\mathbf p+t\mathbf d$ lead to

$$
t(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf d)=\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf q-\mathbf p).
$$

If $\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf d\neq0$, there is one intersection. If it is zero, the line direction is parallel to the plane. Then a zero right side means every line point is in the plane; a nonzero right side means there is no intersection. Do not divide by the dot product before checking this case.

## Other intersection types

For two lines, use separate parameters: $\mathbf p+s\mathbf a=\mathbf q+t\mathbf b$. Solve every component equation. In space, nonparallel lines can still fail to meet; these are **skew lines**. For two planes with independent normals, their two scalar equations leave one free parameter and describe a line. Parallel normals give either equal planes or disjoint parallel planes, as the constants determine.

## Practice

1. Find the intersection of $(x,y,z)=(0,1,2)+t(1,2,-1)$ with the plane $z=0$.
2. Describe the intersection of the planes $x+y+z=3$ and $x-y+z=1$ with a vector equation.

## Worked answers

1. The third coordinate is $2-t$. Set it equal to zero, giving $t=2$. The intersection is $(2,5,0)$. Substituting $t=2$ in all three coordinates confirms that this point lies on the line.
2. Subtract the second equation from the first to obtain $2y=2$, so $y=1$. Substitution gives $x+z=2$. Choose $z=t$; then $x=2-t$. The common line is $\mathbf r=(2,1,0)+t(-1,0,1)$. Each point has $x+y+z=3$ and $x-y+z=1$, so the parameter equation satisfies both planes.
