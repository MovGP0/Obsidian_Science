title: "Components and bases"

> [!TLDR]
> A basis gives each vector a unique list of components; changing the basis changes the list while the vector stays the same.

## Directions that specify every vector

In a plane, a **basis** is an ordered pair $\mathcal B=(\mathbf b_1,\mathbf b_2)$ of nonparallel vectors. Every vector in the plane can be written uniquely as

$$
\mathbf v=c_1\mathbf b_1+c_2\mathbf b_2.
$$

The numbers $c_1$ and $c_2$ are the **components of $\mathbf v$ in $\mathcal B$**. Write their ordered column as $[\mathbf v]_{\mathcal B}=(c_1,c_2)^{\mathsf T}$. The superscript $\mathsf T$ denotes a transpose: it converts the displayed row of entries into a column. The ordering of the basis is part of the definition.

A basis in three-dimensional space has three linearly independent vectors. Independence means that only zero coefficients give a zero combination. [[05.07 Linear combinations]] studies this condition. A two-dimensional basis need not have perpendicular directions or unit-length vectors.

The standard Cartesian basis is $\mathbf e_1=(1,0)$, $\mathbf e_2=(0,1)$. Here $(x,y)=x\mathbf e_1+y\mathbf e_2$. Components are especially simple in this basis, but the geometric vector exists independently of this choice.

## Reconstruct a vector from components

Suppose $\mathbf b_1=(2,0)$ and $\mathbf b_2=(1,1)$, expressed in standard coordinates. If $[\mathbf v]_{\mathcal B}=(1,2)^{\mathsf T}$, reconstruct the vector by multiplying each basis vector by its component and adding:

$$
\mathbf v=1(2,0)+2(1,1)=(2,0)+(2,2)=(4,2).
$$

The list $(1,2)$ is not the standard coordinate list $(4,2)$. Each list answers a different question about the same vector.

![[math-05-oblique-basis.svg|**Fig. 05.2** The vector v = (4, 2) has basis components (1, 2) along b₁ = (2, 0) and b₂ = (1, 1).]]

The dashed construction follows one copy of $\mathbf b_1$ and two copies of $\mathbf b_2$. Their combined displacement ends at the head of $\mathbf v$.

## Find components by solving equations

Given the vector instead, solve for its coefficients. For $\mathbf w=(7,3)$ in the same basis, write

$$
(7,3)=c_1(2,0)+c_2(1,1)=(2c_1+c_2,c_2).
$$

Equality of the second coordinates gives $c_2=3$. The first coordinate then gives $2c_1+3=7$, so $c_1=2$. Thus $[\mathbf w]_{\mathcal B}=(2,3)^{\mathsf T}$.

You can collect the basis vectors into the columns of a matrix $B$:

$$
B=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
[\mathbf v]_{\mathrm{standard}}=B[\mathbf v]_{\mathcal B}.
$$

Because the columns are independent, $B$ has an inverse. Multiplying by $B^{-1}$ finds the basis components. Solving the component equations, as above, is enough even before matrix methods are available.

## Change from one basis to another

Let another basis $\mathcal C=(\mathbf c_1,\mathbf c_2)$ have standard-coordinate matrix $C$. First reconstruct standard coordinates from $\mathcal B$ components. Then resolve the result in $\mathcal C$:

$$
[\mathbf v]_{\mathcal C}=C^{-1}B[\mathbf v]_{\mathcal B}.
$$

This formula is an instruction in two steps, not a change to the geometric vector. For example, take $\mathbf c_1=(1,1)$ and $\mathbf c_2=(-1,1)$. To express $(4,2)$, solve $d_1-d_2=4$ and $d_1+d_2=2$. Adding gives $2d_1=6$, so $d_1=3$ and $d_2=-1$. Hence $(4,2)=3\mathbf c_1-\mathbf c_2$.

Do not mix components from different bases in one addition. Also, squared components do not automatically give squared length in an oblique basis; see [[05.06 General bases]].

## Practice

1. In the basis $\mathbf b_1=(1,1)$, $\mathbf b_2=(1,-1)$, reconstruct the vector with components $(3,-2)$.
2. Find the components of $(5,1)$ in that same basis. Explain why the answer is unique.

## Worked answers

1. Multiply and add: $3(1,1)-2(1,-1)=(3,3)+(-2,2)=(1,5)$. The negative second component takes two steps opposite to $\mathbf b_2$.
2. Write $(5,1)=(a+b,a-b)$. Adding $a+b=5$ and $a-b=1$ gives $2a=6$, so $a=3$ and $b=2$. The basis vectors are nonparallel, so the homogeneous equations $a+b=0$, $a-b=0$ have only the zero solution. Two different decompositions would give a nonzero homogeneous solution after subtraction; therefore the components are unique.
