title: "Magnitude and unit vectors"
---

> [!TLDR]
> A norm measures vector length; dividing a nonzero vector by its norm gives a unit vector in the same direction.

## Euclidean length

The **magnitude** or **Euclidean norm** of a vector $\mathbf v=(v_1,\ldots,v_n)$ in an orthonormal basis is

$$
\|\mathbf v\|=\sqrt{v_1^2+\cdots+v_n^2}.
$$

Here $n$ is the number of components, and orthonormal means that the basis vectors are perpendicular and each has length one. In a plane, the formula follows from the Pythagorean theorem. In three dimensions, apply that theorem first to the first two components and then to the third. The square root is the nonnegative root, so a norm is never negative.

The distance between points $P$ and $Q$ is the norm of their displacement:

$$
d(P,Q)=\|\overrightarrow{PQ}\|=\|\mathbf q-\mathbf p\|.
$$

Thus length belongs to a vector, while distance compares two points. The calculation uses the same formula because their difference is a vector.

## What a norm must satisfy

More generally, a **norm** on a real vector space assigns a number $\|\mathbf v\|$ to each vector and satisfies three rules:

1. $\|\mathbf v\|\geq0$, with equality exactly when $\mathbf v=\mathbf0$.
2. $\|a\mathbf v\|=|a|\,\|\mathbf v\|$ for each real number $a$.
3. $\|\mathbf u+\mathbf v\|\leq\|\mathbf u\|+\|\mathbf v\|$.

The last rule is the **triangle inequality**: a direct side of a triangle is no longer than the path along the other two sides. The absolute value in the second rule is necessary because reversing direction cannot make a length negative.

Not every norm is the Euclidean norm. On $\mathbb R^2$, examples are $\|(x,y)\|_1=|x|+|y|$ and $\|(x,y)\|_\infty=\max(|x|,|y|)$. For $(3,4)$ these give $7$ and $4$, whereas the Euclidean norm gives $5$. Unless specified otherwise, length in this lesson means the Euclidean norm.

## Normalize a vector

A **unit vector** has norm one. For $\mathbf v\neq\mathbf0$, define its normalized vector by

$$
\widehat{\mathbf v}=\frac{\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf v\|}.
$$

The denominator is positive, so this operation preserves direction. By the scalar rule,

$$
\|\widehat{\mathbf v}\|
=\frac{1}{\|\mathbf v\|}\|\mathbf v\|=1.
$$

Therefore $\mathbf v=\|\mathbf v\|\widehat{\mathbf v}$ separates a vector into length and direction. There is no normalization of $\mathbf0$: division by zero is undefined, and the zero vector has no direction to preserve. A vector space consisting only of $\mathbf0$ contains no unit vectors.

## Worked example: direction and a specified length

Let $\mathbf v=(2,-3,6)$. Compute its squared norm before taking the root:

$$
\|\mathbf v\|^2=2^2+(-3)^2+6^2=4+9+36=49.
$$

Thus $\|\mathbf v\|=7$ and

$$
\widehat{\mathbf v}=\left(\frac27,-\frac37,\frac67\right).
$$

To find a vector of length $10$ in the same direction, multiply this unit vector by $10$. The result is $(20/7,-30/7,60/7)$. Its norm is $10\|\widehat{\mathbf v}\|=10$. The negative of that vector also has length $10$, but points in the opposite direction.

A common error is to divide by the sum of the components or by the squared norm. Neither operation generally produces norm one. Another is to use the sum-of-squares formula in a basis whose vectors are not orthonormal. [[05.06 General bases]] supplies the required correction for that case.

## Practice

1. Find the distance from $P=(-1,2)$ to $Q=(5,-6)$ and the unit vector directed from $P$ to $Q$.
2. Find both vectors of Euclidean length $3$ that are parallel to $(1,2,2)$. Why are there exactly two?

## Worked answers

1. The displacement is $(6,-8)$. Its norm is $\sqrt{36+64}=10$. Dividing gives the unit vector $(3/5,-4/5)$; its squared norm is $9/25+16/25=1$.
2. The original norm is $\sqrt{1+4+4}=3$, so $(1,2,2)$ already has the required length. Its negative $(-1,-2,-2)$ is the other answer. Any parallel vector has form $a(1,2,2)$ and norm $3|a|$. The equation $3|a|=3$ gives only $a=1$ and $a=-1$.
