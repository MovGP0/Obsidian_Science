title: "Exponential and logarithmic functions"
---

> [!TLDR]
> An exponential converts addition into multiplication, and its logarithmic inverse converts multiplication into addition.

## Bases and growth

For a fixed real base $a>0$ with $a\ne1$, the exponential function is $f(x)=a^x$ on $\mathbb R$. Its range is $(0,\infty)$, and $a^0=1$. For integer exponents, repeated multiplication gives

$$
a^{x+y}=a^xa^y,\qquad a^{-x}=\frac1{a^x}.
$$

The usual real exponential extends these laws from integer and rational exponents to all real inputs continuously. If $a>1$, the function is strictly increasing. If $0<a<1$, it is strictly decreasing. In either case it never equals zero. For $a>1$, the left end approaches zero and the right end is unbounded. For $0<a<1$, the directions reverse because $a^x=(1/a)^{-x}$.

An exponential changes by a constant **factor** over equal input steps. For $F(x)=Ca^x$ with $C>0$,

$$
\frac{F(x+h)}{F(x)}=\frac{Ca^{x+h}}{Ca^x}=a^h.
$$

This differs from a linear function, which changes by a constant difference. For instance, $3\cdot2^n$ gives the sequence $3,6,12,24$ for $n=0,1,2,3$. The differences are not constant, but each ratio is $2$.

## The logarithmic inverse

For $y>0$, define $\log_a y$ to be the unique real number $x$ such that $a^x=y$. Thus

$$
\log_a(a^x)=x\quad(x\in\mathbb R),
\qquad a^{\log_a y}=y\quad(y>0).
$$

The logarithm has domain $(0,\infty)$ and range $\mathbb R$. It is increasing for $a>1$ and decreasing for $0<a<1$. The number $e$ is the standard natural exponential base, and $\ln y$ means $\log_e y$. Exponential and logarithmic graphs for the same base reflect in the line $y=x$, as explained in [[03.07 Composition and inverse functions]].

For $u,v>0$ and real $t$, the laws are

$$
\log_a(uv)=\log_a u+\log_a v,\qquad
\log_a(u/v)=\log_a u-\log_a v,\qquad
\log_a(u^t)=t\log_a u.
$$

To justify the product law, put $p=\log_a u$ and $q=\log_a v$. Then $u=a^p$ and $v=a^q$, so $uv=a^{p+q}$. Applying the inverse gives $\log_a(uv)=p+q$. The quotient and power laws follow by the same exponent rules. There is no corresponding sum law: $\log_a(u+v)$ is generally not $\log_a u+\log_a v$.

## Changing the base

Let $a,b>0$, with neither equal to $1$. If $x=\log_a y$, then $a^x=y$. Taking base-$b$ logarithms gives $x\log_b a=\log_b y$. Since $\log_b a\ne0$,

$$
\log_a y=\frac{\log_b y}{\log_b a}.
$$

In particular, $\log_a y=\ln y/\ln a$ and $a^x=e^{x\ln a}$. Changing an exponential base changes the horizontal rate through the factor $\ln a$. If $a<1$, this factor is negative, which explains the reflection from growth to decay. Changing a logarithm base rescales its vertical coordinate by $1/\ln a$.

## Worked example with a domain check

Solve $\log_2(x-1)+\log_2(x+1)=3$. Both arguments must be positive, which requires $x>1$. The product law gives $\log_2(x^2-1)=3$. Apply the exponential inverse: $x^2-1=2^3=8$. Thus $x^2=9$, giving candidates $x=\pm3$. Only $3$ satisfies $x>1$. Substitution checks the answer: $\log_2 2+\log_2 4=1+2=3$.

Checking the original domain is essential. Combining logarithms can hide separate argument restrictions. Also, $\ln(x^2)=2\ln|x|$ for $x\ne0$; the expression $2\ln x$ only covers $x>0$.

## Practice

1. Solve $5^{2x-1}=12$ exactly in logarithmic form.
2. Solve $\ln x-\ln(x-2)=\ln3$ over the reals.

## Worked answers

1. Apply $\log_5$ to obtain $2x-1=\log_5 12$. Hence $x=(1+\log_5 12)/2=(1+\ln12/\ln5)/2$. The exponential is one-to-one, so this solution is unique.
2. The domain is $x>2$. The quotient law yields $\ln(x/(x-2))=\ln3$. Injectivity gives $x/(x-2)=3$, so $x=3x-6$ and $x=3$. This satisfies the domain, and the original left side is $\ln3-\ln1=\ln3$.
