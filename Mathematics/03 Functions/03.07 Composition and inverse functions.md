---
title: "Composition and inverse functions"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Composition applies functions in sequence, while an inverse reverses a one-to-one mapping with the correct domain and codomain.

## Composition and its domain

If $f:A\to B$ and $g:B\to C$, their **composition** is $g\circ f:A\to C$, defined by $(g\circ f)(x)=g(f(x))$. The rightmost function acts first. For formulas with natural domains $D_f$ and $D_g$, use

$$
D_{g\circ f}=\{x\in D_f:f(x)\in D_g\}.
$$

This condition checks both stages. It prevents the common error of simplifying a composite formula and admitting inputs for which an intermediate value was undefined. Composition is associative when the mappings have compatible domains and codomains: both $(h\circ g)\circ f$ and $h\circ(g\circ f)$ send $x$ to $h(g(f(x)))$. It is generally not commutative.

For example, let $f(x)=x+1$ and $g(x)=x^2$, both on $\mathbb R$. Then $(g\circ f)(x)=(x+1)^2$, but $(f\circ g)(x)=x^2+1$. At $x=1$, their values are $4$ and $2$. Thus composition order changes the function.

## One-to-one and onto mappings

A function is **one-to-one**, or injective, if $f(x_1)=f(x_2)$ implies $x_1=x_2$. No two different inputs share an output. On a graph, this is the horizontal-line test: each horizontal line meets the graph at most once. A strictly increasing or strictly decreasing function on an interval is injective, because two ordered distinct inputs must have ordered distinct outputs.

A mapping $f:A\to B$ is **onto**, or surjective, if every $y\in B$ occurs as $f(x)$ for some $x\in A$. A function that is both injective and surjective is bijective. It has an inverse $f^{-1}:B\to A$ characterized by

$$
f^{-1}(f(x))=x\quad(x\in A),\qquad
f(f^{-1}(y))=y\quad(y\in B).
$$

An injective function always has an inverse onto its actual range if that range is selected as the inverse domain. The notation $f^{-1}$ means an inverse function, not the reciprocal $1/f$. The graph of the inverse consists of pairs $(f(x),x)$, so it reflects the original graph in $y=x$.

## Worked example: restrict before inverting

The function $q(x)=(x-2)^2+1$ on $\mathbb R$ is not injective: $q(1)=q(3)=2$. Restrict the domain to $[2,\infty)$. Then $x-2\geq0$, and the function is strictly increasing. Its range is $[1,\infty)$, so use

$$
q:[2,\infty)\to[1,\infty).
$$

Solve $y=(x-2)^2+1$ for the allowed $x$. Subtracting $1$ gives $y-1=(x-2)^2$. Taking the nonnegative square root gives $x-2=\sqrt{y-1}$, because the domain requires $x-2\geq0$. Therefore

$$
q^{-1}(y)=2+\sqrt{y-1},\qquad y\geq1.
$$

Check both identities. For $x\geq2$,

$$
q^{-1}(q(x))=2+\sqrt{(x-2)^2}=2+|x-2|=x.
$$

For $y\geq1$, $q(q^{-1}(y))=(\sqrt{y-1})^2+1=y$. The absolute value in the first check explains why the restriction was necessary. Choosing the other branch, $x\leq2$, would instead give inverse $2-\sqrt{y-1}$.

## Worked domain check for composition

Let $f(x)=\sqrt x$ and $g(x)=1/(x-1)$. For $g\circ f$, require $x\geq0$ and $\sqrt x\ne1$. Thus its domain is $[0,1)\cup(1,\infty)$ and its formula is $1/(\sqrt x-1)$. For $f\circ g$, require $x\ne1$ and $1/(x-1)\geq0$. The numerator is positive, so this holds exactly when $x>1$. Its domain is $(1,\infty)$ and its formula is $\sqrt{1/(x-1)}$. The two orders differ in their allowed inputs as well as their values.

## Practice

1. Find the inverse of $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$, $f(x)=3x-5$, and check one composition.
2. Let $a(x)=x^2$ and $b(x)=\ln x$. Find both composite formulas and their natural composition domains.

## Worked answers

1. From $y=3x-5$, add $5$ and divide by $3$: $x=(y+5)/3$. The slope is nonzero, so $f$ is bijective. Hence $f^{-1}(y)=(y+5)/3$ for all real $y$. Directly, $f^{-1}(f(x))=(3x-5+5)/3=x$.
2. The composite $(b\circ a)(x)=\ln(x^2)$ requires $x^2>0$, so its domain is $\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}$. The composite $(a\circ b)(x)=(\ln x)^2$ requires $x>0$. Squaring after the logarithm does not remove the logarithm's domain restriction.
