> [!TLDR]
> Polynomial factors determine zeros and their multiplicities; denominator factors also determine exclusions, holes, and poles of rational functions.

## Polynomial structure

A real **polynomial** has the form $p(x)=a_nx^n+\cdots+a_1x+a_0$, where the coefficients $a_j$ are real, $n$ is a nonnegative integer, and $a_n\ne0$. Its degree is $n$. A constant nonzero polynomial has degree zero. The zero polynomial needs a separate degree convention. Every polynomial has natural real domain $\mathbb R$.

A number $a$ is a zero of **multiplicity** $m$ if

$$
p(x)=(x-a)^m u(x),\qquad u(a)\ne0,
$$

where $m$ is a positive integer. The remaining factor $u$ stays nonzero with the same sign sufficiently near $a$. An odd power of $x-a$ changes sign across $a$, so an odd-multiplicity zero crosses the axis. An even power keeps the same sign, so an even-multiplicity zero touches the axis without crossing it. This local argument is about signs; it does not say that every zero has the same slope.

For example, $p(x)=(x+1)^2(x-3)$ has a double zero at $-1$ and a simple zero at $3$. Away from $-1$, the squared factor is positive. Thus $p(x)<0$ for $x<3$, except at $-1$, where it is zero. It is positive for $x>3$.

## Rational functions and exclusions

A **rational function** has a quotient rule $r(x)=p(x)/q(x)$, where $p,q$ are polynomials and $q$ is not identically zero. Its natural domain excludes every real zero of the original denominator. Simplification can expose behaviour, but it does not restore excluded inputs.

Suppose a numerator has a factor $(x-a)^k$ and the denominator has $(x-a)^m$, with all remaining factors nonzero at $a$. If $k<m$, the uncancelled denominator gives a **pole** of order $m-k$. Values become unbounded near $a$, and $x=a$ is a vertical asymptote. If $k\geq m$, cancellation leaves a finite limiting value, but the original quotient remains undefined at $a$. Its graph has a removable hole. A pole of even order has the same infinite sign on both sides; an odd-order pole has opposite signs, after the sign of the remaining factors is included.

## Worked example: one zero, one hole, one pole

Consider

$$
r(x)=\frac{x^2-1}{x^2-x-2}
=\frac{(x-1)(x+1)}{(x-2)(x+1)}.
$$

The original denominator vanishes at $x=-1$ and $x=2$, so the domain is $\mathbb R\setminus\{-1,2\}$. For every allowed input,

$$
r(x)=\frac{x-1}{x-2}=1+\frac1{x-2}.
$$

The factor at $-1$ cancels. The finite limiting value there is $(-1-1)/(-1-2)=2/3$, so the graph has a hole at $(-1,2/3)$. The remaining denominator gives a simple pole at $2$. For $x<2$ close to $2$, $1/(x-2)$ is large and negative. For $x>2$ close to $2$, it is large and positive.

The numerator zero $x=1$ is allowed, so $(1,0)$ is an intercept. The cancelled value $-1$ is not a zero of this function because it is not an input. To find the sign, examine $(x-1)/(x-2)$. Both factors are negative for $x<1$, so the quotient is positive there, with $-1$ excluded. Between $1$ and $2$, it is negative. Above $2$, it is positive. As $x\to\pm\infty$, $1/(x-2)\to0$, giving the horizontal asymptote $y=1$.

## Practice

1. Find the real zeros, their multiplicities, and the sign of $p(x)=x^2(x-2)^3$.
2. Classify all excluded inputs of $s(x)=(x^2-4)/(x-2)^2$ as holes or poles. Find its zero and its horizontal asymptote.

## Worked answers

1. Zero is a double zero, and $2$ is a triple zero. For $x\ne0$, $x^2>0$, so the sign is the sign of $(x-2)^3$. Thus $p<0$ on $(-\infty,0)\cup(0,2)$ and $p>0$ on $(2,\infty)$. The graph does not change sign at zero, but it does at $2$.
2. The original denominator excludes only $2$. Factoring gives $s(x)=(x+2)/(x-2)=1+4/(x-2)$ for $x\ne2$. One denominator factor remains, so $2$ is a simple pole, not a hole. The zero is $-2$, which is allowed. The last expression shows $s(x)\to1$ at either infinite end, so the horizontal asymptote is $y=1$.
