title: "Checking solutions"

> [!TLDR]
> A candidate is a solution only if it lies in the original domain and satisfies the original statement.

## Candidates and actual solutions

Solving an equation produces candidate values. Verification tests those values against the original equation and its domain. A candidate outside that domain is invalid even if it satisfies a later, simplified equation. Work over the real numbers here. A square root denotes the principal nonnegative root, and a denominator must be nonzero.

There are two different logical relationships between steps. Equivalence, written $P\iff Q$, means that each statement implies the other. A one-way implication $P\implies Q$ means only that a solution of $P$ must satisfy $Q$. If a step is only one-way, its reverse can admit extra candidates. These are called extraneous roots.

Checking a candidate establishes that it works. It does not, by itself, prove that all solutions have been found. For completeness, explain why every original solution must appear among the candidates and then check each candidate. This combines a necessary-condition argument with direct verification.

## Worked radical equation

Solve

$$
\sqrt{x+2}=x.
$$

The root requires $x\ge-2$. The equation also requires $x\ge0$, because the left side is nonnegative. Squaring gives a necessary polynomial condition:

$$
x+2=x^2
\iff x^2-x-2=0
\iff(x-2)(x+1)=0.
$$

Thus the only possible candidates are $2$ and $-1$. Check them in the original equation:

$$
x=2:\quad\sqrt{2+2}=2,
\qquad
x=-1:\quad\sqrt{-1+2}=1\ne-1.
$$

Only $2$ is a solution. Squaring has lost the sign information that distinguished $1$ from $-1$. If the condition $x\ge0$ had been retained at every stage, squaring would have been reversible on that restricted domain.

This example also explains why $\sqrt{x^2}=|x|$. The square contains no record of the original sign. Choosing the positive square root cannot recover a negative input without an absolute value.

## Denominators and lost solutions

Consider the rational equation

$$
\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}=2.
$$

Its original domain is $x\ne1$. Factoring gives $x+1=2$ on that domain, so the only candidate is $x=1$. That input is excluded. Therefore the original equation has no solution. The cancellation is valid; forgetting the attached restriction would create the error. See [[02.03 Rational expressions]] for the domain rule behind this step.

The opposite problem is losing a valid solution by division. In $x(x-3)=0$, division by $x$ produces $x-3=0$ only under the additional assumption $x\ne0$. The original zero-product equation also has the solution $x=0$. Split the zero case before dividing, or use factorisation directly.

Similarly, multiplying an equation by an expression that can be zero can create candidates at its zeros. Such multiplication is an equivalence only where the multiplier is nonzero. Keep conditions beside the calculation rather than trying to reconstruct them at the end.

## Identity, conditional equation, and contradiction

An identity is true for every allowed input in its domain. For example, $2(x+1)=2x+2$ is an identity over the reals because distribution proves equality for arbitrary $x$. An equation such as $2x+1=7$ is conditional: it is true only at $x=3$. A contradiction such as $2x+1=2x+4$ has no solution, because subtraction would require $1=4$.

The statement $x/x=1$ is an identity on the restricted domain $x\ne0$. It is not a statement at $x=0$. Thus “every allowed input” is more precise than “every number.” Testing a few inputs can disprove an alleged identity if one test fails, but successful tests cannot prove it on an infinite domain. Use a general algebraic argument.

## A verification procedure

Record the domain, keep track of reversible and one-way steps, and obtain a finite candidate list or a stated family. Substitute each candidate into the original expressions. For a family, use its general parameter rather than a few samples. For inequalities, test signs on the relevant intervals and inspect endpoints separately. In simultaneous systems, verify every original equation.

Use exact values when possible. A rounded decimal can make unequal expressions appear close. If numerical work is unavoidable, state a tolerance and distinguish approximate agreement from an exact proof.

## Practice

1. Solve $\sqrt{x+6}=x$ over the reals, with a check of every candidate.
2. Classify $3(x-2)=3x-6$ and $3(x-2)=3x-5$. Then solve $x(x+4)=0$ without losing a solution.

## Worked answers

1. The equation requires $x\ge0$. Squaring gives $x^2-x-6=0=(x-3)(x+2)$, so the candidates are $3$ and $-2$. At $3$, $\sqrt9=3$; at $-2$, $\sqrt4=2\ne-2$. Therefore the solution set is $\{3\}$.
2. Expansion makes the first statement $3x-6=3x-6$, an identity for all real $x$. The second reduces to $-6=-5$, a contradiction with no solution. Finally, the zero-product rule gives $x=0$ or $x+4=0$, hence $x=0,-4$. Both make the original product zero.
