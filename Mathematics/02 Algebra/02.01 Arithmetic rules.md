> [!TLDR]
> Use the order of operations and number laws to calculate values without changing their meaning.

## Numbers, operations, and order

An arithmetic expression combines numbers with operations. Unless another set is stated, the numbers in this lesson are real. Addition and multiplication are commutative: $a+b=b+a$ and $ab=ba$. They are also associative: $(a+b)+c=a+(b+c)$ and $(ab)c=a(bc)$. Subtraction and division do not have these properties. For example, $8-3=5$, but $3-8=-5$.

Evaluate brackets first, then powers and roots, then multiplication and division, and finally addition and subtraction. Operations at the same level proceed from left to right. Thus $12/3\times2=8$, whereas $12/(3\times2)=2$. A fraction bar groups its entire numerator and denominator. Write brackets when a line of text could be read in two ways.

The minus sign in $-a$ denotes the additive inverse: $a+(-a)=0$. Subtraction means addition of an inverse, so $a-b=a+(-b)$. A power acts before a leading minus: $-3^2=-(3^2)=-9$, but $(-3)^2=9$. The brackets make the negative number the base of the power.

## Signs and fractions

The distributive law $a(b+c)=ab+ac$ connects addition and multiplication. It also explains the sign rules. Since $0=(-a)(b-b)=(-a)b+(-a)(-b)$ and $(-a)b=-ab$, the second product must equal $ab$. Thus a product of two negative numbers is positive. For nonzero divisors, division follows the same sign rules.

A fraction $a/b$ means $a$ divided by $b$, so $b\ne0$. For $b,d\ne0$,

$$
\frac ab+\frac cd=\frac{ad+bc}{bd},\qquad
\frac ab\frac cd=\frac{ac}{bd}.
$$

The addition formula works because each fraction is multiplied by a form of $1$: $d/d$ or $b/b$. To divide by $c/d$, multiply by $d/c$. This also requires $c\ne0$. Cancel common factors in a product; do not cancel terms across addition. For example, $(2+6)/2=4$, not $1+6$.

## Powers and roots

For a positive integer $n$, $a^n$ is a product of $n$ copies of $a$. Repeated multiplication gives

$$
a^ma^n=a^{m+n},\qquad (a^m)^n=a^{mn},\qquad (ab)^n=a^nb^n.
$$

These extend to integer exponents when negative powers have nonzero bases. Define $a^0=1$ for $a\ne0$ and $a^{-n}=1/a^n$. Then $a^m/a^n=a^{m-n}$ for $a\ne0$. Do not apply these definitions to $0^0$ or a negative power of zero.

The principal square root $\sqrt a$ is the nonnegative number whose square is $a$, and it exists over the reals for $a\ge0$. Consequently $\sqrt{a^2}=|a|$, not always $a$. The equation $u^2=9$ has two solutions, although the expression $\sqrt9$ has the single value $3$. An odd root can have a negative argument: $\sqrt[3]{-8}=-2$.

For $a>0$ and integers $m$ and $n>0$, define $a^{m/n}=(\sqrt[n]a)^m$. The positive-base restriction gives a consistent rule for every rational exponent. Some negative bases also permit rational powers, but an even root of a negative real number does not exist in the real numbers.

## Worked calculation

Calculate $3-(2/3-5/6)/(-1/2)^2+\sqrt{36}$. The inner subtraction and the denominator are

$$
\frac23-\frac56=\frac46-\frac56=-\frac16,
\qquad \left(-\frac12\right)^2=\frac14.
$$

Division by $1/4$ is multiplication by $4$. Therefore the full expression is

$$
3-\left(-\frac16\times4\right)+6
=3+\frac23+6=\frac{29}{3}.
$$

The outer subtraction changes the sign of the negative quotient. Keeping that quotient in brackets prevents a sign error. These rules also support the symbolic work in [[02.02 Equivalent expressions]].

## Practice

1. Evaluate $-2^4+(-2)^4-3(-2)$ and explain the two powers.
2. Simplify $(3/4-1/6)/(7/3)$, and simplify $\sqrt{9t^2}$ for real $t$.

## Worked answers

1. The first power is $-(2^4)=-16$. The second has base $-2$ and equals $16$. Also $-3(-2)=6$. The sum is $-16+16+6=6$.
2. A common denominator gives $3/4-1/6=9/12-2/12=7/12$. Thus $(7/12)(3/7)=1/4$. Since $3|t|\ge0$ and $(3|t|)^2=9t^2$, the principal root is $3|t|$.
