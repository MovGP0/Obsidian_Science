> [!TLDR]
> Completing the square exposes a quadratic's centre and constant part and leads directly to the quadratic formula.

## The square pattern

For real numbers $x$ and $h$, distribution gives

$$
(x+h)^2=x^2+2hx+h^2.
$$

To turn $x^2+px$ into a square, the middle coefficient requires $2h=p$, hence $h=p/2$. Add and subtract $p^2/4$ so that the original value does not change:

$$
x^2+px=x^2+px+\frac{p^2}{4}-\frac{p^2}{4}
=\left(x+\frac p2\right)^2-\frac{p^2}{4}.
$$

The added term completes the square. The subtracted term compensates for that addition. Adding the term without compensation changes an expression; in an equation, adding it to just one side also changes the solution set.

## General quadratic and centre

Let $q(x)=ax^2+bx+c$ with real coefficients and $a\ne0$. Factor $a$ from the first two terms before completing the square:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q(x)&=a\left(x^2+\frac ba x\right)+c\\
&=a\left[\left(x+\frac b{2a}\right)^2-\frac{b^2}{4a^2}\right]+c\\
&=a\left(x+\frac b{2a}\right)^2+c-\frac{b^2}{4a}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Define the centre coordinate $h=-b/(2a)$ and the constant part $k=c-b^2/(4a)$. Then

$$
q(x)=a(x-h)^2+k.
$$

The graph has vertex $(h,k)$ and symmetry axis $x=h$. More precisely, $q(h+t)=at^2+k=q(h-t)$ for every real displacement $t$. The parabola has reflection symmetry about this vertical line; it does not have point symmetry about its vertex.

Since $(x-h)^2\ge0$, $k$ is the minimum value if $a>0$ and the maximum value if $a<0$. The factor $a$ determines the opening direction and the vertical scale. The coordinate $h$ tells where the extremum occurs; it is not generally a root.

## Worked example

Complete the square in $q(x)=2x^2-8x+5$. First extract $2$:

$$
q(x)=2(x^2-4x)+5.
$$

Half the coefficient $-4$ is $-2$, whose square is $4$. Therefore

$$
q(x)=2\bigl[(x-2)^2-4\bigr]+5
=2(x-2)^2-3.
$$

The compensation inside the brackets contributes $-8$, not $-4$, because it is multiplied by $2$. The vertex is $(2,-3)$ and the minimum is $-3$. To find the zeros, set $q(x)=0$:

$$
2(x-2)^2=3,
\qquad (x-2)^2=\frac32,
\qquad x=2\pm\sqrt{\frac32}.
$$

These roots are equally far from $x=2$. The graph shows the vertex, the symmetry axis, and the two intersections with the horizontal axis.

## Deriving the quadratic formula

Start with $ax^2+bx+c=0$, where $a\ne0$. The completed form above gives

$$
a\left(x+\frac b{2a}\right)^2=\frac{b^2-4ac}{4a}.
$$

Divide by $a$, then multiply by $4a^2$:

$$
\left(x+\frac b{2a}\right)^2=\frac{b^2-4ac}{4a^2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
(2ax+b)^2=b^2-4ac.
$$

Write $D=b^2-4ac$. If $D\ge0$, both possible signs of the square root give $2ax+b=\pm\sqrt D$, hence

$$
x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt D}{2a}.
$$

Using the squared form avoids an incorrect substitution $\sqrt{4a^2}=2a$ when $a<0$; the principal root is actually $2|a|$. The sign pair in the final formula includes both branches for either sign of $a$.

If $D>0$, the roots are distinct. If $D=0$, they coincide at $h$. If $D<0$, a real square cannot equal $D$, so there are no real roots. No claim about complex roots is needed for this real-variable argument.

## Practice

1. Complete the square in $x^2+6x+5$ and solve the equation in which it equals zero.
2. Find the maximum of $-3x^2+12x-7$, and state where it occurs.

## Worked answers

1. Add and subtract $9$: $x^2+6x+5=(x+3)^2-9+5=(x+3)^2-4$. Setting this to zero gives $(x+3)^2=4$, so $x+3=\pm2$ and $x=-1$ or $x=-5$.
2. Factor $-3$ from the variable terms: $-3(x^2-4x)-7=-3[(x-2)^2-4]-7=-3(x-2)^2+5$. The square is nonnegative, so its multiple by $-3$ is nonpositive. The maximum is $5$, reached only when $x=2$.
