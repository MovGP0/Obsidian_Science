title: "Simultaneous equations"

> [!TLDR]
> A simultaneous solution satisfies every equation; substitution and elimination reduce the system while preserving that requirement.

## One set of unknowns, several conditions

A system of simultaneous equations places several conditions on the same unknowns. A solution of a system in $x,y$ is an ordered pair $(x,y)$ that satisfies every equation. Here all coefficients and unknowns are real. A linear equation has unknowns only to the first power, with no products of unknowns. Thus $2x+3y=7$ is linear, but $xy=7$ is not.

One linear equation in two unknowns usually has infinitely many solutions. Adding another equation can select one pair, leave the same family, or exclude all pairs. Geometrically, equations with nonzero coefficient pairs describe lines. Two lines can intersect once, coincide, or be parallel and distinct.

The methods below replace a system with an equivalent system: both must have the same solutions. You can exchange equations, multiply an equation by a nonzero number, or add a multiple of one equation to another while keeping the first equation. These operations can be reversed. Keeping only the sum of two equations usually loses information.

## Substitution

Substitution expresses one unknown in terms of the others, then uses that expression in the remaining equations. Consider

$$
\begin{cases}
2x+y=7,\\
x-y=2.
\end{cases}
$$

The second equation gives $y=x-2$. Substituting into the first gives

$$
2x+(x-2)=7
\iff3x=9
\iff x=3.
$$

Now recover the eliminated unknown: $y=3-2=1$. The solution is $(3,1)$, not merely $x=3$. Check both conditions: $2(3)+1=7$ and $3-1=2$.

Substitution is efficient when a coefficient is $1$ or $-1$. With general coefficients, it may introduce fractions. Fractions are valid, but elimination can keep the intermediate arithmetic simpler.

## Elimination

Elimination combines equations so that a selected unknown disappears. Solve

$$
\begin{cases}
2x+3y=13,\\
3x-2y=0.
\end{cases}
$$

Multiply the first equation by $2$ and the second by $3$:

$$
4x+6y=26,\qquad9x-6y=0.
$$

Adding gives $13x=26$, hence $x=2$. Substitution into $3x-2y=0$ gives $6-2y=0$, so $y=3$. The first equation confirms $4+9=13$. Opposite coefficients of $y$ were chosen so that the sum removed $y$ exactly.

For a general pair $ax+by=e$ and $cx+dy=f$, multiply the first equation by $d$ and subtract $b$ times the second. This gives

$$
(ad-bc)x=de-bf.
$$

The number $\Delta=ad-bc$ is the determinant of the coefficient array. If $\Delta\ne0$, then

$$
x=\frac{de-bf}{\Delta},\qquad
y=\frac{af-ce}{\Delta}.
$$

The second formula follows by eliminating $x$ instead. If $\Delta=0$, do not divide by it. Inspect the remaining equations for a contradiction or a free parameter.

## Three unknowns and exceptional cases

For three unknowns, eliminate in stages. Take

$$
\begin{cases}
x+y+z=6,\\
x-y+z=2,\\
2x+y-z=1.
\end{cases}
$$

Subtract the second equation from the first: $2y=4$, so $y=2$. The first and third then become $x+z=4$ and $2x-z=-1$. Their sum gives $3x=3$, so $x=1$ and $z=3$. The triple $(1,2,3)$ satisfies all three original equations.

A zero row can have two meanings. From $x+y=2$ and $2x+2y=4$, elimination gives $0=0$. The second equation adds no restriction, so write the family $(x,y)=(t,2-t)$ for arbitrary real $t$. If the second right-hand side were $5$, elimination would give $0=1$. That contradiction means there is no solution.

Do not treat $0=0$ as a value for an unknown. It states that one condition is redundant. Also do not infer consistency from one successful substitution; every original equation must be checked, as described in [[02.08 Checking solutions]].

## Practice

1. Solve $3x+2y=12$ and $x-2y=-4$ by elimination.
2. Classify the system $x+y=2$, $2x+2y=k$ for each real value of the parameter $k$.

## Worked answers

1. Adding the equations removes $y$ and gives $4x=8$, so $x=2$. The second equation gives $2-2y=-4$, hence $y=3$. The first gives $6+6=12$, so $(2,3)$ satisfies both.
2. Subtract twice the first equation from the second to obtain $0=k-4$. If $k\ne4$, the system has no solution. If $k=4$, the second equation repeats the first, and all solutions are $(t,2-t)$ with $t\in\mathbb R$. There is no value of $k$ that gives a unique solution.
