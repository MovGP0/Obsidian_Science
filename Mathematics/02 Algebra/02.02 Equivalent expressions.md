> [!TLDR]
> Expansion, collection, and factorisation give different forms of the same expression on its domain.

## What equivalence means

An expression gives a value after its variables have values. Two expressions are equivalent on a domain if they give the same value at every allowed input in that domain. Here $x$ is real. The expressions $2(x+3)$ and $2x+6$ are equivalent for all $x$. The expressions $x/x$ and $1$ agree only where $x\ne0$, because the first has no value at zero.

A polynomial in $x$ is a finite sum of terms $a_kx^k$, where each exponent $k$ is a nonnegative integer and each coefficient $a_k$ is a fixed number. The degree of a nonzero polynomial is its largest exponent with a nonzero coefficient. A constant nonzero polynomial has degree zero. The zero polynomial has no degree under this convention.

Like terms have the same variable powers. Thus $3x^2$ and $-5x^2$ combine to $-2x^2$, but $3x^2$ and $-5x$ do not combine. In several variables, every power must match: $xy^2$ and $x^2y$ are different term types.

## Expansion from distribution

The distributive law states $a(b+c)=ab+ac$. Applying it twice gives

$$
(a+b)(c+d)=a(c+d)+b(c+d)=ac+ad+bc+bd.
$$

Each term of the first bracket multiplies each term of the second. Signs are part of the terms. For example,

$$
(x-3)(x+2)=x^2+2x-3x-6=x^2-x-6.
$$

The area model shows the same rule for positive side lengths. Its four subareas add to the full rectangle.

![[math-02-product-expansion.svg|**Fig. 02.1** A rectangle with sides x + 3 and x + 2 splits into four areas. The diagram assumes x > 0; the algebraic identity holds for every real x.]]

The geometric restriction belongs to the picture, not to the polynomial identity. Expansion uses number laws that remain valid when $x$ is zero or negative.

## Worked expansion and collection

Simplify $(2x-3)(x+4)-x(x-1)$. First expand each complete product:

$$
(2x-3)(x+4)=2x^2+8x-3x-12=2x^2+5x-12,
$$

$$
x(x-1)=x^2-x.
$$

Subtracting the second expression changes the signs of both of its terms:

$$
2x^2+5x-12-(x^2-x)
=2x^2+5x-12-x^2+x=x^2+6x-12.
$$

A check at $x=1$ gives $-5$ in both forms. That check can find some errors, but one input cannot prove an identity. The distributive steps prove agreement for every real input.

## Factorisation reverses expansion

To factor an expression is to write it as a product. Begin with any factor common to all terms. For example,

$$
6x^3-15x^2=3x^2(2x-5).
$$

Multiplication verifies the result without division by $x$, so the identity includes $x=0$. This distinction matters when factors are later cancelled in [[02.03 Rational expressions]].

For a monic quadratic $x^2+bx+c$, try integers $r,s$ with $r+s=b$ and $rs=c$. Indeed,

$$
(x+r)(x+s)=x^2+(r+s)x+rs.
$$

Thus $x^2+5x+6=(x+2)(x+3)$. The sum and product tests are both necessary. Some polynomials have no real linear factorisation; $x^2+1$ is always positive for real $x$.

Useful identities, each obtained by distribution, are

$$
(a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2,\qquad
(a-b)^2=a^2-2ab+b^2,\qquad
a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b).
$$

Grouping can expose a factor that is initially hidden. For instance,

$$
x^3+2x^2-9x-18=x^2(x+2)-9(x+2)
=(x+2)(x^2-9)=(x+2)(x-3)(x+3).
$$

Do not omit the middle term in a squared sum. In general $(a+b)^2\ne a^2+b^2$. Also, a factorisation is not a solution set until an equation has been stated.

## Practice

1. Expand and collect $(x-2)^2+4x$. Does it equal $x^2$?
2. Factor $4x^2-12x+9$, then factor $2x^3-8x$ completely over the reals.

## Worked answers

1. Distribution gives $(x-2)^2=x^2-4x+4$. Adding $4x$ leaves $x^2+4$. The difference from $x^2$ is always $4$, so the expressions are never equal for real $x$.
2. The first expression is $(2x-3)^2$, since the cross term is $2(2x)(-3)=-12x$. For the second, remove $2x$: $2x(x^2-4)$. The remaining difference of squares gives $2x(x-2)(x+2)$.
