> [!TLDR]
> Isolate the selected variable with reversible operations, and state the conditions that make each inverse operation valid.

## What is being isolated?

A formula relates several quantities or mathematical variables. To make $x$ the subject is to express its permitted values in terms of the other symbols. Treat those other symbols as fixed parameters during the calculation. This does not mean that their values are known; it means that the rearrangement must state which parameter values it permits.

Work over the real numbers unless stated otherwise. Record the original domain first. Additions and subtractions can always be reversed when the expressions are defined. Division requires a nonzero divisor. Taking a reciprocal requires a nonzero expression. Taking a real square root requires a nonnegative argument and can introduce two branches when it is used to solve a square equation.

It is useful to read the formula as a sequence of operations and reverse them in the opposite order. For example, $y=3(x-2)+5$ subtracts $2$ from $x$, multiplies by $3$, and adds $5$. Therefore subtract $5$, divide by $3$, and add $2$:

$$
y-5=3(x-2),\qquad
\frac{y-5}{3}=x-2,\qquad
x=2+\frac{y-5}{3}.
$$

## A parameter can make division invalid

Consider $u=a(v-b)$, where the target is $v$ and $u,a,b$ are parameters. If $a\ne0$, then

$$
v=b+\frac ua.
$$

If $a=0$, return to the original formula: it becomes $u=0$. For $u\ne0$ there is no solution. For $u=0$ every real $v$ is a solution. The statement $v=b+u/a$ covers only the first branch. It cannot describe the zero-coefficient cases because its fraction is undefined there.

This is the same distinction between a unique solution, an identity, and a contradiction that appears in [[02.04 Equations and inequalities]]. It also explains why a computer algebra result must be read together with its assumptions.

## Worked rational rearrangement

Make $x$ the subject of

$$
y=\frac{3x-2}{x+4}.
$$

The original expression requires $x\ne-4$. On that domain, multiplication by $x+4$ is reversible:

$$
y(x+4)=3x-2.
$$

Expand, then collect all terms containing $x$ on one side:

$$
yx+4y=3x-2,
\qquad (y-3)x=-2-4y.
$$

If $y\ne3$, divide by $y-3$ and change both signs:

$$
x=\frac{4y+2}{3-y}.
$$

If $y=3$, the preceding equation would read $0=-14$, so there is no solution. Also check that the new formula never produces the original forbidden input:

$$
x+4=\frac{4y+2+4(3-y)}{3-y}
=\frac{14}{3-y}\ne0.
$$

Thus the rearranged formula is valid for exactly $y\ne3$, with the original restriction automatically satisfied. For example, $y=1$ gives $x=3$, and $(9-2)/(3+4)=1$ verifies it. The missing value $y=3$ is a mathematical feature of the relation.

## Inverses and branches

The reciprocal relation $y=1/(x-2)$ requires $x\ne2$. Its numerator is nonzero, so $y\ne0$. Multiplying gives $y(x-2)=1$, then $x=2+1/y$. Stating $y\ne0$ is essential before the division.

For a square relation $y=a(x-h)^2+k$ with $a\ne0$, subtract $k$ and divide by $a$:

$$
(x-h)^2=\frac{y-k}{a}.
$$

Real solutions exist only if $(y-k)/a\ge0$. If this condition holds,

$$
x=h\pm\sqrt{\frac{y-k}{a}}.
$$

The two branches coincide when the root is zero. A restriction such as $x\ge h$ selects the plus branch. Without such a restriction, replacing the result with only the principal root loses solutions. When $a=0$, the original formula is simply $y=k$ and must be treated separately.

## Practice

1. Make $r$ the subject of $s=(r+1)/(r-2)$ and state the permitted values of $s$.
2. Solve $u=(t-1)^2+4$ for real $t$. Then apply the additional condition $t\le1$.

## Worked answers

1. First require $r\ne2$. Multiply to obtain $sr-2s=r+1$, so $(s-1)r=2s+1$. If $s\ne1$, $r=(2s+1)/(s-1)$. This gives $r-2=3/(s-1)\ne0$. If $s=1$, the equation would give $0=3$, so no solution exists.
2. Subtract $4$: $(t-1)^2=u-4$. Thus $u\ge4$ is necessary and sufficient for a real solution. The solutions are $t=1\pm\sqrt{u-4}$. Under $t\le1$, retain $t=1-\sqrt{u-4}$. At $u=4$, both branches give $t=1$.
