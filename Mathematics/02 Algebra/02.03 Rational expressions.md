> [!TLDR]
> Factor before cancellation and retain every restriction imposed by the original denominators and divisors.

## Expression and domain

A rational expression is a quotient $p(x)/q(x)$ of polynomials, where $q$ is not the zero polynomial. For real inputs, its domain contains precisely the values for which $q(x)\ne0$. The denominator can vanish at some inputs even though it is not the zero polynomial. For example, $q(x)=x^2-1$ excludes $x=-1$ and $x=1$.

Write domain restrictions before simplification. A shorter formula can conceal a value that the original formula did not allow. The equality

$$
\frac{x^2-9}{x-3}=x+3\qquad(x\ne3)
$$

means that the values agree on the original domain. It does not define the original quotient at $x=3$. Assigning the value $6$ there would extend the function to a larger domain.

## Why cancellation works

For $g(x)\ne0$ and $b(x)\ne0$,

$$
\frac{g(x)a(x)}{g(x)b(x)}
=\frac{g(x)}{g(x)}\frac{a(x)}{b(x)}
=\frac{a(x)}{b(x)}.
$$

Cancellation removes a common nonzero factor. It does not remove matching symbols from arbitrary sums. For instance, $(x+2)/x=1+2/x$ for $x\ne0$; it is not $2$. Use the factorisation methods in [[02.02 Equivalent expressions]] to expose a product first.

Consider

$$
R(x)=\frac{x^2-9}{x^2-x-6}.
$$

The denominator factors as $(x-3)(x+2)$, so $x\ne3,-2$. The numerator is $(x-3)(x+3)$. Therefore

$$
R(x)=\frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{(x-3)(x+2)}
=\frac{x+3}{x+2},\qquad x\ne3,-2.
$$

At $x=1$, both forms give $4/3$. At $x=3$, the shorter quotient is defined, but $R$ is not. At $x=-3$, the numerator is zero and the denominator is nonzero, so $R(-3)=0$ is allowed. A zero numerator alone causes no problem.

## Addition and a common denominator

To add rational expressions, use a common denominator that is nonzero throughout the allowed domain. For example, the expression

$$
\frac1{x-1}-\frac2{x+1}
$$

requires $x\ne1,-1$. Multiply the first fraction by $(x+1)/(x+1)$ and the second by $(x-1)/(x-1)$:

$$
\frac{(x+1)-2(x-1)}{(x-1)(x+1)}
=\frac{x+1-2x+2}{x^2-1}
=\frac{3-x}{x^2-1}.
$$

The brackets around the second numerator are essential: the minus sign applies to both terms of $2(x-1)$. Adding denominators, such as replacing the original expression with $-1/(2x)$, has no basis in the fraction rules.

## Division has extra restrictions

Dividing by a rational expression requires that the divisor is defined and nonzero. For example,

$$
\frac{x}{x+1}\div\frac{x-2}{x+1}
$$

first requires $x\ne-1$. It also requires $x\ne2$, because the divisor is zero there. Now replace division by multiplication by the reciprocal:

$$
\frac{x}{x+1}\frac{x+1}{x-2}
=\frac{x}{x-2},\qquad x\ne-1,2.
$$

The excluded input $-1$ remains excluded after cancellation. A reciprocal cannot repair an expression that was undefined before the division.

When solving an equation with rational expressions, clearing denominators is valid on the stated domain because the multiplying denominator is nonzero there. Record that domain and check candidates in the original equation. See [[02.08 Checking solutions]] for transformations that can produce invalid candidates.

## Practice

1. Simplify $(x^2-4)/(x^2+4x+4)$ and state all original restrictions.
2. Simplify $\bigl(1/x+1/(x+1)\bigr)\div(1/x)$ and state all restrictions.

## Worked answers

1. Factor the numerator as $(x-2)(x+2)$ and the denominator as $(x+2)^2$. The original denominator is zero only at $x=-2$. For $x\ne-2$, cancel one factor $x+2$ to obtain $(x-2)/(x+2)$.
2. The summands require $x\ne0,-1$. The divisor $1/x$ is never zero on its domain. A common denominator gives $(2x+1)/(x(x+1))$. Multiplication by the reciprocal $x$ then gives $(2x+1)/(x+1)$, with both original restrictions $x\ne0,-1$ retained.
