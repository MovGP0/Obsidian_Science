---
title: "Frame changes for connections and curvature"
---

> [!TLDR]
> A frame change adds a derivative term to the connection matrix, while curvature transforms by conjugation alone.

## Declare the matrix direction

On an open set, let $e=(e_1,\ldots,e_n)$ be a row of basis vectors and $\theta$ the column of dual one-forms. Choose a smooth map $A$ into the invertible real matrices and define a new frame by

$$
e'=eA,\qquad e'_b=e_aA^a{}_b.
$$

The dual coframe and vector component column transform as

$$
\theta'=A^{-1}\theta,\qquad v'=A^{-1}v.
$$

Indeed, $V=ev=e'A^{-1}v$, and $\theta'(e')$ is the identity matrix. These formulas fix which inverse belongs where. If both frames are orthonormal for a constant metric matrix $\eta$, then $A^T\eta A=\eta$. In positive definite signature this is the orthogonal-group condition. General linear frame changes remain valid if the metric components are also transformed.

## Derive the connection law

Use $\nabla_Xe_b=e_a\omega^a{}_b(X)$. Differentiating the changed frame gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_Xe'_b
&=(\nabla_Xe_a)A^a{}_b+e_aX(A^a{}_b)\\
&=e\,[\omega(X)A+X(A)].
\end{aligned}
$$

Replace $e$ with $e'A^{-1}$ and read the new coefficient matrix:

$$
\omega'=A^{-1}\omega A+A^{-1}dA.
$$

The matrix $dA$ contains the exterior derivative of each scalar entry. Its derivative term is required by the product rule. Therefore connection forms do not transform as an endomorphism-valued tensor. If $A$ is constant, the derivative term vanishes, which can hide this distinction in elementary examples.

For the same reason, the component derivative remains consistent:

$$
dv'+\omega'v'=A^{-1}(dv+\omega v).
$$

To check it, differentiate $v'=A^{-1}v$ and use $d(A^{-1})=-A^{-1}(dA)A^{-1}$. The derivatives of $A$ cancel. Thus the covariant derivative is a geometric operation, although its connection matrix has an extra transformation term.

## Curvature has no derivative term

Define curvature by $R(X,Y)=\nabla_X\nabla_Y-\nabla_Y\nabla_X-\nabla_{[X,Y]}$ and $R(X,Y)e=e\Omega(X,Y)$. Since $R$ is linear over functions in the vector acted on,

$$
R(X,Y)e'=R(X,Y)(eA)=e\Omega(X,Y)A
=e'A^{-1}\Omega(X,Y)A.
$$

Therefore

$$
\Omega'=A^{-1}\Omega A.
$$

One can also obtain this from $\Omega=d\omega+\omega\wedge\omega$ by substituting the connection law; the terms containing $dA$ cancel. The displayed law refers to the two-form matrix before expanding its arguments. When components in the new coframe are needed, transform the two argument slots as well:

$$
R'^a{}_{bcd}=(A^{-1})^a{}_p A^q{}_b A^r{}_c A^s{}_d R^p{}_{qrs}.
$$

Conjugation alone transforms the output and acted-on vector slots. The additional two factors account for $e'_c,e'_d$.

## Worked example: a connection created by basis rotation

In the Euclidean plane, start with $e=(\partial_x,\partial_y)$, for which $\omega=0$ and $\Omega=0$. On a polar chart with $r>0$, choose

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\phi&-\sin\phi\\\sin\phi&\cos\phi\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The columns give the radial and unit angular vectors. Since $A^{-1}=A^T$, direct differentiation gives

$$
A^{-1}dA=
\begin{pmatrix}0&-d\phi\\d\phi&0\end{pmatrix}=\omega'.
$$

This new connection matrix is nonzero. Nevertheless, $d(d\phi)=0$ on the chosen chart and all products contain $d\phi\wedge d\phi=0$, so $d\omega'+\omega'\wedge\omega'=0$. This agrees with $\Omega'=A^{-1}0A=0$. The nonzero coefficients describe rotation of the frame; they do not create curvature.

More generally, rotate a positive definite surface frame through a smooth angle $\psi$. If $J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $\omega=J\alpha$, then $\omega'=J(\alpha+d\psi)$. Its curvature is $Jd\alpha$ because $d^2\psi=0$. The area form is unchanged by this orientation-preserving rotation. Therefore the coefficient called Gaussian curvature stays the same. This is consistent with the tensor contractions in [[24.08 Contractions]].

## Practice

1. In a line bundle with frame $e$ and connection $\omega$, set $e'=e\,e^h$ for a smooth real function $h$. Find the new connection and curvature.
2. At one point, a two-dimensional curvature endomorphism is $B=\begin{pmatrix}0&-k\\k&0\end{pmatrix}$. Change basis by the constant matrix $A=\operatorname{diag}(2,1)$. Compute $B'$ and explain what stays fixed.

## Worked answers

1. The scalar frame matrix is $A=e^h$, so $A^{-1}dA=dh$ and $\omega'=\omega+dh$. A scalar one-form wedges with itself to zero, so $\Omega=d\omega$. Then $\Omega'=d\omega+d^2h=d\omega=\Omega$. A nonzero $dh$ can therefore change the connection coefficient without changing curvature.
2. Multiplication gives $B'=A^{-1}BA=\begin{pmatrix}0&-k/2\\2k&0\end{pmatrix}$. Its entries differ, but its trace is still zero and determinant is still $k^2$. It is the same linear map in a different basis. The new basis is not orthonormal for the original Euclidean metric: its metric matrix is $A^TA=\operatorname{diag}(4,1)$. Using the identity metric with these new entries would change the problem.
