> [!TLDR]
> An orthonormal frame makes the metric matrix constant and diagonal, with the signs fixed by the metric signature.

## What the metric condition means

Let $g$ be a smooth, nondegenerate symmetric metric on an $n$-dimensional manifold. Its **signature** records the number of negative and positive squares. On a sufficiently small open set, choose a frame $e_a$ with

$$
g(e_a,e_b)=\eta_{ab},\qquad
\eta=\operatorname{diag}(\underbrace{-1,\ldots,-1}_{q},
\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{n-q}).
$$

This is an **orthonormal frame**, also called a pseudo-orthonormal frame when $q>0$. For a Riemannian metric, $q=0$ and $\eta$ is the identity. The signs are part of the geometry. A real basis change cannot turn an indefinite metric into a positive definite metric.

Let $\theta^a$ be the dual coframe, so $\theta^a(e_b)=\delta^a_b$. The tensor expression for the metric is

$$
g=\eta_{ab}\,\theta^a\otimes\theta^b.
$$

Thus $g(V,W)=\eta_{ab}v^aw^b$ for $V=v^ae_a$ and $W=w^be_b$. The coefficients are constant in this frame. The frame fields themselves can vary from point to point, so constant coefficients do not imply zero curvature or zero connection coefficients.

## Constructing the frame

For a positive definite metric and an initial frame $u_1,\ldots,u_n$, the Gram–Schmidt construction starts with

$$
e_1=\frac{u_1}{\sqrt{g(u_1,u_1)}},\qquad
w_k=u_k-\sum_{a<k}g(u_k,e_a)e_a,
\qquad e_k=\frac{w_k}{\sqrt{g(w_k,w_k)}}.
$$

At each step the projection terms remove components in earlier directions. Positive definiteness makes the remaining nonzero vector have a positive squared length. Smooth input fields give smooth output fields on a region where these denominators do not vanish.

In indefinite signature, a nonzero vector can have zero squared length. The displayed algorithm can then fail. If previously chosen vectors obey $g(e_a,e_a)=\varepsilon_a\in\{-1,1\}$, the projection coefficient is $\varepsilon_a g(u_k,e_a)$, and normalization uses $\sqrt{|g(w_k,w_k)|}$. One must first choose suitable non-null directions. This local construction does not guarantee a global frame.

## Worked example: a metric with a cross term

On $\mathbb R^2$, consider

$$
g=4\,dx\otimes dx+2\,dx\otimes dy
+2\,dy\otimes dx+2\,dy\otimes dy.
$$

In the usual shorthand this is $4dx^2+4dx\,dy+2dy^2$. The matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\2&2\end{pmatrix}$; its leading principal minors are $4$ and $4$, so it is positive definite. Complete a square:

$$
g=(2dx+dy)\otimes(2dx+dy)+dy\otimes dy.
$$

Choose $\theta^1=2dx+dy$ and $\theta^2=dy$. To find the dual vectors, impose the four pairings. They give

$$
e_1=\tfrac12\partial_x,\qquad
e_2=-\tfrac12\partial_x+\partial_y.
$$

Indeed, $g(e_1,e_1)=4/4=1$, $g(e_1,e_2)=-1+1=0$, and $g(e_2,e_2)=1-2+2=1$. If $V=\partial_x+2\partial_y$, its frame components are $(4,2)$, so $g(V,V)=4^2+2^2=20$. Direct multiplication by the coordinate metric also gives $4+8+8=20$.

## Curved and indefinite examples

For $g=dr^2+f(r)^2d\phi^2$ on a region with $f>0$, take $\theta^1=dr$, $\theta^2=f\,d\phi$. The frame is $e_1=\partial_r$, $e_2=f^{-1}\partial_\phi$. This construction works for $f=r$, $f=\sin r$ on $0<r<\pi$, and $f=\sinh r$ on $r>0$. These choices have different curvatures, although all have frame metric $\delta_{ab}$. [[24.06 Cartan’s second structure equation]] computes the difference.

For $g=-du^2+e^{2u}dv^2$, the coframe $\theta^1=du$, $\theta^2=e^u dv$ gives $g=-(\theta^1)^2+(\theta^2)^2$. Replacing the minus sign by a plus sign would change the metric rather than simplify it.

An orthonormal coframe is not generally a list of coordinate differentials. In the last example, $d\theta^2=e^u du\wedge dv$ is nonzero, so there cannot be a local function $z$ with $dz=\theta^2$: an exact differential always has zero exterior derivative. This is the precise reason that a constant frame metric need not make the original metric a constant coordinate metric. [[24.03 Non-coordinate frames]] develops this distinction through commutators.

## Practice

1. Find an orthonormal coframe and its dual frame for $g=9dx^2+e^{2x}dy^2$.
2. For $g=-4dx^2+9dy^2$, compute the squared length of $V=\partial_x+\partial_y$ in coordinates and in an orthonormal frame. Is $V$ a unit vector?

## Worked answers

1. Set $\theta^1=3dx$, $\theta^2=e^xdy$. Then $g=(\theta^1)^2+(\theta^2)^2$. Duality gives $e_1=\frac13\partial_x$ and $e_2=e^{-x}\partial_y$. Each form evaluates to one on its matching vector and zero on the other.
2. Choose $\theta^1=2dx$, $\theta^2=3dy$ and $e_1=\frac12\partial_x$, $e_2=\frac13\partial_y$. Then $V=2e_1+3e_2$, so its squared length is $-2^2+3^2=5$, the same as $-4+9$. It is not a unit vector. Since its squared length is positive, $V/\sqrt5$ has squared length one.
