---
title: "Non-coordinate frames"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Frame commutators measure failure to be a coordinate basis, and the exterior derivatives of the dual coframe contain the same information.

## Coordinate fields commute

In a coordinate chart $x^1,\ldots,x^n$, the vector fields $\partial_a=\partial/\partial x^a$ satisfy $[\partial_a,\partial_b]=0$. The **Lie bracket** is defined by its action on smooth functions:

$$
[X,Y](h)=X(Y(h))-Y(X(h)).
$$

It measures the difference between the two orders of differentiation. A general frame $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ need not commute. Its **structure functions** $C^a{}_{bc}$ are defined by

$$
[e_b,e_c]=C^a{}_{bc}e_a,\qquad C^a{}_{bc}=-C^a{}_{cb}.
$$

The coefficients can be functions of position. Constant structure coefficients are a special case, for example in left-invariant frames on a Lie group. The word “structure” alone does not imply constancy.

A frame with nonzero brackets cannot equal a coordinate basis on that region. Conversely, a smooth commuting frame admits suitable coordinates locally. This last statement is local: topology can obstruct a single global coordinate system even if the frame commutes.

## The dual exterior derivative

Let $\theta^a(e_b)=\delta^a_b$. For every one-form $\alpha$,

$$
d\alpha(X,Y)=X(\alpha(Y))-Y(\alpha(X))-\alpha([X,Y]).
$$

Apply this identity to $\alpha=\theta^a$, $X=e_b$, and $Y=e_c$. The first two terms vanish because the pairings are constant. Thus

$$
d\theta^a(e_b,e_c)=-C^a{}_{bc},\qquad
d\theta^a=-\tfrac12 C^a{}_{bc}\,\theta^b\wedge\theta^c.
$$

The factor $1/2$ is necessary when both $b,c$ are summed. The pair $(c,b)$ contributes the same two-form as $(b,c)$ because both the coefficient and the wedge change sign. An equivalent expression is $d\theta^a=-\sum_{b<c}C^a{}_{bc}\theta^b\wedge\theta^c$.

There is also a direct local reason that vanishing structure functions give coordinates. If every $d\theta^a=0$, the local Poincaré lemma gives functions $y^a$ such that $\theta^a=dy^a$ on a sufficiently small contractible set. Their differentials are independent, so the inverse function theorem makes $y^1,\ldots,y^n$ a coordinate system. Duality then gives $e_a=\partial/\partial y^a$.

## Worked example: normalized polar directions

On $r>0$, choose $e_1=\partial_r$ and $e_2=r^{-1}\partial_\phi$. Their dual forms are $\theta^1=dr$, $\theta^2=r\,d\phi$. The bracket product rule gives

$$
[e_1,e_2]=[\partial_r,r^{-1}\partial_\phi]
=\partial_r(r^{-1})\partial_\phi
=-r^{-2}\partial_\phi=-r^{-1}e_2.
$$

Thus $C^2{}_{12}=-1/r$. Independently,

$$
d\theta^2=d(r\,d\phi)=dr\wedge d\phi
=r^{-1}\theta^1\wedge\theta^2.
$$

Evaluation on $(e_1,e_2)$ gives $1/r=-C^2{}_{12}$, as required. Although polar coordinates exist, their coordinate basis is $(\partial_r,\partial_\phi)$; normalizing the angular vector produces a different basis that does not commute. Figure 24.1 in [[24.01 Frames and coframes]] shows the scale factor responsible for this difference.

## What nonzero brackets do not say

Nonzero structure functions do not by themselves mean nonzero curvature or nonzero torsion. The polar example lies in a flat Euclidean plane. Its usual Levi-Civita connection has zero torsion, although $d\theta^2\ne0$. In [[24.05 Cartan’s first structure equation]], a connection term cancels this exterior derivative. Torsion compares the connection with the brackets; the brackets alone describe the frame.

Structure functions also need not transform as components of a tensor under a position-dependent frame change. Differentiation of the frame-change matrix adds extra terms. A constant change of basis is a simpler special case.

## Practice

1. On $\mathbb R^2$, take $e_1=\partial_x$, $e_2=e^x\partial_y$. Find the bracket, dual coframe, and exterior derivative of $\theta^2$.
2. For $\theta^1=dx$, $\theta^2=dy+x\,dx$, find the dual frame and determine whether it is a coordinate frame locally.

## Worked answers

1. The bracket is $[e_1,e_2]=e^x\partial_y=e_2$, so $C^2{}_{12}=1$. Duality gives $\theta^1=dx$, $\theta^2=e^{-x}dy$. Then $d\theta^2=-e^{-x}dx\wedge dy=-\theta^1\wedge\theta^2$. Its value on $(e_1,e_2)$ is $-1$, which agrees with the bracket formula.
2. Solving the pairing equations gives $e_1=\partial_x-x\partial_y$ and $e_2=\partial_y$. Their bracket is zero because the coefficient $-x$ is independent of $y$. Also, $d\theta^2=dx\wedge dx=0$. Define $u=x$, $v=y+x^2/2$. Then $du=\theta^1$, $dv=\theta^2$, and the frame equals $(\partial_u,\partial_v)$. The nonconstant coefficients in the original chart therefore do not prevent a coordinate description in another chart.
