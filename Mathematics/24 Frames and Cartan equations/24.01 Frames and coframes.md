title: "Frames and coframes"

> [!TLDR]
> A frame gives a local basis of tangent vectors; its dual coframe extracts the corresponding vector components.

## Two bases at each point

Let $U$ be an open set in a smooth $n$-dimensional manifold $M$. A **local frame** is an ordered list of smooth vector fields $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ which form a basis of each tangent space $T_pM$, for $p\in U$. The frame need not come from coordinates. Smoothness permits differentiation of its vectors, while pointwise independence permits a unique component expansion.

The **dual coframe** consists of one-forms $\theta^1,\ldots,\theta^n$ such that

$$
\theta^a(e_b)=\delta^a_b.
$$

Here $\delta^a_b$ equals one if $a=b$ and zero otherwise. Repeated upper and lower indices are summed from $1$ to $n$. Superscripts label coframe elements and vector components; they are not powers. If $V=v^a e_a$, duality gives $\theta^b(V)=v^b$. For a one-form $\alpha=\alpha_a\theta^a$, its coefficients satisfy $\alpha_a=\alpha(e_a)$. Thus vectors and covectors use the same pairing, but their component transformations run in opposite directions.

These are local statements. A manifold can admit frames on every small chart without admitting a single frame on the whole manifold. No metric is needed to define a frame or its dual.

## Conversion from coordinates

In coordinates $x^1,\ldots,x^n$, write $\partial_\mu=\partial/\partial x^\mu$. Let

$$
e_a=E^\mu{}_a\partial_\mu,\qquad
\theta^a=F^a{}_\mu\,dx^\mu.
$$

Greek indices label coordinate components and Latin indices label frame components. Duality requires

$$
F^a{}_\mu E^\mu{}_b=\delta^a_b,
\qquad F=E^{-1}.
$$

The inverse exists because the columns of $E$ are independent. If $V=V^\mu\partial_\mu=v^ae_a$, comparison of coefficients gives

$$
V^\mu=E^\mu{}_av^a,\qquad
v^a=F^a{}_\mu V^\mu.
$$

For a covector, substitution into $\alpha=\alpha_a\theta^a$ instead gives $\alpha_\mu=\alpha_aF^a{}_\mu$. As a check, the scalar $\alpha(V)$ equals both $\alpha_\mu V^\mu$ and $\alpha_av^a$.

## Worked example: the polar frame

On a region of the plane with $r>0$ and a smooth choice of angle $\phi$, set $x=r\cos\phi$ and $y=r\sin\phi$. The chain rule gives

$$
\partial_r=\cos\phi\,\partial_x+\sin\phi\,\partial_y,
\qquad
\partial_\phi=-r\sin\phi\,\partial_x+r\cos\phi\,\partial_y.
$$

Choose $e_1=\partial_r$ and $e_2=r^{-1}\partial_\phi$. Their dual forms are $\theta^1=dr$ and $\theta^2=r\,d\phi$. For example, $\theta^2(e_2)=r\,d\phi(r^{-1}\partial_\phi)=1$. The coordinate vector $\partial_\phi$ and the frame vector $e_2$ have the same direction, but their lengths differ in the Euclidean metric.

![[math-24-polar-frame-duality.svg|**Fig. 24.1** The polar frame at a point with r = 2. The angular coordinate vector is twice the unit angular frame vector; the dual coframe compensates for this scale.]]

If $V=3\partial_r+2\partial_\phi$, then

$$
V=3e_1+2r e_2.
$$

At $r=2$, its frame components are $(3,4)$, not $(3,2)$. At $\phi=\pi/2$, $e_1=\partial_y$ and $e_2=-\partial_x$, so the same vector is $-4\partial_x+3\partial_y$. For $\alpha=2dr+5d\phi$, the frame expansion is $2\theta^1+(5/r)\theta^2$. Evaluation gives $\alpha(V)=6+10=16$ in either basis.

## Common errors

Do not confuse the dual coframe with the list of metric duals $g(e_a,\cdot)$. These lists agree in a positive definite orthonormal frame, but they need not agree in a general frame. Also, the polar construction excludes $r=0$: an inverse matrix or an angle formula that fails there cannot define a smooth frame there. See [[24.02 Orthonormal frames]] for the metric condition and [[24.03 Non-coordinate frames]] for the role of commutators.

## Practice

1. On the plane, take $e_1=2\partial_x$ and $e_2=\partial_x+\partial_y$. Find the dual coframe and the frame components of $V=5\partial_x+3\partial_y$.
2. With the same frame, write $\alpha=dx-2dy$ in the coframe and verify $\alpha(V)$.

## Worked answers

1. Solve $V=2v^1\partial_x+v^2(\partial_x+\partial_y)$. The $y$ coefficient gives $v^2=3$; the $x$ coefficient gives $2v^1+3=5$, so $v^1=1$. The inverse relations are $\theta^1=(dx-dy)/2$ and $\theta^2=dy$. Direct evaluation on $e_1,e_2$ gives the identity matrix, and evaluation on $V$ gives $(1,3)$.
2. Since $dx=2\theta^1+\theta^2$ and $dy=\theta^2$, substitution gives $\alpha=2\theta^1-\theta^2$. Therefore $\alpha(V)=2(1)-3=-1$. The coordinate calculation gives $5-2(3)=-1$ as required.
