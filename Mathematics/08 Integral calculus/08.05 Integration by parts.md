---
title: "Integration by parts"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Integration by parts transfers a derivative between factors and includes a product evaluated at the boundaries.

## Derive the identity

Let $u$ and $v$ be continuously differentiable real functions on $[a,b]$. The product rule is

$$
(uv)'=u'v+uv'.
$$

Integrate both sides and use [[08.03 The fundamental theorem]] on the left:

$$
[u(x)v(x)]_a^b=\int_a^b u'(x)v(x)\,dx+\int_a^b u(x)v'(x)\,dx.
$$

Move the first integral to the other side to obtain

$$
\int_a^b uv'\,dx=[uv]_a^b-\int_a^b u'v\,dx.
$$

In the common differential notation, $du=u'\,dx$ and $dv=v'\,dx$, so the indefinite version is

$$
\int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du.
$$

The purpose is to replace one integral by a simpler one. The factor called $u$ is differentiated; the factor called $dv$ must be integrated to find $v$. Choosing $u$ whose derivative is simpler, such as a polynomial or logarithm, often helps. This is a strategy, not a theorem that every choice will improve the integral.

## Worked example: preserve the boundary product

Evaluate $I=\int_0^1 xe^x\,dx$. Choose $u=x$ and $dv=e^x\,dx$. Then $du=dx$ and $v=e^x$. Substitution into the definite rule gives

$$
I=[xe^x]_0^1-\int_0^1e^x\,dx
=e-[e^x]_0^1
=e-(e-1)=1.
$$

The first term is $1\cdot e-0\cdot1=e$. The second integral is $e-1$. If the boundary product were dropped, the result would be $1-e$, which is negative even though the original integrand is nonnegative. A sign check therefore detects this omission.

For an indefinite version, $\int xe^x\,dx=xe^x-e^x+C$. Differentiate the complete expression: $(xe^x-e^x)'=e^x+xe^x-e^x=xe^x$. The extra derivative term from the product cancels with the derivative of the second term.

## A logarithm can be one factor

An integrand need not display an obvious product. On $x>0$, write $\ln x=(\ln x)\cdot1$. Choose $u=\ln x$, $dv=dx$, $du=dx/x$, and $v=x$. Then

$$
\int\ln x\,dx=x\ln x-\int x\frac1x\,dx=x\ln x-x+C.
$$

This choice works because differentiating the logarithm gives $1/x$, which cancels the new factor $x$. The reverse choice would require integrating $\ln x$ in order to find $v$ and would make no progress.

## Repeated integration and returning integrals

For $\int x^2e^x\,dx$, choose $u=x^2$ first. Its derivative is $2x$, so another integration by parts reduces the polynomial degree again:

$$
\int x^2e^x\,dx=x^2e^x-2\int xe^x\,dx
=e^x(x^2-2x+2)+C.
$$

Sometimes the original integral returns. Set $I=\int e^x\cos x\,dx$. Two applications give $I=e^x\cos x+\int e^x\sin x\,dx$ and $\int e^x\sin x\,dx=e^x\sin x-I$. Thus $2I=e^x(\cos x+\sin x)$, up to an arbitrary constant, and $I=\tfrac12e^x(\cos x+\sin x)+C$. Solving the resulting algebraic equation is valid; repeatedly applying the rule without collecting $I$ would obscure this step.

For improper integrals, apply the rule on a finite truncated interval first, then take limits of the complete expression. Writing a boundary symbol at infinity does not establish that its limit exists. Cancellation of divergent pieces also requires care.

## Practice

1. Evaluate $\int_0^\pi x\sin x\,dx$.
2. Find $\int x\ln x\,dx$ for $x>0$.

## Worked answers

1. Choose $u=x$, $dv=\sin x\,dx$, so $v=-\cos x$ and $du=dx$. The integral is $[-x\cos x]_0^\pi+\int_0^\pi\cos x\,dx=\pi+[\sin x]_0^\pi=\pi$. Both endpoint values of the sine term are zero.
2. Choose $u=\ln x$, $dv=x\,dx$, giving $du=dx/x$ and $v=x^2/2$. Then the result is $(x^2/2)\ln x-(1/2)\int x\,dx=(x^2/2)\ln x-x^2/4+C$. Differentiation gives $x\ln x+x/2-x/2=x\ln x$.
