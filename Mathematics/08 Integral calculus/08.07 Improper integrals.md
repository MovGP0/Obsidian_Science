---
title: "Improper integrals"
---

> [!TLDR]
> An improper integral is a limit of ordinary integrals; it converges only when every required one-sided limit is finite.

## Define the missing endpoint by a limit

The usual Riemann integral assumes a bounded function on a closed bounded interval. An infinite interval or an unbounded integrand requires a new definition. If $f$ is integrable on every finite interval $[a,R]$, define

$$
\int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx=\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_a^Rf(x)\,dx.
$$

The integral **converges** if this limit exists as a finite real number. It **diverges** otherwise, including when the values tend to positive or negative infinity or keep oscillating. Infinity is a limit direction, not a number to substitute into an antiderivative.

For a function unbounded near a finite left endpoint $a$, define

$$
\int_a^bf(x)\,dx=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow0}\int_{a+\varepsilon}^bf(x)\,dx.
$$

Use an analogous limit from the left at a singular right endpoint. If a singularity lies at $c\in(a,b)$, split the integral at $c$ and require the two one-sided integrals to converge separately. Likewise, $\int_{-\infty}^\infty f$ means $\int_{-\infty}^cf+\int_c^\infty f$, with both terms finite. The choice of finite split point does not change a convergent answer.

## Worked example: two power tests

For a real exponent $p$, first consider $\int_1^\infty x^{-p}\,dx$. If $p\ne1$, integration on $[1,R]$ gives

$$
\int_1^R x^{-p}\,dx=\frac{R^{1-p}-1}{1-p}.
$$

If $p>1$, then $1-p<0$, so $R^{1-p}\to0$. The limit is $1/(p-1)$. If $p<1$, the power grows without bound, so the integral diverges. At $p=1$, the truncated integral is $\ln R$, which also diverges. Thus convergence at infinity requires $p>1$.

Now consider $\int_0^1x^{-p}\,dx$. For $p\ne1$,

$$
\int_\varepsilon^1x^{-p}\,dx=\frac{1-\varepsilon^{1-p}}{1-p}.
$$

Here the small endpoint reverses the condition: $\varepsilon^{1-p}\to0$ when $p<1$, giving $1/(1-p)$. For $p>1$ the integral diverges, and $p=1$ gives $-\ln\varepsilon\to\infty$. The same power can therefore converge at zero and diverge at infinity, or the reverse. The endpoint under study is essential.

For example, $\int_0^1x^{-1/2}\,dx=2$, although its integrand is unbounded. An infinite height at one endpoint alone does not force infinite area.

## Comparison and absolute convergence

Suppose $0\leq f(x)\leq g(x)$ for all sufficiently large $x$, and both are integrable on finite intervals in question. If $\int g$ converges at infinity, the integrals of $f$ are increasing and bounded above, so $\int f$ converges. If $\int f$ diverges to infinity, then $\int g$ also diverges. Comparison only needs to hold near the problematic endpoint; changing an ordinary finite portion cannot affect convergence.

For $x\geq1$, $0<1/(x^2+1)\leq1/x^2$. The convergent power test therefore proves $\int_1^\infty dx/(x^2+1)$ converges. Its actual value follows from arctangent: $\pi/2-\pi/4=\pi/4$.

For signed functions, convergence of $\int|f|$ implies convergence of $\int f$; this is called **absolute convergence**. Oscillation can permit convergence without absolute convergence, but positive comparison bounds do not by themselves establish such a signed limit.

## Cancellation does not repair divergence

For $f(x)=1/x$ on $[-1,1]$, the two one-sided integrals diverge with opposite signs. The improper integral does not exist. Symmetric truncation gives $\int_{-1}^{-\varepsilon}dx/x+\int_\varepsilon^1dx/x=0$, but its limit is a **Cauchy principal value**, a different definition. Never replace two required limits by one linked limit without stating this change.

## Practice

1. Evaluate $\int_2^\infty x^{-3}\,dx$ by its definition.
2. Does $\int_0^1(1-x)^{-2}\,dx$ converge?

## Worked answers

1. On $[2,R]$, the integral is $[-1/(2x^2)]_2^R=1/8-1/(2R^2)$. The final term tends to zero, so the integral converges to $1/8$.
2. Truncate at $1-\varepsilon$. Since an antiderivative is $(1-x)^{-1}$, the truncated value is $1/\varepsilon-1$. This tends to positive infinity, so the integral diverges. A finite upper endpoint does not guarantee a finite integral.
