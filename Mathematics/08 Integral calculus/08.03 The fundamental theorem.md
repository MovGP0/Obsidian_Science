> [!TLDR]
> For a continuous integrand, differentiating accumulated area recovers the integrand, and an antiderivative evaluates a definite integral.

## Differentiate accumulation

Let $f$ be continuous on a closed interval $[a,b]$. Define its **accumulation function** by

$$
A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt,\qquad a\leq x\leq b.
$$

The letter $t$ is the integration variable; $x$ is the variable upper bound. The first part of the fundamental theorem states that $A$ is differentiable for $a<x<b$ and

$$
A'(x)=f(x).
$$

To see why, subtract two accumulated values. Additivity of integrals gives

$$
\frac{A(x+h)-A(x)}h=\frac1h\int_x^{x+h}f(t)\,dt.
$$

This is the average value of $f$ across a short interval, with the usual orientation if $h<0$. Subtract $f(x)$ and use continuity:

$$
\left|\frac{A(x+h)-A(x)}h-f(x)\right|
\leq\sup_{t\text{ between }x\text{ and }x+h}|f(t)-f(x)|\longrightarrow0.
$$

The supremum tends to zero because all these points approach $x$. This proves the derivative claim. At $a$ and $b$, the matching one-sided derivatives hold. A nonnegative integrand makes $A$ nondecreasing; a negative value of $f$ makes $A$ locally decrease.

## Evaluate with an antiderivative

Suppose $F'=f$ on the interval, with $F$ continuous at the endpoints. Both $F$ and $A$ have derivative $f$ in the interior. Their difference has derivative zero and is therefore constant by the mean value theorem. Since $A(a)=0$, this constant is $F(a)$. Consequently,

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a)=[F(x)]_a^b.
$$

The bracket notation means “value at the upper bound minus value at the lower bound.” A constant of integration cancels: $(F(b)+C)-(F(a)+C)=F(b)-F(a)$. This theorem joins the sum-based definition in [[08.01 Definite integrals]] to the functions in [[08.02 Antiderivatives]]. It is an evaluation rule with hypotheses, not permission to subtract values across a singularity.

## Worked example: two changing bounds

Define

$$
H(x)=\int_{x^2}^{2x}e^{t^2}\,dt.
$$

There is no need to find an elementary antiderivative of $e^{t^2}$. Let $A(u)=\int_0^u e^{t^2}\,dt$. Additivity gives $H(x)=A(2x)-A(x^2)$. Apply the chain rule to each term:

$$
H'(x)=A'(2x)\cdot2-A'(x^2)\cdot2x
=2e^{4x^2}-2xe^{x^4}.
$$

In general, for continuous $f$ and differentiable bounds $u,v$,

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\int_{u(x)}^{v(x)}f(t)\,dt
=f(v(x))v'(x)-f(u(x))u'(x).
$$

The lower bound contributes a minus sign. Each moving bound also contributes its own derivative. This formula assumes that $f$ depends on $t$ alone. An integrand that also depends explicitly on $x$ needs a further rule and conditions.

For a direct evaluation, take $\int_1^2(3x^2-2)\,dx$. An antiderivative is $F(x)=x^3-2x$, so the value is $(8-4)-(1-2)=5$. Differentiate $F$ first to check that the chosen expression is correct.

## Conditions and common errors

Continuity is a convenient sufficient hypothesis. More general versions exist, but a Riemann integrable function need not equal the derivative of its accumulation function at each discontinuity. For example, changing a function at a single point does not change its integral, although it changes that point value.

Do not evaluate $\int_{-1}^1x^{-2}\,dx$ by subtracting values of $-1/x$. The integrand is undefined at zero, so the continuous-interval theorem does not apply. [[08.07 Improper integrals]] supplies the needed separate limits, which diverge in this example.

## Practice

1. Differentiate $B(x)=\int_1^{x^3}\cos t\,dt$.
2. Evaluate $\int_0^1(2x+1)^2\,dx$ by first expanding the integrand.

## Worked answers

1. Define $A(u)=\int_1^u\cos t\,dt$. Then $B=A\circ(x\mapsto x^3)$. The theorem and chain rule give $B'(x)=\cos(x^3)\,3x^2$. Omitting $3x^2$ would treat the upper bound as $x$.
2. Expand $(2x+1)^2=4x^2+4x+1$. An antiderivative is $4x^3/3+2x^2+x$. Endpoint subtraction gives $4/3+2+1=13/3$, since the value at zero is zero.
