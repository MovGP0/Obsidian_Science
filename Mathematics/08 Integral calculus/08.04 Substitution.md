title: "Substitution in integrals"

> [!TLDR]
> Substitution reverses the chain rule; change the integrand, differential, and definite bounds together.

## Derive the rule

Suppose $f$ is continuous on an interval containing the values of a continuously differentiable function $g$. If $F'=f$, the chain rule gives

$$
\frac{d}{dx}F(g(x))=f(g(x))g'(x).
$$

Reversing this equation gives the substitution rule

$$
\int f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx=F(g(x))+C.
$$

The notation $u=g(x)$ and $du=g'(x)\,dx$ records this derivative factor. It is a compact way to apply the chain rule. One must identify both the inner function and its derivative. Replacing a complicated expression by $u$ without changing the rest of the integrand does not produce a valid integral.

For a definite integral, apply the fundamental theorem to the same composite function:

$$
\int_a^b f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx
=F(g(b))-F(g(a))
=\int_{g(a)}^{g(b)}f(u)\,du.
$$

Here $g$ is continuously differentiable on $[a,b]$ and $f$ is continuous on a suitable interval. This form does not require $g$ to be one-to-one. The derivative factor accounts for any reversals. When solving $x$ as a function of $u$ instead, one must select a branch on which this inverse is valid.

## Worked example: transform every part

Evaluate $I=\int_0^1 2x\cos(x^2+1)\,dx$. Set $u=x^2+1$. Then $du=2x\,dx$. At $x=0$, $u=1$; at $x=1$, $u=2$. Thus

$$
I=\int_1^2\cos u\,du=[\sin u]_1^2=\sin2-\sin1.
$$

After the substitution, the entire integral is written in $u$. The original bounds $0,1$ would be incorrect beside the new integrand. An alternative is to keep the calculation indefinite, obtain $\sin(x^2+1)$, and then use the original bounds. Both methods give the same number, but their variables and bounds must not be mixed.

A constant factor can be supplied outside an integral. For example,

$$
\int x\sqrt{x^2+4}\,dx
=\frac12\int u^{1/2}\,du
=\frac13u^{3/2}+C
=\frac13(x^2+4)^{3/2}+C.
$$

Here $u=x^2+4$ and $du=2x\,dx$, so $x\,dx=du/2$. Differentiating the final answer gives $(1/3)(3/2)(x^2+4)^{1/2}(2x)=x\sqrt{x^2+4}$, which checks the factor.

## Orientation and domain

If the new variable decreases as the old variable increases, the new bounds occur in reverse order. For instance, set $u=1-x$ in $\int_0^1 2(1-x)\,dx$. Then $du=-dx$ and

$$
\int_0^1 2(1-x)\,dx=-\int_1^0 2u\,du=\int_0^1 2u\,du=1.
$$

The minus sign and reversed limits are two parts of one consistent transformation. Keeping the minus sign while also silently keeping the old orientation would give the wrong sign.

Check logarithms and roots carefully. The substitution $u=x^2$ does not allow the unqualified statement $x=\sqrt u$ on a negative interval. On $x<0$, the inverse is $x=-\sqrt u$. If the transformation crosses branches, split the interval where needed. Also, replacing $x$ by an expression does not remove a singularity from the original domain; improper limits must still be considered.

## Choosing a substitution

Look for a composite expression and a factor proportional to its derivative. Powers of a linear expression, logarithmic derivatives $g'/g$, and expressions such as $x\cos(x^2)$ are common cases. A substitution is useful when it makes the new integral simpler and keeps its domain clear. If it makes the expression more complex, another method, such as [[08.05 Integration by parts]], can be more suitable.

## Practice

1. Evaluate $\int_0^2 x/(x^2+1)\,dx$.
2. Find $\int\sin(3x)\,dx$ and verify its coefficient.

## Worked answers

1. Set $u=x^2+1$, so $du=2x\,dx$ and the bounds become $1,5$. Therefore the integral is $(1/2)\int_1^5u^{-1}\,du=(1/2)[\ln u]_1^5=(1/2)\ln5$. The logarithm has a positive argument throughout.
2. Set $u=3x$, so $dx=du/3$. The integral is $-(1/3)\cos u+C=-(1/3)\cos(3x)+C$. Its derivative is $-(1/3)(-\sin(3x))3=\sin(3x)$.
