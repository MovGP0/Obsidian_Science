> [!TLDR]
> Simplify an integrand, select a known derivative pattern, and verify the resulting antiderivative by differentiation.

## Basic patterns

An elementary integral can often be evaluated by reversing familiar derivative rules. Work on an interval where the integrand and proposed antiderivative are defined. Constants are independent of the integration variable. Linearity gives

$$
\int(\alpha f+\beta g)\,dx=\alpha\int f\,dx+\beta\int g\,dx,
$$

with one arbitrary constant included in the final expression. For powers, exponentials, and elementary trigonometric functions, the main patterns are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int x^r\,dx&=\frac{x^{r+1}}{r+1}+C &&(r\ne-1),\\
\int\frac1x\,dx&=\ln|x|+C &&(x\ne0),\\
\int e^{kx}\,dx&=\frac1k e^{kx}+C &&(k\ne0),\\
\int\cos(kx)\,dx&=\frac1k\sin(kx)+C &&(k\ne0),\\
\int\sin(kx)\,dx&=-\frac1k\cos(kx)+C &&(k\ne0).
\end{aligned}
$$

The power rule requires a real differentiable branch; use $x>0$ if the exponent is an arbitrary real number. Each factor $1/k$ compensates for the chain rule. When $k=0$, treat the integrand directly: for example, $e^{0x}=1$ integrates to $x+C$. Do not divide by zero in a formula whose hypothesis excludes it.

For $a>0$, $a\ne1$, write $a^x=e^{x\ln a}$ to obtain $\int a^x\,dx=a^x/\ln a+C$. Further useful patterns are $\int\sec^2x\,dx=\tan x+C$ and $\int(1+x^2)^{-1}\,dx=\arctan x+C$.

## Algebra before integration

Expand a polynomial and integrate its terms. For a rational function $P(x)/Q(x)$, first compare degrees. If the numerator degree is at least the denominator degree, polynomial division separates a polynomial part from a proper fraction. Factor the denominator and use partial fractions when possible. The identity is an algebraic equality only where the original denominator is nonzero.

For trigonometric powers, an identity can expose an elementary pattern. Since $\sin^2x=(1-\cos2x)/2$,

$$
\int\sin^2x\,dx=\frac x2-\frac{\sin2x}{4}+C.
$$

The square of a sine is not integrated by squaring an antiderivative of sine. Integration is linear over sums, but does not preserve multiplication or composition. For a composite pattern, use [[08.04 Substitution]]; for products such as $xe^x$, use [[08.05 Integration by parts]].

## Worked example: partial fractions

Find an antiderivative of $(3x+5)/((x+1)(x+2))$. Seek constants $A,B$ such that

$$
\frac{3x+5}{(x+1)(x+2)}=\frac A{x+1}+\frac B{x+2}.
$$

Multiply by the denominator to obtain $3x+5=A(x+2)+B(x+1)$. Comparing coefficients gives $A+B=3$ and $2A+B=5$. Subtract the first equation from the second: $A=2$, then $B=1$. Therefore

$$
\int\frac{3x+5}{(x+1)(x+2)}\,dx
=2\ln|x+1|+\ln|x+2|+C.
$$

This holds on each interval separated by $-2$ and $-1$. Differentiation gives $2/(x+1)+1/(x+2)$, which combines back to the original fraction. The absolute values are necessary if a selected interval has negative factors.

Not every quadratic denominator factors over the reals. For example, set $u=x/2$ in $\int dx/(x^2+4)$. Since $dx=2\,du$ and $x^2+4=4(u^2+1)$, the answer is $\tfrac12\arctan(x/2)+C$. Completing the square can reduce other positive quadratic denominators to this pattern.

## A reliable sequence

First state excluded points and any branch choices. Then simplify the expression before selecting a method. After integration, differentiate the full proposed answer, including coefficients and inner functions. For a definite integral, subtract endpoint values only after confirming that the integrand is continuous on the whole interval. A formula can be algebraically correct on each side of a pole without permitting evaluation across the pole.

## Practice

1. Find $\int(3x^2-4e^{2x}+2\cos x)\,dx$.
2. Evaluate $\int_0^1(x+1)/(x^2+2x+2)\,dx$.

## Worked answers

1. Integrate the terms separately to get $x^3-2e^{2x}+2\sin x+C$. Its derivative is $3x^2-4e^{2x}+2\cos x$. In particular, the exponential term acquires a factor $2$ when differentiated.
2. The denominator derivative is $2x+2=2(x+1)$. Set $u=x^2+2x+2$, $du=2(x+1)\,dx$; the bounds become $2,5$. The integral is $\tfrac12\int_2^5du/u=\tfrac12\ln(5/2)$. The denominator equals $(x+1)^2+1$, so no singular point lies in the interval.
