> [!TLDR]
> An antiderivative has a specified derivative; an indefinite integral represents all such functions on an interval.

## Reverse the derivative

Let $f:I\to\mathbb R$ be a real function on an open interval $I$. A differentiable function $F$ is an **antiderivative** of $f$ on $I$ if

$$
F'(x)=f(x)\qquad\text{for every }x\in I.
$$

The indefinite integral denotes the family of antiderivatives:

$$
\int f(x)\,dx=F(x)+C,\qquad C\in\mathbb R.
$$

Here $C$ is independent of $x$. Differentiation removes this constant because $C'=0$. The expression is a family of functions, whereas a definite integral with fixed bounds is a number. The notation $dx$ specifies the variable of integration; other symbols can act as constant parameters.

For example, $F(x)=x^3$ has derivative $3x^2$. Every function $x^3+C$ therefore has the same derivative. To show these are all the possibilities on an interval, suppose $G'=F'$. Set $H=G-F$. Then $H'=0$. For any two points $u<v$ in the interval, the mean value theorem gives a point $c\in(u,v)$ with

$$
H(v)-H(u)=H'(c)(v-u)=0.
$$

Thus $H$ has the same value everywhere on the interval, so $G=F+C$. The interval assumption matters: on two disconnected intervals, the constants can be different.

## Basic rules and their domains

Reverse the power rule to obtain, for $r\ne-1$,

$$
\int x^r\,dx=\frac{x^{r+1}}{r+1}+C.
$$

Use an interval on which the real powers involved are defined and differentiable; $x>0$ is always suitable for real $r$. Integer powers can permit larger domains. The excluded exponent has a separate rule:

$$
\int\frac1x\,dx=\ln|x|+C,\qquad x\ne0.
$$

For $x>0$, this follows from $(\ln x)'=1/x$. For $x<0$, the chain rule gives $(\ln(-x))'=(-1)/(-x)=1/x$. An antiderivative on $\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}$ can use a constant $C_-$ for $x<0$ and an independent constant $C_+$ for $x>0$. One written $C$ commonly means that one interval has been selected.

Linearity follows directly from the derivative rule: if $F'=f$ and $G'=g$, then $(\alpha F+\beta G)'=\alpha f+\beta g$ for constants $\alpha,\beta$. It permits termwise integration of finite sums. There is no corresponding rule that integrates a product by integrating each factor.

## Worked example: determine the constant

Find $F$ such that $F'(x)=6x^2-4x+3$ and $F(1)=5$. Integrate each term:

$$
F(x)=2x^3-2x^2+3x+C.
$$

Substitute $x=1$ into the additional condition:

$$
5=2-2+3+C=3+C,
\qquad C=2.
$$

Therefore $F(x)=2x^3-2x^2+3x+2$. Differentiate the proposed answer to check the equation: $F'=6x^2-4x+3$. Then check the value separately: $F(1)=5$. The derivative equation determines the shape of the graph, and the point condition selects its vertical position.

An additional condition is essential for a unique answer. Without it, no one value of $C$ has priority. Conversely, a derivative and an incompatible set of point conditions can have no solution. If $F'=0$, for example, requiring $F(0)=1$ and $F(1)=2$ is impossible on a single interval.

## Existence and practical limits

Every continuous function on an interval has an antiderivative, as [[08.03 The fundamental theorem]] shows. This does not mean that an elementary formula exists. The function $e^{-x^2}$ has an antiderivative defined by an integral, even though elementary functions do not express it. Finding no familiar formula is not a proof that no antiderivative exists.

## Practice

1. Find all antiderivatives of $4x^3-2/x$ on $x>0$ and verify the result.
2. Find $G$ on $(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$ if $G'(x)=\sec^2x$ and $G(0)=-3$.

## Worked answers

1. The power and logarithm rules give $F(x)=x^4-2\ln x+C$. Its derivative is $4x^3-2/x$. All other antiderivatives differ by a constant because $(0,\infty)$ is an interval.
2. Since $(\tan x)'=\sec^2x$, write $G(x)=\tan x+C$. The condition gives $-3=0+C$, so $G(x)=\tan x-3$. The interval excludes the poles of tangent, which are not allowed in the derivative equation.
