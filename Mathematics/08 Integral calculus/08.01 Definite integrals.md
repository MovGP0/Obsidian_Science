title: "Definite integrals"

> [!TLDR]
> A definite integral is the limit of sums over small intervals; it measures signed accumulation between two bounds.

## From a partition to an integral

Let $f$ be a bounded real function on the closed interval $[a,b]$, with $a<b$. A **partition** is a finite ordered set of points

$$
P:\quad a=x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_n=b.
$$

The width of interval $i$ is $\Delta x_i=x_i-x_{i-1}$. Select a sample point $\xi_i$ in each interval $[x_{i-1},x_i]$. The associated **Riemann sum** is

$$
S(f,P,\xi)=\sum_{i=1}^n f(\xi_i)\Delta x_i.
$$

Each term is the height of one rectangle times its width. A negative height gives a negative term. The mesh $\lVert P\rVert=\max_i\Delta x_i$ is the largest width. To refine the entire partition, this largest width must tend to zero; adding points only near one endpoint is insufficient.

The function is **Riemann integrable** if all such sums tend to the same finite number $I$ as the mesh tends to zero, regardless of the sample points. We then write

$$
I=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx.
$$

More precisely, for every $\varepsilon>0$, there is a $\delta>0$ such that every tagged partition with $\lVert P\rVert<\delta$ has $|S-I|<\varepsilon$. Every continuous function on a closed bounded interval is Riemann integrable. A bounded function with only finitely many discontinuities is also integrable. Boundedness alone does not guarantee integrability.

## Signed area and bounds

If $f\geq0$, the integral is the area below the graph and above the horizontal axis. Where $f<0$, the contribution is negative. Thus the geometric area between the graph and axis is $\int_a^b|f(x)|\,dx$, which can differ from the signed integral.

![[math-08-signed-area.svg|**Fig. 08.1** The line y = x − 1 has a negative triangle on [0,1] and a positive triangle on [1,3]. Their signed contributions are −1/2 and 2.]]

The integration bounds have an order. Define

$$
\int_a^a f(x)\,dx=0,
\qquad
\int_b^a f(x)\,dx=-\int_a^b f(x)\,dx.
$$

For an intermediate point $c$, additivity gives $\int_a^b f=\int_a^c f+\int_c^b f$. With the orientation convention, this equation also holds when $c$ lies outside $[a,b]$, provided the required integrals exist. The letter inside the integral is a dummy variable: replacing $x$ with $t$ changes no value. The bounds and function determine the result.

## Worked example: evaluate a sum exactly

Find $\int_0^1x^2\,dx$ from right endpoint sums. Use $n$ equal intervals, so $\Delta x=1/n$ and $\xi_i=i/n$. Then

$$
S_n=\sum_{i=1}^n\left(\frac{i}{n}\right)^2\frac1n
=\frac{1}{n^3}\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}6
=\frac13+\frac1{2n}+\frac1{6n^2}.
$$

The last two terms tend to zero, so the limit is $1/3$. Continuity guarantees that other sample points give the same integral. A single right endpoint sum is an approximation, not the integral itself. Since $x^2$ increases on this interval, its right endpoint rectangles overestimate the area.

For the line in Fig. 08.1, elementary triangle areas give

$$
\int_0^3(x-1)\,dx=-\frac{1\cdot1}{2}+\frac{2\cdot2}{2}=\frac32.
$$

The total unsigned area is instead $1/2+2=5/2$. Changing the upper bound from $3$ to $1$ removes the positive triangle, so the integral becomes $-1/2$. Moving an upper bound to the right does not always increase an integral: its change depends on the sign of the added integrand.

## Practice

1. Use left endpoint sums to evaluate $\int_0^2x\,dx$. State whether the finite sums under- or overestimate the value.
2. Given $\int_1^4f(x)\,dx=7$ and $\int_1^2f(x)\,dx=3$, find $\int_4^2f(x)\,dx$.

## Worked answers

1. The width is $2/n$ and the left endpoints are $2i/n$ for $i=0,\ldots,n-1$. Hence $L_n=(4/n^2)\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}i=2(n-1)/n$. Its limit is $2$. Since $x$ increases, each left rectangle is below the graph; every finite sum underestimates the integral.
2. Additivity gives $7=3+\int_2^4f$, so $\int_2^4f=4$. Reversing the bounds changes the sign: $\int_4^2f=-4$. No assumption about the sign of $f$ is needed.

For a way to evaluate these limits through derivatives, see [[08.03 The fundamental theorem]].
