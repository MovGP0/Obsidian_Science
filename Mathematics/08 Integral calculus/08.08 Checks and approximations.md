title: "Checks and approximations for integrals"

> [!TLDR]
> Use derivatives, signs, symmetry, and numerical sums to test an integral and estimate its value with stated limits.

## Check the structure before the arithmetic

Let $I=\int_a^bf(x)\,dx$, with $a<b$ and $f$ Riemann integrable. An exact symbolic answer should satisfy basic constraints. If $f\geq0$, then $I\geq0$. If constants $m,M$ satisfy $m\leq f(x)\leq M$ throughout the interval, then

$$
m(b-a)\leq I\leq M(b-a).
$$

These follow by bounding every rectangle in the Riemann sums and taking limits. A result outside these bounds is wrong, regardless of how plausible its algebra looks. Reversing the bounds must reverse the sign; an interval of zero length must give zero.

For an indefinite answer $F(x)+C$, calculate $F'(x)$ and compare with the original integrand on its domain. This is a decisive local check of an antiderivative. For a definite answer obtained from $F(b)-F(a)$, also check subtraction order, endpoint substitution, and continuity across the interval. A correct derivative on separate intervals does not justify crossing a singular point.

## Symmetry reduces work

For an even integrable function, $f(-x)=f(x)$, substitution $u=-x$ gives

$$
\int_{-a}^a f(x)\,dx=2\int_0^af(x)\,dx.
$$

For an odd integrable function, $f(-x)=-f(x)$, the left and right contributions cancel, so the integral is zero. Both statements require a symmetric interval and an existing integral. The improper integral of $1/x$ across zero is not justified by oddness; its two one-sided integrals diverge.

For example, $x^3+\cos x$ is the sum of an odd and an even function. Therefore $\int_{-1}^1(x^3+\cos x)\,dx=2\int_0^1\cos x\,dx=2\sin1$. The cubic contribution vanishes before any endpoint arithmetic.

## Elementary numerical rules

Divide $[a,b]$ into $n$ equal pieces with width $h=(b-a)/n$ and endpoints $x_i=a+ih$. The left, right, midpoint, and trapezoidal approximations are

$$
\begin{aligned}
L_n&=h\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(x_i),&
R_n&=h\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_i),\\
M_n&=h\sum_{i=1}^{n}f\!\left(\frac{x_{i-1}+x_i}{2}\right),&
T_n&=\frac{L_n+R_n}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The trapezoidal rule uses a straight line between successive graph points. For increasing $f$, every left rectangle is below the graph and every right rectangle is above it, so $L_n\leq I\leq R_n$. Also $R_n-L_n=h(f(b)-f(a))$, since the interior terms cancel. This gives a certified interval for $I$ without finding an antiderivative.

## Worked example: compare four estimates

For $f(x)=x^2$ on $[0,1]$ with $n=4$, $h=1/4$. The endpoint values are $0,1/16,1/4,9/16,1$. Consequently,

$$
L_4=\frac14\left(0+\frac1{16}+\frac14+\frac9{16}\right)=\frac7{32},
\qquad R_4=\frac{15}{32}.
$$

Their average is $T_4=11/32$. For the midpoint rule, the points are $1/8,3/8,5/8,7/8$, so

$$
M_4=\frac14\frac{1+9+25+49}{64}=\frac{21}{64}.
$$

The exact value is $I=1/3$. Thus $L_4<I<R_4$, $M_4<I$, and $T_4>I$. Convexity explains the last two signs: chords lie above a convex graph, while the average of each interval is at least the midpoint value. These signs reverse for a concave graph.

If $f$ has a continuous second derivative with $|f''|\leq K$, standard error bounds are $|I-T_n|\leq K(b-a)h^2/12$ and $|I-M_n|\leq K(b-a)h^2/24$. For $x^2$, $K=2$, giving bounds $1/96$ and $1/192$, respectively; both are attained here. Such bounds need smoothness and a justified value of $K$. Merely obtaining similar decimal values with two meshes does not prove an error estimate.

## Practice

1. Evaluate $\int_{-2}^2(x^5+3x^2)\,dx$ with symmetry.
2. Bound $\int_0^1e^x\,dx$ with $L_2,R_2$, then give the trapezoidal estimate.

## Worked answers

1. The odd term $x^5$ contributes zero. The even term gives $2\int_0^2 3x^2\,dx=2[x^3]_0^2=16$. This is positive, as the remaining integrand is nonnegative.
2. With $h=1/2$, $L_2=(1+e^{1/2})/2$ and $R_2=(e^{1/2}+e)/2$. Since $e^x$ increases, these are lower and upper bounds. Their mean is $T_2=(1+2e^{1/2}+e)/4$. Convexity makes it an upper estimate of the exact value $e-1$; the left-right gap is $(e-1)/2$.
