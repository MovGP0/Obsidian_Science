> [!TLDR]
> A sequence converges when all sufficiently late terms lie as close as required to one fixed value.

## Terms and limits

A real sequence is a function from the positive integers to the real numbers. Write it as $(a_n)_{n\geq1}$, where $n$ is the index and $a_n$ is the term at that index. The list $1,1/2,1/3,\ldots$ has the rule $a_n=1/n$. An explicit rule gives each term directly. A recurrence gives a starting value and a rule that uses earlier terms.

The statement $a_n\to L$ means that the sequence has the finite limit $L$. Its precise definition is

$$
\text{for every }\varepsilon>0\text{ there is an integer }N\text{ such that }
n\geq N\implies |a_n-L|<\varepsilon.
$$

The number $\varepsilon$ is any permitted error. The index $N$ may depend on that error. The condition applies to **every** term after $N$, not just to a selected set of terms. A finite number of initial terms cannot change a limit.

For $a_n=1/n$, choose an integer $N>1/\varepsilon$. Then $n\geq N$ implies $|a_n-0|=1/n\leq1/N<\varepsilon$. Thus $1/n\to0$. A graph can suggest the limit, but this inequality proves it for every requested accuracy.

## Bounds and order

A sequence is bounded above if some real number $M$ satisfies $a_n\leq M$ for every $n$. It is bounded below if some $m$ satisfies $m\leq a_n$. It is bounded if both statements hold. It is nondecreasing if $a_{n+1}\geq a_n$ and nonincreasing if $a_{n+1}\leq a_n$. Here, “monotone” permits equal successive terms.

Every convergent real sequence is bounded. To see this, apply the limit definition with $\varepsilon=1$. All late terms lie between $L-1$ and $L+1$. The remaining initial terms form a finite set, so they also have a bound. A bounded sequence need not converge: $a_n=(-1)^n$ stays in $[-1,1]$, but its odd terms equal $-1$ and its even terms equal $1$.

The monotone convergence theorem states that a bounded monotone real sequence converges. For a nondecreasing sequence, let $L$ be the least upper bound of its terms. For every $\varepsilon>0$, some term exceeds $L-\varepsilon$; otherwise $L-\varepsilon$ would be a smaller upper bound. All later terms lie between that term and $L$, which proves convergence. The nonincreasing case uses the greatest lower bound.

## Worked example: a recurrence

Let $a_1=0$ and $a_{n+1}=(a_n+2)/2$. First prove $0\leq a_n<2$ by induction. It holds for $a_1$. If it holds for $a_n$, then $0\leq(a_n+2)/2<2$, so it holds for $a_{n+1}$. Also,

$$
a_{n+1}-a_n=\frac{2-a_n}{2}>0.
$$

The sequence is increasing and bounded above. Therefore it has a limit $L$. Taking limits in the recurrence gives $L=(L+2)/2$, hence $L=2$. Solving this equation alone would not prove convergence: the bound and monotonicity justified taking the limit. In fact, induction gives $a_n=2-2^{2-n}$, which confirms the result directly.

## Divergence and common errors

The sequence $a_n=n$ grows beyond every fixed bound. We write $a_n\to+\infty$, but this is not convergence to a real number. The sequence $(-1)^n n$ has neither a finite limit nor one direction of infinite growth. Distinct subsequential limits also rule out convergence: every subsequence of a convergent sequence must have the same limit.

Do not infer convergence from a decreasing gap $|a_{n+1}-a_n|\to0$. For example, $\sqrt n$ has this property because its gap equals $1/(\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt n)$, yet it is unbounded. Limits of sums concern partial sums, a separate sequence introduced in [[09.02 Infinite series]].

## Practice

1. Prove that $a_n=n/(n+1)$ increases to $1$, and find a valid $N$ for error $0.01$.
2. Decide whether $b_n=(-1)^n+1/n$ is bounded and whether it converges.

## Worked answers

1. Rewrite $a_n=1-1/(n+1)$. The subtracted fraction decreases, so $a_n$ increases, and $a_n<1$. Its distance from $1$ is $1/(n+1)$. With $N=100$, every $n\geq N$ gives error at most $1/101<0.01$. The same argument with $N>1/\varepsilon$ proves the limit for arbitrary error.
2. Since $0<1/n\leq1$ and $|(-1)^n|=1$, we have $|b_n|\leq2$. It is bounded. Its even subsequence tends to $1$, whereas its odd subsequence tends to $-1$. If the full sequence converged, both subsequences would have the same limit. Therefore it does not converge.
