> [!TLDR]
> Distinguish convergent infinite processes from finite approximations and control the error of a truncated expansion.

## Scope

A sequence describes an ordered set of values; a series describes the limit of partial sums. Taylor expansions and other series are useful only when their convergence or approximation error is understood.

## Starting knowledge

Use elementary functions from [[Functions - Overview]], derivatives from [[Differential calculus - Overview]], and definite and improper integrals from [[Integral calculus - Overview]].

## Topics to cover

- [[09.01 Sequences|Sequences]]: limits, boundedness, monotonicity, and examples that fail to converge.
- [[09.02 Infinite series|Infinite series]]: partial sums, geometric series, and the difference between convergence of terms and convergence of their sum.
- [[09.03 Convergence tests|Convergence tests]]: comparison, ratio, and integral tests, including their limits of applicability.
- [[09.04 Absolute and conditional convergence|Absolute and conditional convergence]]: explain why rearrangement is not harmless for every series.
- [[09.05 Power series|Power series]]: centre, radius of convergence, endpoint checks, and operations within a justified interval.
- [[09.06 Taylor and Maclaurin expansions|Taylor and Maclaurin expansions]]: construct coefficients from derivatives and expand common elementary functions.
- [[09.07 Remainders and error|Remainders and error]]: estimate a truncation error and state the small parameter and the approximation order.
- [[09.08 Asymptotic notation|Asymptotic notation]]: distinguish a local finite-order approximation from a claim that an infinite Taylor series equals its function.

## Learning outcomes

After studying this topic, you should be able to:

- Calculate a geometric sum and state the condition under which its infinite form converges.
- Construct a Taylor polynomial and bound or estimate its remainder near the expansion point.
- Find a power series’ convergence interval, checking its endpoints separately.
