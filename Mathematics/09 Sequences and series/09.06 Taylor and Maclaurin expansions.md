> [!TLDR]
> Taylor coefficients come from derivatives at a centre, but a remainder estimate is needed to prove equality with an infinite series.

## Match derivatives at a point

Let a function $f$ have derivatives through order $m$ at a real centre $a$. Seek a polynomial

$$
P_m(x)=c_0+c_1(x-a)+\cdots+c_m(x-a)^m
$$

whose first $m$ derivatives agree with those of $f$ at $a$. Substitution gives $P_m(a)=c_0$. Differentiation gives $P_m'(a)=c_1$, $P_m''(a)=2!c_2$, and, in general, $P_m^{(k)}(a)=k!c_k$. Higher powers vanish at $a$ after $k$ derivatives, and lower powers have already differentiated to zero. Therefore

$$
P_m(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k.
$$

Here $k!=1\cdot2\cdots k$, with $0!=1$, and $f^{(0)}=f$. This is the Taylor polynomial of degree at most $m$. Its degree may be smaller if a coefficient vanishes. When $a=0$, it is called a Maclaurin polynomial. If derivatives of all orders exist, the same coefficients define a formal Taylor series. A further argument must show that the remainder $f(x)-P_m(x)$ tends to zero before the infinite series can be identified with $f$.

## Common expansions and their domains

All derivatives of $e^x$ equal $e^x$, so their values at $0$ are $1$. This gives

$$
e^x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^n}{n!},\qquad x\in\mathbb R.
$$

Taylor's remainder theorem proves this equality: for fixed $x$, the magnitude of the remainder after degree $m$ is at most $e^{|x|}|x|^{m+1}/(m+1)!$, which tends to zero. The factorial grows faster than this fixed power, as its successive ratio tends to zero.

The derivatives of sine cycle through $\sin x,\cos x,-\sin x,-\cos x$. Their values at zero therefore give

$$
\sin x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n x^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!},
\qquad
\cos x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n x^{2n}}{(2n)!}.
$$

Both identities hold for all real $x$, since all derivatives have magnitude at most $1$ and the Taylor remainder tends to zero. Odd and even powers reflect the odd symmetry of sine and the even symmetry of cosine.

Integrating the geometric series for $1/(1+x)$ inside $|x|<1$ gives

$$
\log(1+x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}x^n}{n},
\qquad |x|<1.
$$

Its radius is $1$, although the real logarithm exists for all $x>-1$. The series converges at $x=1$ and diverges at $x=-1$. Proving the value at $x=1$ needs an endpoint argument; the open-interval derivation is explained in [[09.05 Power series]]. A function's domain and its Taylor series' convergence interval are not the same thing.

## Worked example: move the centre

Find the cubic Taylor polynomial of $f(x)=\log x$ at $a=1$. Its derivatives are

$$
f'(x)=\frac1x,\quad f''(x)=-\frac1{x^2},\quad
f'''(x)=\frac2{x^3}.
$$

At $1$, the function value is $0$ and these derivatives are $1,-1,2$. Divide each by the corresponding factorial:

$$
P_3(x)=(x-1)-\frac{(x-1)^2}{2}+\frac{(x-1)^3}{3}.
$$

At $x=1.1$, set $h=x-1=0.1$. Then $P_3(1.1)=0.1-0.005+0.000333\ldots=0.095333\ldots$. Since $f^{(4)}(x)=-6/x^4$, its magnitude on $[1,1.1]$ is at most $6$. The remainder magnitude is at most $6(0.1)^4/4!=0.000025$. This converts a polynomial calculation into a justified approximation.

## Limitations and errors

Do not omit the factorial or use powers of $x$ when the centre is $a\ne0$. A finite Taylor polynomial is local information. Even infinitely many derivatives do not by themselves establish equality with the infinite Taylor series; a smooth counterexample is given in [[09.08 Asymptotic notation]]. Error control and the meaning of approximation order appear in [[09.07 Remainders and error]].

## Practice

1. Find the degree-four Maclaurin polynomial of $\cos(2x)$.
2. Find the quadratic Taylor polynomial of $e^x$ about $a=1$ and bound its error at $x=1.1$ using $e^{1.1}<4$.

## Worked answers

1. Start from $\cos u=1-u^2/2!+u^4/4!+\cdots$ and substitute $u=2x$. The polynomial is $1-2x^2+(2/3)x^4$. The factors $2^2$ and $2^4$ must be applied to the entire powers; substituting only into the numerator's symbol would miss them.
2. Each derivative at $1$ equals $e$. Hence $P_2(x)=e[1+(x-1)+(x-1)^2/2]$, and $P_2(1.1)=1.105e$. On $[1,1.1]$, the third derivative is below $4$. The remainder is therefore less than $4(0.1)^3/3!=1/1500$, about $0.000667$.
