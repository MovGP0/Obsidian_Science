> [!TLDR]
> Asymptotic notation describes limiting error or relative size; a finite-order local expansion does not assert equality with an infinite series.

## State the limiting regime

Let $f$ and $g$ be real functions near a point $a$, with $g(x)\ne0$ in a sufficiently small punctured neighborhood. The notation

$$
f(x)=O(g(x))\quad(x\to a)
$$

means that there are constants $C>0$ and $\delta>0$ such that $|f(x)|\leq C|g(x)|$ whenever $0<|x-a|<\delta$. This is an eventual upper bound up to a fixed factor. The constant cannot depend on $x$. For one-sided limits, restrict to that side; for $x\to\infty$, replace the neighborhood condition with $x>A$.

The notation $f(x)=o(g(x))$ means $f(x)/g(x)\to0$. Thus little-$o$ says that $f$ becomes negligible relative to $g$, a stronger conclusion than a bounded ratio. Finally, $f(x)\sim g(x)$ means $f(x)/g(x)\to1$. This states relative equivalence, and is stronger than an upper bound.

For example, as $h\to0$, $3h^2+h^3=O(h^2)$ because the ratio in absolute value is $|3+h|$, which is at most $4$ for $|h|<1$. It is not $o(h^2)$ because the ratio tends to $3$. It is $o(h)$ because $(3h^2+h^3)/h=3h+h^2\to0$. The limiting variable and the comparison scale are part of every statement.

## Finite expansions

An equation such as

$$
e^h=1+h+\frac{h^2}{2}+O(h^3)\quad(h\to0)
$$

means that the difference between $e^h$ and the displayed polynomial has magnitude at most $C|h|^3$ for all sufficiently small $h$. It does not specify the error at $h=2$, nor does it provide a numerical constant unless one is proved. Taylor's theorem gives a choice: on $|h|\leq1$, the third derivative has magnitude at most $e$, so the error is at most $e|h|^3/6$.

The symbol $O(h^3)$ represents a class of possible functions, not one fixed number. Its use with an equals sign is conventional and is not a symmetric equality. For $|h|<1$, an $O(h^4)$ error is also $O(h^3)$, but that weaker statement loses useful information. Rules such as $O(h^p)+O(h^q)=O(h^{\min(p,q)})$ require the same limiting regime. Cancellation may produce a smaller error than this guaranteed bound.

## Worked example: division of expansions

Find a quadratic expansion of $e^h/(1+h)$ as $h\to0$. Restrict to $|h|<1/2$, so the denominator stays away from zero. A finite geometric identity gives

$$
\frac1{1+h}=1-h+h^2-\frac{h^3}{1+h}
=1-h+h^2+O(h^3).
$$

The final bound holds because $1/|1+h|\leq2$. Multiply by $e^h=1+h+h^2/2+O(h^3)$. The constant and linear coefficients are $1$ and $-1+1=0$. The quadratic coefficient is $1-1+1/2=1/2$. Bounded polynomial factors preserve the error order, so

$$
\frac{e^h}{1+h}=1+\frac{h^2}{2}+O(h^3).
$$

This also proves $e^h/(1+h)-1\sim h^2/2$, since division by $h^2/2$ leaves $1+O(h)\to1$. The equivalence concerns the difference from $1$, not the full function's leading constant alone.

## Smooth does not mean equal to a Taylor series

Define $f(0)=0$ and $f(x)=e^{-1/x^2}$ for $x\ne0$. For every nonnegative integer $m$,

$$
\frac{e^{-1/x^2}}{|x|^m}\longrightarrow0\quad(x\to0).
$$

To justify this, put $t=1/x^2\to\infty$. Choose an integer $k>m/2$. Since $e^t\geq t^k/k!$ for $t>0$, we get $0\leq t^{m/2}e^{-t}\leq k!t^{m/2-k}\to0$.

For $x\ne0$, each derivative of $f$ has the form $P(1/x)e^{-1/x^2}$ for a polynomial $P$. Differentiation preserves this form. The displayed decay shows that each such expression tends to zero. Inductively, the derivative at zero also exists and equals zero, since its difference quotient adds only one further factor $1/x$. Thus $f$ is infinitely differentiable with $f^{(n)}(0)=0$ for every $n$.

Its Taylor series at zero is identically zero, but $f(x)>0$ at every nonzero $x$. It has a valid zero polynomial approximation to every finite power order without equalling its Taylor series on any neighborhood. An analytic function, by contrast, equals its Taylor series near the centre. See [[09.06 Taylor and Maclaurin expansions]] and [[09.07 Remainders and error]] for cases where a remainder bound establishes equality.

## Practice

1. Decide whether $h^3=O(h^2)$ and whether $h^3=o(h^2)$ as $h\to0$.
2. Find the leading behavior of $(\sin h-h)/h^3$ as $h\to0$ through nonzero values.

## Worked answers

1. Both statements hold. For $|h|<1$, $|h^3|\leq|h^2|$, so $C=1$ proves big-$O$. Also $h^3/h^2=h\to0$, which proves little-$o$. At infinity the first statement would fail, showing why the regime must be stated.
2. Taylor's theorem gives $\sin h=h-h^3/6+O(h^5)$. Subtract $h$ and divide by $h^3$ to obtain $-1/6+O(h^2)$. The error tends to zero, so the limit is $-1/6$. Equivalently, $\sin h-h\sim-h^3/6$.
