title: "Absolute and conditional convergence"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Absolute convergence permits arbitrary rearrangement of a series; conditional convergence can make the sum depend on the order.

## Two types of convergence

A real or complex series $\sum a_n$ is absolutely convergent if $\sum|a_n|$ converges. It is conditionally convergent if $\sum a_n$ converges but $\sum|a_n|$ diverges. A divergent series belongs to neither class. These definitions compare two separate sequences of partial sums; taking absolute values after the sum is not the same as summing absolute values.

Absolute convergence implies convergence. For $m>n$, the triangle inequality gives

$$
\left|\sum_{k=n+1}^{m}a_k\right|
\leq\sum_{k=n+1}^{m}|a_k|.
$$

If the absolute series converges, its tails become arbitrarily small. The displayed inequality gives the same property for the original partial sums. They form a Cauchy sequence, so they converge in $\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$. Completeness of the number system is used at this last step. Methods to test the absolute series appear in [[09.03 Convergence tests]].

## Alternating series

Suppose $b_n\geq0$, $b_{n+1}\leq b_n$, and $b_n\to0$. Then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n+1}b_n$ converges. Its even partial sums increase because

$$
S_{2m+2}-S_{2m}=b_{2m+1}-b_{2m+2}\geq0.
$$

Its odd partial sums decrease. Every even partial sum is at most every later odd partial sum, and their gap $S_{2m+1}-S_{2m}=b_{2m+1}$ tends to zero. The two subsequences therefore approach the same limit $S$. This argument also proves the alternating-series error estimate

$$
|S-S_N|\leq b_{N+1}.
$$

The remainder has the sign of the first omitted term. Alternating signs alone are insufficient: the decreasing-magnitude and zero-limit conditions justify this version of the test and its error bound.

The alternating harmonic series $1-1/2+1/3-1/4+\cdots$ converges by this test. Its absolute series is the divergent harmonic series, so its convergence is conditional. In contrast, $\sum(-1)^{n+1}/n^2$ converges absolutely because $\sum1/n^2$ converges.

## Why the order can matter

A rearrangement uses each original term exactly once but changes their order. For an absolutely convergent series with sum $S$, choose $N$ so that the absolute tail after $N$ is below $\varepsilon$. A sufficiently late rearranged partial sum contains all first $N$ original terms. The difference between that sum and $S$ consists only of omitted tail terms, with magnitude at most their total absolute sum. It is therefore below $\varepsilon$. Every rearrangement has sum $S$.

For a conditionally convergent real series, the positive terms have infinite total and the negative terms have total $-\infty$. To see why, write $a_n=p_n-q_n$, with $p_n=\max(a_n,0)$ and $q_n=\max(-a_n,0)$. If one of $\sum p_n$ and $\sum q_n$ were finite, convergence of their difference would force the other to be finite as well. Their sum $\sum|a_n|$ would then converge, a contradiction.

Thus one can take unused positive terms until their running sum exceeds any chosen real target $T$, then unused negative terms until it falls below $T$, and repeat. Use each sign's terms in their original order. The steps tend to zero because the original terms tend to zero. Overshoots and undershoots therefore tend to zero; the rearranged sum is $T$. This explains the rearrangement theorem for real conditional series. The claim concerns reordering terms, not changing their values.

## Worked example: a changed harmonic sum

Let the alternating harmonic sum be $S$. Rearrange it in blocks with two positive terms followed by one negative term:

$$
1+\frac13-\frac12+\frac15+\frac17-\frac14+\cdots.
$$

Let $H_m=\sum_{k=1}^m1/k$. After $m$ blocks the sum is

$$
T_{3m}=H_{4m}-\frac12H_{2m}-\frac12H_m
=\left(H_{4m}-\frac12H_{2m}\right)-\frac12H_m.
$$

The original partial sum satisfies $S_{2m}=H_{2m}-H_m$. Consequently,

$$
T_{3m}=S_{4m}+\frac12S_{2m}\longrightarrow\frac32S.
$$

The one or two terms between block endpoints tend to zero, so the full rearranged sequence has this limit. Moreover $S\geq S_2=1/2>0$, so $3S/2\ne S$. The calculation proves an actual change without needing to identify $S$ as $\log2$.

## Practice

1. Classify $\sum(-1)^{n+1}/\sqrt n$.
2. How many terms of $\sum(-1)^{n+1}/n^2$ suffice for error below $10^{-4}$?

## Worked answers

1. The magnitudes $1/\sqrt n$ decrease to zero, so the alternating test proves convergence. Their positive series is a $p$-series with $p=1/2\leq1$, which diverges. Thus the original series is conditionally convergent.
2. The alternating estimate gives error at most $1/(N+1)^2$. Choose $N=100$: the bound is $1/101^2<10^{-4}$. The choice $N=99$ gives a bound equal to $10^{-4}$, so it does not establish the requested strict inequality.
