> [!TLDR]
> A power series converges inside a radius about its centre; each finite endpoint requires a separate check.

## Centre and radius

A real power series has the form

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n(x-a)^n,
$$

where $c_n$ are fixed coefficients, $a$ is the centre, and $x$ is the variable. The term with $n=0$ is the constant $c_0$, including at $x=a$. For each fixed $x$, this expression is an ordinary numerical series. Changing $x$ can change whether it converges.

There is a radius $R\in[0,\infty]$ such that the series converges absolutely for $|x-a|<R$ and diverges for $|x-a|>R$. At $|x-a|=R$, either behavior can occur. If $R=0$, only the centre is guaranteed. If $R=\infty$, convergence holds for every real $x$. When the limit $L=\lim|c_{n+1}/c_n|$ exists with $0<L<\infty$, the ratio test gives $R=1/L$.

The reason for the interior interval is comparison with a geometric series. If the series converges at a point $b\ne a$, its terms $c_n(b-a)^n$ are bounded by some $M$. For $|x-a|<|b-a|$,

$$
|c_n(x-a)^n|\leq M\left|\frac{x-a}{b-a}\right|^n.
$$

The ratio on the right is below $1$, so absolute convergence follows. The full radius theorem formalizes the boundary between such interior and exterior points.

## Worked example: both endpoints

Consider

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(x-2)^n}{n3^n}.
$$

For fixed $x\ne2$, the ratio of successive term magnitudes is

$$
\frac{|x-2|}{3}\frac{n}{n+1}\longrightarrow\frac{|x-2|}{3}.
$$

It converges absolutely if $|x-2|<3$ and diverges if $|x-2|>3$. Thus the centre is $2$ and the radius is $3$. At $x=5$ the series becomes $\sum1/n$, which diverges. At $x=-1$ it becomes $\sum(-1)^n/n$, which converges conditionally by the alternating test. The real interval of convergence is therefore $[-1,5)$. The ratio test at the endpoints has limit $1$ and cannot replace these separate checks.

## Differentiation and integration

Inside $|x-a|<R$, a power series represents a differentiable function and may be differentiated or integrated term by term:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n(x-a)^n
=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nc_n(x-a)^{n-1},
$$

$$
\int_a^x\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n(t-a)^n\,dt
=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{c_n}{n+1}(x-a)^{n+1}.
$$

Both resulting power series have radius $R$. Their endpoint behavior can differ. These operations are justified on each closed interval strictly inside the radius, where suitable geometric bounds give uniform convergence. They are not permissions to differentiate arbitrary convergent function series.

For $|x|<1$, the geometric series gives $1/(1-x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n$. Integrate from $0$ to $x$:

$$
-\log(1-x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}
=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{x^n}{n}.
$$

The integral constant is fixed because both sides are zero at $x=0$. At $x=-1$, a separate alternating-series argument gives convergence. The identity at that endpoint requires a continuity-limit argument, beyond simply substituting into a theorem stated for $|x|<1$.

## Products and useful checks

Within a common interval of absolute convergence, two power series may be multiplied by collecting terms of equal degree. If the coefficients are $c_n$ and $d_n$, the coefficient of $(x-a)^n$ in their product is $\sum_{k=0}^n c_kd_{n-k}$. Absolute convergence justifies the regrouping. Always state the interval with a resulting identity, and check that the first few coefficients agree with direct finite multiplication. Connections with derivative-based coefficients appear in [[09.06 Taylor and Maclaurin expansions]].

## Practice

1. Find the centre, radius, and real interval of convergence of $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(x+1)^n/(n^2 2^n)$.
2. Derive a series for $1/(1-x)^2$, and state where your derivation is valid.

## Worked answers

1. The successive ratio tends to $|x+1|/2$, so the centre is $-1$ and $R=2$. At $x=1$ the series is $\sum1/n^2$; at $x=-3$ its absolute series is also $\sum1/n^2$. Both endpoints converge absolutely. The interval is $[-3,1]$.
2. Differentiate $1/(1-x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}x^n$ inside $|x|<1$. This gives $1/(1-x)^2=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nx^{n-1}$. Equivalently, reindex with $k=n-1$ to obtain $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(k+1)x^k$. At $x=1$ and $x=-1$ the terms do not tend to zero, so neither endpoint is included.
