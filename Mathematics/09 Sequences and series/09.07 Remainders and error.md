title: "Remainders and error"
---

> [!TLDR]
> An approximation is controlled when its omitted terms have a proved bound on a stated interval.

## Define the error before estimating it

For an approximation $A(x)$ to a real function $f(x)$, define the signed remainder as $R(x)=f(x)-A(x)$. The absolute error is $|R(x)|$. If $f(x)\ne0$, the relative error is $|R(x)|/|f(x)|$. An absolute error bound does not automatically give a relative error bound near a zero of $f$.

For a Taylor approximation at $a$, the local variable is $h=x-a$. This is the small parameter: the estimate concerns $h\to0$. A polynomial through degree $m$ often has remainder $O(|h|^{m+1})$, provided the next derivative is bounded near $a$. State the power explicitly. The phrase “third-order approximation” can mean either a cubic polynomial or an error of order $h^3$ in different conventions.

## Taylor remainder bounds

Suppose $f$ has continuous derivatives through order $m+1$ on an interval containing $a$ and $x$. The Taylor polynomial is $P_m(x)=\sum_{k=0}^m f^{(k)}(a)(x-a)^k/k!$. The integral remainder is

$$
R_m(x)=\frac1{m!}\int_a^x f^{(m+1)}(t)(x-t)^m\,dt.
$$

This formula follows by repeated integration of the fundamental theorem of calculus, or by repeated integration by parts. It retains the oriented integral when $x<a$. Taking absolute values and using $|f^{(m+1)}(t)|\leq M$ on the segment between the two points gives

$$
|R_m(x)|\leq\frac{M}{m!}\int_0^{|x-a|}u^m\,du
=\frac{M|x-a|^{m+1}}{(m+1)!}.
$$

Equivalently, under these hypotheses there is a point $\xi$ between $a$ and $x$ such that $R_m(x)=f^{(m+1)}(\xi)(x-a)^{m+1}/(m+1)!$. This is the Lagrange remainder. The unknown $\xi$ is not normally the midpoint. A bound must hold over the full segment, not just at the centre.

## Worked example: choose a polynomial degree

Approximate $e^{0.2}$ with error below $10^{-4}$. At zero all derivatives equal $1$, and on $[0,0.2]$ they are at most $e^{0.2}<2$. Thus

$$
|R_m(0.2)|<\frac{2(0.2)^{m+1}}{(m+1)!}.
$$

For $m=3$, the bound is $2(0.2)^4/24=0.0001333\ldots$, which is too large to certify the tolerance. For $m=4$, it becomes $2(0.2)^5/120=0.000005333\ldots<10^{-4}$. Therefore use

$$
P_4(0.2)=1+0.2+\frac{0.2^2}{2}+\frac{0.2^3}{6}+\frac{0.2^4}{24}
=1.2214.
$$

The estimate proves that the exact value lies between $1.2214$ and $1.221405334$. The lower endpoint uses the positive sign of the exponential remainder. The coarse bound does not prove that degree three actually fails; it only fails to certify degree three.

## Other useful remainder estimates

For an alternating series with decreasing nonnegative term magnitudes $b_n\to0$, the tail after $N$ terms has magnitude at most $b_{N+1}$. These conditions matter; see [[09.04 Absolute and conditional convergence]]. For a positive decreasing series $\sum f(n)$ with finite sum, the integral comparison gives

$$
\int_{N+1}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx
\leq\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}f(n)
\leq\int_N^{\infty}f(x)\,dx.
$$

For a geometric tail with first omitted term $ar^N$ and $|r|<1$, its absolute value is at most $|a||r|^N/(1-|r|)$. Select the estimate that matches the actual series. Taylor bounds are often general, while an exact tail formula can be much sharper.

## Practice

1. Bound the error in $\sin(0.1)\approx0.1-0.1^3/6$.
2. Give a sufficient number of terms for $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/n^2$ to have tail below $0.01$.

## Worked answers

1. Use the degree-four Taylor polynomial of sine, whose fourth-degree coefficient is zero. Its fifth derivative is $\cos x$, with magnitude at most $1$. Thus the displayed cubic expression has error at most $(0.1)^5/5!=1/12000000$, about $8.34\times10^{-8}$. Since the first omitted term is positive, the approximation is an underestimate at this point.
2. The function $f(x)=x^{-2}$ is positive, continuous, and decreasing for $x\geq1$. The upper tail bound is $\int_N^\infty x^{-2}\,dx=1/N$. Taking $N=101$ gives a bound $1/101<0.01$. The sufficient count comes from a proved inequality; it need not be the smallest possible count.
