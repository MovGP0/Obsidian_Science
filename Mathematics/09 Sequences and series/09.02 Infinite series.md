> [!TLDR]
> An infinite series converges when its sequence of finite partial sums has a finite limit.

## A sum defined through a sequence

Let $(a_n)_{n\geq1}$ be a real sequence. The notation $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n$ represents a series. Define its $N$th partial sum by

$$
S_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}a_n=a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_N.
$$

The series converges to $S$ if $S_N\to S$ as $N\to\infty$. Otherwise it diverges. Each $S_N$ is an ordinary finite sum; the infinite sum is a limit, not a final addition after infinitely many steps. The sequence $(a_n)$ and the sequence $(S_N)$ are different objects. The limit definition in [[09.01 Sequences]] applies to $(S_N)$.

If a series converges, its terms must tend to zero. Indeed, for $n\geq2$,

$$
a_n=S_n-S_{n-1}\longrightarrow S-S=0.
$$

This is a necessary condition only. For the harmonic series $\sum1/n$, group terms into blocks with indices $2^{k-1}+1$ through $2^k$. There are $2^{k-1}$ terms in that block, and each is at least $1/2^k$. The block sum is at least $1/2$. Thus $S_{2^K}\geq1+K/2$, which grows without bound. The terms tend to zero, but the partial sums do not have a finite limit.

## Geometric series

A geometric series has terms $a r^n$ for $n\geq0$, where $a$ is the initial term and $r$ is the common ratio. To avoid an index shift, let $G_N$ contain exactly $N$ terms:

$$
G_N=a+ar+\cdots+ar^{N-1}.
$$

Multiply by $r$ and subtract. Every middle term cancels, so $(1-r)G_N=a(1-r^N)$. For $r\ne1$,

$$
G_N=\frac{a(1-r^N)}{1-r}.
$$

If $|r|<1$, then $r^N\to0$, and therefore

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}ar^n=\frac{a}{1-r},
\qquad
R_N=\frac{ar^N}{1-r}.
$$

Here $R_N$ is the infinite sum minus the first $N$ terms. If $a\ne0$, the series diverges for $|r|\geq1$: when $r=1$ the sums grow linearly, when $r=-1$ they oscillate, and when $|r|>1$ the terms fail to tend to zero. The zero series $a=0$ is the trivial exception.

## Worked example: a telescoping sum

Consider $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/[n(n+1)]$. Partial fractions give

$$
\frac1{n(n+1)}=\frac1n-\frac1{n+1}.
$$

For a finite upper index $N$, cancellation is valid:

$$
S_N=\left(1-\frac12\right)+\left(\frac12-\frac13\right)
+\cdots+\left(\frac1N-\frac1{N+1}\right)
=1-\frac1{N+1}.
$$

Taking $N\to\infty$ gives sum $1$. The exact remainder is $1/(N+1)$. For error below $0.001$, take $N=1000$, since $1/1001<0.001$. Retaining the final uncancelled term is essential: cancellation does not make every finite sum equal to $1$.

## What operations are justified?

For convergent series with sums $A$ and $B$, the series $\sum(ca_n+db_n)$ has sum $cA+dB$ for fixed real constants $c,d$. This follows by taking limits of the corresponding finite partial sums. This argument does not justify subtracting two divergent infinite sums. Nor does it justify changing the order of terms arbitrarily; that issue depends on [[09.04 Absolute and conditional convergence]]. Removing or adding finitely many terms preserves convergence, but usually changes the sum.

## Practice

1. Find $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}3(1/4)^{n-1}$ and its error after five terms.
2. Determine whether $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n/(n+1)$ converges. Explain which test is sufficient.

## Worked answers

1. The initial term is $a=3$ and ratio is $r=1/4$, with $|r|<1$. The sum is $3/(1-1/4)=4$. The tail after five terms is $3(1/4)^5/(1-1/4)=4/4^5=1/256$. Thus the five-term sum is $4-1/256$ and underestimates the sum.
2. The terms satisfy $n/(n+1)=1-1/(n+1)\to1$, not zero. The necessary term condition fails, so the series diverges. There is no need to calculate its partial sums. A limit of terms equal to zero would require further work, as the harmonic example shows.
