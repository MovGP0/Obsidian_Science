title: "Rotations and products"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Multiplying complex numbers multiplies their moduli and adds their arguments; a unit-modulus factor gives a rotation about the origin.

## Multiplication in polar form

Let $z=re^{i\theta}$ and $w=se^{i\phi}$ be nonzero complex numbers, with positive real moduli $r,s$ and real arguments $\theta,\phi$. Euler’s formula and the exponential product rule give

$$
zw=rs\,e^{i(\theta+\phi)}.
$$

Thus $|zw|=rs$ and $\theta+\phi$ is an argument of the product. Arguments are defined modulo $2\pi$: their sum need not be the principal argument in $(-\pi,\pi]$. If it is outside that interval, add or subtract full turns.

Fix $w=se^{i\phi}$ and regard multiplication by $w$ as a map $z\mapsto wz$. Every point turns through angle $\phi$ around the origin, then its distance from the origin is multiplied by $s$. The order of these two actions does not affect the result. If $s=1$, distances are unchanged and the map is a rotation. If $s>1$, distances increase; if $0<s<1$, they decrease. Multiplication by zero sends every point to zero and has no rotation angle.

## The same rule in coordinates

Write $z=x+iy$ and $w=a+bi$. Expansion gives

$$
wz=(ax-by)+i(bx+ay).
$$

As a map of real coordinate columns, this is

$$
\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}
\longmapsto
\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\b&a\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}.
$$

If $w=e^{i\phi}$, then $a=\cos\phi$, $b=\sin\phi$, and the displayed matrix is the usual plane-rotation matrix. For example, multiplication by $i$ takes $(x,y)$ to $(-y,x)$: a counterclockwise quarter turn. Multiplication by $-1$ gives a half turn, and multiplication by $-i$ gives a clockwise quarter turn.

This coordinate form checks the sign convention. A positive angle is counterclockwise. Conjugation, which maps $(x,y)$ to $(x,-y)$, is a reflection and cannot be achieved by one fixed complex multiplier for all inputs.

## Powers and repeated rotations

For an integer $n\ge1$, repeated multiplication yields **De Moivre’s formula**:

$$
z^n=r^ne^{in\theta}
=r^n(\cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta)).
$$

Each multiplication adds another angle $\theta$ and another factor $r$ to the modulus. The formula also holds for $n=0$ when $z\ne0$, since $z^0=1$. For negative integers, use $z^{-1}=r^{-1}e^{-i\theta}$. Noninteger powers require an argument choice and are not covered by the same single-valued rule without further conditions.

For $r=1$, all powers stay on the unit circle. If $\theta/(2\pi)$ is rational, write it as $p/q$ in lowest terms with $q>0$. Then $z^q=e^{2\pi ip}=1$ and the powers repeat after $q$ steps. If that ratio is irrational, no two integer powers coincide: equality of two would imply $(n-m)\theta=2\pi k$, a rational ratio.

## Worked example: simplify a sixth power

Take $z=1+i$. Its modulus is $\sqrt2$ and its argument is $\pi/4$, so

$$
z^6=(\sqrt2)^6e^{6i\pi/4}
=8e^{3i\pi/2}=-8i.
$$

The modulus has become $8$, while the total rotation is $3\pi/2$. A rectangular check is short: $(1+i)^2=2i$, so $(1+i)^6=(2i)^3=8i^3=-8i$.

For division, subtract arguments and divide moduli:

$$
\frac zw=\frac rs e^{i(\theta-\phi)},\qquad w\ne0.
$$

This reverses the rotation and scale caused by multiplication by $w$. It is often simpler than expanding a large quotient in rectangular coordinates.

## Practice

1. Rotate $2-i$ counterclockwise by $\pi/2$, without changing its modulus.
2. Calculate $(\sqrt3+i)^3$ in polar form and then in rectangular form.

### Worked answers

1. Multiply by $e^{i\pi/2}=i$: $i(2-i)=2i-i^2=1+2i$. Both the original and final moduli are $\sqrt5$, which checks the preserved length.
2. The modulus of $\sqrt3+i$ is $2$ and its argument is $\pi/6$. Cubing gives $2^3e^{i\pi/2}=8e^{i\pi/2}=8i$. The argument was multiplied by $3$ and the modulus was raised to the third power, rather than multiplied by $3$.
