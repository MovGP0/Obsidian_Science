> [!TLDR]
> Complex numbers extend real arithmetic with a number whose square is −1; calculate with the usual algebra rules and then collect real and imaginary terms.

## What a complex number means

The equation $x^2=-1$ has no real solution, because every real square is nonnegative. Introduce the **imaginary unit** $i$, defined by $i^2=-1$. A complex number has the form

$$
z=a+bi,\qquad a,b\in\mathbb R.
$$

The set of all these numbers is $\mathbb C$. The real part is $\operatorname{Re}z=a$ and the imaginary part is $\operatorname{Im}z=b$. Both parts are real numbers: the imaginary part is $b$, rather than $bi$. A real number is included by setting $b=0$. A purely imaginary number has $a=0$.

Equality means equality of both parts. Thus $a+bi=c+di$ if and only if $a=c$ and $b=d$. This rule lets us solve an equation with complex numbers by comparing two real equations. There is no ambiguity in the representation: if $a-c=(d-b)i$ and $d-b$ were nonzero, $i$ would be real, which contradicts $i^2=-1$.

## Addition and multiplication

For real numbers $a,b,c,d$, addition is component by component:

$$
(a+bi)+(c+di)=(a+c)+(b+d)i.
$$

Subtraction uses the same rule with minus signs. For multiplication, distribute all terms and replace $i^2$ by $-1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(a+bi)(c+di)
&=ac+adi+bci+bdi^2\\
&=(ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i.
\end{aligned}
$$

Complex addition and multiplication are associative and commutative. Multiplication distributes over addition, so familiar polynomial methods still apply. For example, $(z+w)^2=z^2+2zw+w^2$ for complex $z,w$. A useful cycle is

$$
i^0=1,\quad i^1=i,\quad i^2=-1,\quad i^3=-i,\quad i^4=1.
$$

For a nonnegative integer exponent, divide the exponent by four and use its remainder. Thus $i^{23}=i^3=-i$.

## Division and its restriction

Division by zero is undefined. If $w=c+di\ne0$, at least one of $c,d$ is nonzero, and hence $c^2+d^2>0$. Multiply numerator and denominator by $c-di$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{a+bi}{c+di}
&=\frac{(a+bi)(c-di)}{(c+di)(c-di)}\\
&=\frac{(ac+bd)+(bc-ad)i}{c^2+d^2}\\
&=\frac{ac+bd}{c^2+d^2}
+\frac{bc-ad}{c^2+d^2}i.
\end{aligned}
$$

The denominator becomes real because the middle terms cancel. The number $c-di$ is the **conjugate** of $w$; its properties are developed in [[06.03 Conjugation and modulus]]. This calculation proves that every nonzero complex number has a multiplicative inverse.

## Worked example: a quotient and a check

Calculate $(3+2i)/(1-i)$. The conjugate of the denominator is $1+i$, so

$$
\frac{3+2i}{1-i}
=\frac{(3+2i)(1+i)}{(1-i)(1+i)}
=\frac{3+3i+2i-2}{2}
=\frac12+\frac52i.
$$

Check by multiplication:

$$
\left(\frac12+\frac52i\right)(1-i)
=\left(\frac12+\frac52\right)
+\left(\frac52-\frac12\right)i
=3+2i.
$$

The check recovers the numerator, so it also checks both signs in the quotient.

## Compare complex numbers with care

Complex numbers support equality and arithmetic, but they have no ordering that extends the real order and obeys all ordered-field rules. If such an order existed, every square would be nonnegative. Both $1=1^2$ and $-1=i^2$ would then be nonnegative, which contradicts $-1<0$ in the real numbers. You can compare moduli, such as $|1+i|<|3|$, but this compares two real distances. It does not define an order between the original complex numbers.

## Errors to avoid

Do not replace $i^2$ by $1$. Do not divide real and imaginary parts separately: $(a+bi)/(c+di)$ is not $a/c+(b/d)i$. The square-root identity $\sqrt{xy}=\sqrt{x}\sqrt{y}$ is not valid for arbitrary complex inputs with the principal square root. For example, $\sqrt{(-1)(-1)}=1$, whereas $\sqrt{-1}\sqrt{-1}=i^2=-1$.

## Practice

1. Calculate $(2-3i)(-1+4i)$ and state its real and imaginary parts.
2. Solve $(2+i)z=5$ and verify your solution.

### Worked answers

1. Distribute to get $-2+8i+3i-12i^2$. Since $-12i^2=12$, the result is $10+11i$. Its real part is $10$ and its imaginary part is $11$.
2. Since $2+i\ne0$, division gives $z=5(2-i)/(4+1)=2-i$. Multiplication gives $(2+i)(2-i)=4-i^2=5$, so this value satisfies the equation.
