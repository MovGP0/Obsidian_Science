title: "Polar representation"

> [!TLDR]
> A nonzero complex number is specified by its positive modulus and an angle; angles that differ by a whole number of full turns represent the same number.

## From coordinates to radius and angle

Let $z=x+iy\ne0$, with $x,y\in\mathbb R$. Its modulus is $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}>0$. An **argument** of $z$ is a real angle $\theta$, measured counterclockwise from the positive real axis, for which

$$
x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta.
$$

Angles in this article use radians, so a full turn is $2\pi$. Substituting these component expressions gives the **polar form**

$$
z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta).
$$

Division by $r$ places the point on the unit circle. The normalized coordinates satisfy $(x/r)^2+(y/r)^2=1$, which explains why a suitable angle exists. Polar form records distance and direction; rectangular form records horizontal and vertical components.

The right triangle in Fig. 06.2 connects the two descriptions. The angle identifies the direction, while the length $r$ gives the scale. Equal length scales on both axes preserve that angle.

## Why an argument is not unique

The identities $\cos(\theta+2\pi k)=\cos\theta$ and $\sin(\theta+2\pi k)=\sin\theta$ hold for every integer $k$. Therefore all arguments are

$$
\theta+2\pi k,\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.
$$

This is equality **modulo $2\pi$**. An argument is a possible angle; the **principal argument** is a selected one. We use the convention

$$
\operatorname{Arg}z\in(-\pi,\pi].
$$

With this convention the negative real axis has principal argument $\pi$, not $-\pi$. Other books can use $[0,2\pi)$, so state the interval before comparing answers. The number $0$ has modulus zero but no argument: all directions lead to the same origin when multiplied by zero.

## A safe conversion method

First calculate $r$. Then identify the quadrant from the signs of $x$ and $y$. When $x\ne0$, $\tan\theta=y/x$, but tangent alone determines an angle only modulo $\pi$. The value $\arctan(y/x)$ lies between $-\pi/2$ and $\pi/2$, so it can select the wrong quadrant. The two-argument function $\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)$ includes the sign information. Check its endpoint convention if an input lies on the negative real axis.

For points on the axes, use their directions directly: positive real, positive imaginary, negative real, and negative imaginary points have principal arguments $0$, $\pi/2$, $\pi$, and $-\pi/2$, respectively.

## Worked example: choose the correct quadrant

Let $z=-\sqrt3+i$. Its modulus is $r=\sqrt{3+1}=2$. Dividing the coordinates by $r$ gives

$$
\cos\theta=-\frac{\sqrt3}{2},\qquad
\sin\theta=\frac12.
$$

The real part is negative and the imaginary part is positive, so the point lies in quadrant II. Its principal argument is $5\pi/6$. Thus

$$
z=2\left(\cos\frac{5\pi}{6}+i\sin\frac{5\pi}{6}\right).
$$

All arguments are $5\pi/6+2\pi k$. A direct inverse tangent would instead give $\arctan(-1/\sqrt3)=-\pi/6$, a quadrant IV angle. That angle points to $\sqrt3-i$, the opposite point.

To convert back, multiply $r$ by the cosine and sine separately. A negative radius is not needed: absorb its sign by adding $\pi$ to the angle. The usual polar convention always uses $r>0$ for a nonzero number.

## Phase changes and continuity

The terms argument and phase both refer to an angle. A path around the origin can have a continuous angle that grows beyond $\pi$. Its principal argument instead jumps from near $\pi$ to near $-\pi$ at the negative real axis. That jump belongs to the selected angle convention; it does not mean that the complex point jumps. This distinction is useful in [[06.08 Complex-valued functions]].

## Practice

1. Find the modulus, principal argument, and all arguments of $-1-i$.
2. Convert $4(\cos(-\pi/3)+i\sin(-\pi/3))$ to rectangular form.

### Worked answers

1. The modulus is $\sqrt2$. Both components are negative, so the principal argument in $(-\pi,\pi]$ is $-3\pi/4$. All arguments are $-3\pi/4+2\pi k$, with $k\in\mathbb Z$.
2. Since $\cos(-\pi/3)=1/2$ and $\sin(-\pi/3)=-\sqrt3/2$, multiplication by $4$ gives $2-2\sqrt3i$. Its positive real and negative imaginary parts confirm quadrant IV.
