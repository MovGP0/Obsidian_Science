> [!TLDR]
> Euler’s formula writes the unit-circle point with angle θ as eⁱᶿ = cos θ + i sin θ and turns angle addition into multiplication.

## Define the complex exponential

For a complex number $z$, define the exponential by its power series

$$
e^z=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^n}{n!}
=1+z+\frac{z^2}{2!}+\frac{z^3}{3!}+\cdots.
$$

Here $0!=1$ and $n!=1\cdot2\cdots n$ for a positive integer $n$. The series converges absolutely for every $z$: the ratio of successive absolute terms is $|z|/(n+1)$, which tends to zero. This definition extends the real exponential. Absolute convergence permits separation into even and odd powers and permits the product rearrangements used below.

For real $\theta$, substitute $z=i\theta$. Because $i^{2k}=(-1)^k$ and $i^{2k+1}=i(-1)^k$, the even and odd parts become

$$
\begin{aligned}
e^{i\theta}
&=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k\theta^{2k}}{(2k)!}
+i\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k\theta^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}\\
&=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta.
\end{aligned}
$$

The final equality uses the power series for real cosine and sine, with angles in radians. This is **Euler’s formula**. It connects a function defined by an infinite sum with a geometric point on the unit circle.

## Unit circle and polar form

By Euler’s formula,

$$
|e^{i\theta}|=\sqrt{\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta}=1.
$$

The point therefore has unit distance from the origin and argument $\theta$ modulo $2\pi$. The polar expression from [[06.04 Polar representation]] becomes the compact form $z=re^{i\theta}$ for $r>0$. It is a complex number, not merely a formal symbol for an angle.

The values at quarter turns are $e^0=1$, $e^{i\pi/2}=i$, $e^{i\pi}=-1$, and $e^{3i\pi/2}=-i$. In particular, $e^{i\pi}+1=0$. Periodicity gives $e^{i(\theta+2\pi k)}=e^{i\theta}$ for every integer $k$. Hence the complex exponential is not one-to-one.

## Why exponent addition still works

For complex $z,w$, multiply the two absolutely convergent series and collect terms of total degree $n$. The binomial theorem yields

$$
\begin{aligned}
e^ze^w
&=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}
\frac{z^k w^{n-k}}{k!(n-k)!}\\
&=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(z+w)^n}{n!}
=e^{z+w}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Taking $w=-z$ shows $e^ze^{-z}=1$, so the exponential never vanishes. Taking $z=x$ and $w=iy$, where $x,y$ are real, gives

$$
e^{x+iy}=e^x(\cos y+i\sin y).
$$

Its modulus is $e^x$ and its argument is $y$ modulo $2\pi$. A real part in the exponent changes the modulus; an imaginary part changes the angle.

## Worked example: sum and difference of exponentials

Euler’s formula with $-\theta$ gives $e^{-i\theta}=\cos\theta-i\sin\theta$. Add and subtract the two formulas:

$$
\cos\theta=\frac{e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}}2,
\qquad
\sin\theta=\frac{e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}}{2i}.
$$

For $\theta=\pi/3$, the two exponentials are $1/2+i\sqrt3/2$ and $1/2-i\sqrt3/2$. Their sum is $1$, so dividing by $2$ gives $\cos(\pi/3)=1/2$. Their difference is $i\sqrt3$, so dividing by $2i$ gives $\sin(\pi/3)=\sqrt3/2$. The imaginary units cancel in this last quotient, leaving a real sine value.

These identities also show why real trigonometric functions can be calculated using complex algebra. Do not omit the $i$ in the sine denominator. Do not use degree values directly in the power series: the series formulas use radians.

## Practice

1. Express $e^{\log 3+i\pi/2}$ in rectangular form, where $\log 3$ is the real natural logarithm.
2. Find all real $\theta$ for which $e^{i\theta}=-i$.

### Worked answers

1. Split the exponential: $e^{\log3}e^{i\pi/2}=3(0+i)=3i$. Its modulus is $3$ and its direction is the positive imaginary axis.
2. Compare cosine and sine: they must be $0$ and $-1$. The angles are $\theta=-\pi/2+2\pi k$ for $k\in\mathbb Z$. Adding a whole turn preserves both components, so these give every solution.
