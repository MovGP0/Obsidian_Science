title: "Roots of complex numbers"

> [!TLDR]
> A nonzero complex number has exactly n distinct nth roots, equally spaced on a circle whose radius is the nth root of its modulus.

## What root problem is being solved

Fix a positive integer $n$ and a complex number $z\ne0$. An **nth root** of $z$ is a complex number $w$ that satisfies $w^n=z$. Write $z=re^{i\theta}$, where $r>0$ and $\theta$ is any argument. Write a candidate root as $w=\rho e^{i\alpha}$, with $\rho>0$.

By the power rule from [[06.06 Rotations and products]],

$$
w^n=\rho^ne^{in\alpha}.
$$

Equality with $z$ requires equal moduli and angles equal modulo $2\pi$. Therefore $\rho^n=r$ and $n\alpha=\theta+2\pi k$ for some integer $k$. Since $\rho>0$, the real equation has the unique positive solution $\rho=r^{1/n}$. The roots are

$$
w_k=r^{1/n}\exp\left(i\frac{\theta+2\pi k}{n}\right),
\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1.
$$

To see why this list is complete, every possible root must satisfy the two equations just derived. Increasing $k$ by $n$ adds $2\pi$ to $\alpha$, so it repeats the same root. For $0\le j<k<n$, the angle difference $2\pi(k-j)/n$ is strictly between $0$ and $2\pi$, so those roots are distinct. This establishes exactly $n$ distinct roots for this equation, without needing a general theorem about arbitrary polynomials.

## Equally spaced points

All roots have the same modulus $r^{1/n}$. Consecutive roots differ in argument by $2\pi/n$. They are the vertices of a regular $n$-gon for $n\ge3$, a pair of opposite points for $n=2$, and a single point for $n=1$. Changing the chosen argument $\theta$ by a full turn changes their order but leaves the set unchanged.

The roots of **unity** solve $w^n=1$. Take $r=1$ and $\theta=0$:

$$
\omega_k=e^{2\pi ik/n},\qquad k=0,\ldots,n-1.
$$

If $\omega=e^{2\pi i/n}$, all roots of unity are $1,\omega,\omega^2,\ldots,\omega^{n-1}$. Any root $w_0$ of a nonzero $z$ generates all its roots by $w_k=w_0\omega_k$. This follows because $(w_0\omega_k)^n=w_0^n\omega_k^n=z$.

## Worked example: every cube root of −8

Use $-8=8e^{i\pi}$. The root modulus is $8^{1/3}=2$, and the three angles are

$$
\alpha_k=\frac{\pi+2\pi k}{3}
=\frac\pi3,\ \pi,\ \frac{5\pi}3.
$$

Converting each root to rectangular form gives

$$
w_0=1+i\sqrt3,\qquad w_1=-2,\qquad w_2=1-i\sqrt3.
$$

Check the first root by its polar form: $(2e^{i\pi/3})^3=8e^{i\pi}=-8$. The real root satisfies $(-2)^3=-8$. The third root is the conjugate of the first; cubing preserves conjugation, and $\overline{-8}=-8$, so it also works. A real equation can thus have nonreal solutions when solved in $\mathbb C$.

## Limitations and notation

The symbol $z^{1/n}$ can mean a selected principal value rather than the whole set. State whether you need one root or all roots. Dividing only the principal argument by $n$ finds one value and can miss the other $n-1$. Also, the root modulus is $r^{1/n}$, not $r/n$.

For $z=0$, $w^n=0$ has just the one distinct root $w=0$; the argument calculation is not applicable. For $n>1$, zero is called a root of multiplicity $n$ of the polynomial $w^n$, but multiplicity does not make it $n$ different points.

## Practice

1. Find all fourth roots of $16$ and verify one nonreal root.
2. Find both square roots of $3+4i$ using a rectangular calculation.

### Worked answers

1. The modulus is $16^{1/4}=2$, and the angles are $0,\pi/2,\pi,3\pi/2$. The roots are $2,2i,-2,-2i$. For example, $(2i)^4=16i^4=16$.
2. Write $w=a+bi$ with real $a,b$. Comparing $w^2=(a^2-b^2)+2abi$ with $3+4i$ gives $a^2-b^2=3$ and $ab=2$. Also $|w|^2=|3+4i|=5$, so $a^2+b^2=5$. Addition gives $2a^2=8$, hence $a=\pm2$. Since $ab=2$, $b$ is respectively $1$ or $-1$. The roots are $2+i$ and $-2-i$.
