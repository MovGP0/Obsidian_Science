> [!TLDR]
> A complex number is a point or displacement in a plane; addition joins displacements, and the modulus of a difference gives distance.

## Coordinates and displacements

Write a complex number as $z=x+iy$, where $x,y$ are real and $i^2=-1$. Associate $z$ with the point $(x,y)$ in a Cartesian plane. The horizontal axis is the real axis and the vertical axis is the imaginary axis. Equal scales on the two axes are required if lengths and angles are to have their usual geometric meaning.

The same number can describe the displacement vector from the origin to $(x,y)$. These are two interpretations of one ordered pair. A point indicates a location. A displacement indicates a change in location and can be translated without changing its components. The point $0$ is the origin, and the displacement $0$ leaves every point fixed.

For example, $z=-2+3i$ lies two units left and three units above the origin. Its coordinates are $(-2,3)$, not $(3,-2)$. The real part $\operatorname{Re}z=-2$ is a horizontal coordinate; the imaginary part $\operatorname{Im}z=3$ is a vertical coordinate.

## Addition as translation

If $z=x+iy$ and $w=u+iv$, complex addition gives

$$
z+w=(x+u)+i(y+v).
$$

Starting at the point $z$, move $u$ units horizontally and $v$ units vertically. The resulting point is $z+w$. Equivalently, place the tail of the vector $w$ at the head of $z$. The vector from the original origin to the final head represents the sum. This is the **head-to-tail rule**.

![[math-06-complex-addition.svg|**Fig. 06.1** The displacements z = 2 + i and w = −1 + 2i add to z + w = 1 + 3i. The two translated edges complete a parallelogram.]]

The other path goes first along $w$ and then along $z$. Both paths reach the same corner of the parallelogram. This explains geometrically why $z+w=w+z$. Subtraction reverses a displacement: $z-w=z+(-w)$. The vector from $w$ to $z$ is therefore $z-w$.

## Length and distance

The **modulus** of $z=x+iy$ is the nonnegative number

$$
|z|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}.
$$

This is the distance from $z$ to the origin by the Pythagorean theorem. To find the distance between $z=x+iy$ and $w=u+iv$, first form the displacement between them:

$$
d(z,w)=|z-w|=\sqrt{(x-u)^2+(y-v)^2}.
$$

Thus $|z|$ is not usually the distance from $z$ to an arbitrary point; that point must be subtracted first. The set $|z-z_0|=r$, for a fixed center $z_0$ and $r>0$, is a circle of radius $r$. Replacing $=$ by $<$ gives the open disk inside it.

## Worked example: a midpoint and two distances

Let $z_1=1+2i$ and $z_2=5-i$. Their midpoint is obtained by averaging both coordinates:

$$
m=\frac{z_1+z_2}{2}=\frac{6+i}{2}=3+\frac12i.
$$

The displacement from the first point to the second is $z_2-z_1=4-3i$. Its length is $\sqrt{4^2+(-3)^2}=5$. From the first point to the midpoint the displacement is $m-z_1=2-\frac32i$, whose length is

$$
\sqrt{2^2+\left(-\frac32\right)^2}
=\sqrt{\frac{25}{4}}=\frac52.
$$

The midpoint divides the segment into equal lengths, as expected. More generally, $z(t)=(1-t)z_1+tz_2$, with $0\le t\le1$, describes the whole segment. Here $t$ is a real fraction of the displacement from $z_1$ to $z_2$.

## A geometric bound

The direct path from $0$ to $z+w$ cannot be longer than the broken path through $z$. This gives the triangle inequality $|z+w|\le|z|+|w|$. Equality holds when the nonzero displacements point in the same direction. Opposite displacements can cancel, so their lengths do not generally add. See [[06.03 Conjugation and modulus]] for an algebraic proof.

## Practice

1. Find the distance between $-1+4i$ and $2$.
2. Describe the set $|z-(2-i)|=3$. Determine whether $2+2i$ and $2+i$ belong to it.

### Worked answers

1. Subtract the points: $(-1+4i)-2=-3+4i$. The distance is $\sqrt{(-3)^2+4^2}=5$.
2. The set is the circle with center $(2,-1)$ and radius $3$. For $2+2i$, the difference from the center is $3i$, with modulus $3$, so the point is on the circle. For $2+i$, the difference is $2i$, with modulus $2$, so the point lies inside and does not satisfy the equality.
