---
title: "Conjugation and modulus"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Conjugation reflects a complex number across the real axis, and the product of a number with its conjugate is the square of its modulus.

## Definitions and geometry

For $z=a+bi$, with $a,b\in\mathbb R$, define its **complex conjugate** and **modulus** by

$$
\overline z=a-bi,\qquad |z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}.
$$

Conjugation changes only the sign of the imaginary coordinate. In [[06.02 The complex plane]], this is reflection in the real axis. The modulus is distance from the origin, so $|z|\ge0$, and $|z|=0$ if and only if $z=0$. Conjugate points have the same distance from the origin: $|\overline z|=|z|$.

Conjugation twice restores the original number. Adding or subtracting a conjugate separates the components:

$$
\overline{\overline z}=z,\qquad
\operatorname{Re}z=\frac{z+\overline z}{2},\qquad
\operatorname{Im}z=\frac{z-\overline z}{2i}.
$$

The last quotient is real. For example, if $z=2-5i$, then $(z-\overline z)/(2i)=(-10i)/(2i)=-5$.

## Product identities and reciprocals

Direct multiplication gives

$$
z\overline z=(a+bi)(a-bi)=a^2-b^2i^2=a^2+b^2=|z|^2.
$$

Conjugation respects sums and products:

$$
\overline{z+w}=\overline z+\overline w,
\qquad \overline{zw}=\overline z\,\overline w.
$$

To see the product rule, write $w=c+di$. The real part of $zw$ is $ac-bd$ and its imaginary part is $ad+bc$. Multiplying $(a-bi)(c-di)$ gives the same real part and the negative imaginary part. Thus the two sides agree component by component.

If $z\ne0$, then $|z|^2>0$, so the identity for $z\overline z$ gives the reciprocal

$$
\frac1z=\frac{\overline z}{|z|^2}.
$$

The denominator is the **square** of the modulus. Using only $|z|$ would usually give the wrong reciprocal. The formula cannot be used for $z=0$.

A number equals its conjugate exactly when its imaginary part is zero. Indeed, $a+bi=a-bi$ implies $2bi=0$, hence $b=0$. Likewise, $z=-\overline z$ is equivalent to $a=0$, so it describes purely imaginary numbers, including zero. These tests let you identify whether an algebraic expression is real or purely imaginary without first reducing it to numerical coordinates.

## Magnitudes of products

Using the product rule for conjugation and commutativity,

$$
|zw|^2=(zw)\overline{zw}
=z\overline z\,w\overline w=|z|^2|w|^2.
$$

Both moduli are nonnegative, so taking nonnegative square roots gives $|zw|=|z||w|$. For $w\ne0$, apply this to $(z/w)w=z$ to obtain $|z/w|=|z|/|w|$. For every integer $n\ge0$, repeated multiplication gives $|z^n|=|z|^n$.

## Worked example: simplify before expanding

Let $z=3-4i$ and $w=-1+2i$. Then $|z|=5$ and $|w|=\sqrt5$. Their product is

$$
zw=-3+6i+4i-8i^2=5+10i.
$$

Its modulus is $\sqrt{25+100}=5\sqrt5$, which agrees with $|z||w|$. The reciprocal of $z$ is $(3+4i)/25$. Consequently,

$$
\frac wz=\frac{(-1+2i)(3+4i)}{25}
=\frac{-11+2i}{25}.
$$

As a check, the modulus of this quotient is $\sqrt{121+4}/25=\sqrt5/5$, which equals $|w|/|z|$.

## Why sums need an inequality

For any $q=a+bi$, the bound $\operatorname{Re}q=a\le\sqrt{a^2+b^2}=|q|$ holds. Expand the square of a sum's modulus:

$$
\begin{aligned}
|z+w|^2
&=|z|^2+|w|^2+2\operatorname{Re}(z\overline w)\\
&\le |z|^2+|w|^2+2|z||w|\\
&=(|z|+|w|)^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Taking square roots proves the triangle inequality. It is generally an inequality, not equality: $|1+(-1)|=0$, although $|1|+|-1|=2$. Also distinguish $|z|^2=z\overline z$ from $z^2$; the latter need not even be real.

## Practice

1. Find the reciprocal of $-2+2i$ and check its modulus.
2. If $z\overline z=13$ and $\operatorname{Re}z=2$, determine all possible $z$.

### Worked answers

1. The modulus squared is $4+4=8$. The reciprocal is $(-2-2i)/8=(-1-i)/4$. Its modulus is $\sqrt2/4=1/(2\sqrt2)$, the reciprocal of $|-2+2i|=2\sqrt2$.
2. Write $z=2+bi$ with real $b$. Then $z\overline z=4+b^2=13$, so $b^2=9$ and $b=\pm3$. Thus $z=2+3i$ or $z=2-3i$. Both values satisfy the original conditions.
