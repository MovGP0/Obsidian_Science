title: "Complex-valued functions"

> [!TLDR]
> A complex-valued function has real and imaginary component functions; modulus and phase describe the same output in polar form, except that phase is undefined at zeros.

## Separate input and output

A **complex-valued function** is a rule $f:D\to\mathbb C$, where $D$ is a specified domain. The input need not be complex. For a real input $t\in D\subseteq\mathbb R$, write

$$
f(t)=u(t)+iv(t),\qquad
u(t)=\operatorname{Re}f(t),\quad v(t)=\operatorname{Im}f(t).
$$

The component functions $u,v$ have real outputs. For a complex input $z=x+iy$, one instead writes $f(x+iy)=u(x,y)+iv(x,y)$; each component can then depend on two real coordinates. Specifying the domain matters: $f(z)=1/z$ is defined on $\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$, whereas $f(t)=1/(1+it)$ is defined for every real $t$.

A graph of $u(t)$ or $v(t)$ against $t$ shows one component. The curve $t\mapsto(u(t),v(t))$ in the complex plane shows the complete complex output. It does not use $t$ as a coordinate axis. Different inputs can reach the same point, so a curve alone may not show the direction or number of traversals.

## Modulus and phase functions

Define the real nonnegative function

$$
R(t)=|f(t)|=\sqrt{u(t)^2+v(t)^2}.
$$

At inputs for which $R(t)>0$, an argument function $\phi(t)$ can be chosen so that

$$
f(t)=R(t)e^{i\phi(t)},\qquad
u(t)=R(t)\cos\phi(t),\quad
v(t)=R(t)\sin\phi(t).
$$

At a zero of $f$, the modulus is zero but the phase is undefined. For nonzero values the principal phase $\operatorname{Arg}f(t)$ is unique after choosing an interval, here $(-\pi,\pi]$. An angle that differs by $2\pi k$ describes the same output. A continuous phase, when available on the chosen domain, can leave that interval. It must not be confused with the principal phase.

For example, $f(t)=e^{it}$ has $u(t)=\cos t$, $v(t)=\sin t$, and $R(t)=1$. A continuous phase on $\mathbb R$ is $\phi(t)=t$. The principal phase jumps at odd multiples of $\pi$ even though $f$ remains continuous. This is an angle-convention effect, as explained in [[06.04 Polar representation]].

## Worked example: a rational function

Let $f(t)=1/(1+it)$ for real $t$. Since $|1+it|^2=1+t^2>0$, it has no excluded real inputs. Multiply by the denominator's conjugate:

$$
f(t)=\frac{1-it}{1+t^2}
=\frac1{1+t^2}-i\frac{t}{1+t^2}.
$$

Therefore $u(t)=1/(1+t^2)$ and $v(t)=-t/(1+t^2)$. The modulus is

$$
R(t)=\sqrt{\frac{1+t^2}{(1+t^2)^2}}
=\frac1{\sqrt{1+t^2}}.
$$

The real part is always positive, so the principal phase lies between $-\pi/2$ and $\pi/2$. Its tangent is $v/u=-t$, hence $\phi(t)=-\arctan t$. At $t=1$, the output is $(1-i)/2$, its modulus is $1/\sqrt2$, and its phase is $-\pi/4$. These are different quantities: the real part is $1/2$, not $1/\sqrt2$.

There is also a useful plane description. Since $u^2+v^2=1/(1+t^2)=u$,

$$
\left(u-\frac12\right)^2+v^2=\frac14.
$$

The outputs lie on the circle centered at $(1/2,0)$ with radius $1/2$. The point $0$ is approached as $|t|\to\infty$ but is never attained for a finite real input. An equation for a curve must therefore be accompanied by its actual range restrictions.

## Operations and a limitation

For complex-valued functions $f,g$ on a common domain, addition gives $\operatorname{Re}(f+g)=\operatorname{Re}f+\operatorname{Re}g$. Products instead mix components: if $f=u+iv$ and $g=p+iq$, then $fg=(up-vq)+i(uq+vp)$. In particular, $\operatorname{Re}(fg)$ need not equal $up$. Moduli multiply, while phases add modulo $2\pi$ at inputs where both functions are nonzero.

## Practice

1. For $f(t)=(t+i)^2$ on $\mathbb R$, find its real part, imaginary part, modulus, and all real zeros.
2. For $g(t)=-1+it$, find the principal phase at $t=1,0,-1$. Explain its change near zero.

### Worked answers

1. Expand: $f(t)=t^2-1+2it$, so $u=t^2-1$ and $v=2t$. The modulus is $|t+i|^2=t^2+1$. This is positive for every real $t$, so there are no real-input zeros. Equivalently, $v=0$ forces $t=0$, where $u=-1\ne0$.
2. The points are $-1+i$, $-1$, and $-1-i$, with principal phases $3\pi/4$, $\pi$, and $-3\pi/4$. As $t$ goes from small positive to small negative values, the point crosses the negative real axis continuously. The principal phase jumps across its endpoint convention; a continuous local phase can use angles slightly larger than $\pi$ below that axis.
