> [!TLDR]
> A partial derivative changes one input while all other inputs remain fixed; a total differential combines all first-order input changes.

## Differentiate a coordinate slice

Let $f$ be a real-valued function on an open set $D\subseteq\mathbb R^n$, and let $\mathbf a\in D$. Write $\mathbf e_i$ for the vector with $1$ in coordinate $i$ and $0$ elsewhere. The $i$th partial derivative is

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(\mathbf a)
=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(\mathbf a+h\mathbf e_i)-f(\mathbf a)}h,
$$

when this real limit exists. Only $x_i$ changes in this quotient. All other coordinates have their values at $\mathbf a$. The partial derivative is the ordinary derivative of this one-variable slice.

For a formula $f(x,y)$, the symbols $f_x$ and $f_y$ are shorthand for its two partial derivatives. A subscript names the input coordinate, not an output component. Partial derivatives of a vector map are computed component by component and assembled in [[12.03 Total derivatives]].

## Worked example: constants depend on the chosen slice

For $f(x,y)=x^2y+e^{xy}$, differentiation with respect to $x$ treats $y$ as a constant. The power rule gives $2xy$. The ordinary chain rule gives $y e^{xy}$ because the derivative of $xy$ with respect to $x$ is $y$. Thus

$$
f_x=2xy+ye^{xy},\qquad f_y=x^2+xe^{xy}.
$$

At $(1,0)$ these values are $0$ and $2$. Changing $x$ alone has zero first-order effect there, but changing $y$ alone has a first-order effect of twice the increment. This does not imply that changing both coordinates has no effect.

If $f$ is differentiable at $(a,b)$, its total differential is the linear map

$$
df_{(a,b)}(h,k)=f_x(a,b)h+f_y(a,b)k.
$$

The notation $df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy$ names the same linear expression; $dx$ and $dy$ act as coordinate increments. At $(1,0)$ the differential of this example is $2k$. It estimates the full output change for a displacement $(h,k)$, with an error smaller than its length to first order.

## A dependency changes the question

Suppose $y=x^2$ and define the one-variable function $q(x)=f(x,x^2)$. Then $dq/dx$ changes both inputs of $f$. The chain rule gives

$$
q'(x)=f_x(x,x^2)+2x f_y(x,x^2).
$$

This is generally different from $f_x(x,x^2)$. The instruction “hold $y$ fixed” is part of a partial derivative. Substituting $y=x^2$ before differentiating removes that instruction and produces a different function. [[12.04 Multivariable chain rules]] explains how to track such dependencies.

## Why partial derivatives alone are insufficient

Consider the function

$$
u(x,y)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{xy}{x^2+y^2},&(x,y)\ne(0,0),\\
0,&(x,y)=(0,0).
\end{cases}
$$

Along either coordinate axis the function is identically zero, so $u_x(0,0)=u_y(0,0)=0$. Along the line $y=x\ne0$, however, $u(x,x)=1/2$. The function is not even continuous at the origin. Its two coordinate slices fail to describe its behavior along the diagonal. A total differential with the required approximation property cannot exist there.

A useful sufficient condition is that all first partial derivatives exist and are continuous on an open neighborhood of the point. Then the function is differentiable at that point. Continuity of partial derivatives is sufficient; it is not a necessary condition for differentiability.

## Practice

1. Compute $f_x$ and $f_y$ for $f(x,y)=\log(x^2+y^2)$ on its real domain, then evaluate them at $(1,2)$.
2. For $f(x,y)=xy^2$ and $y=1+x$, compare $f_x(0,1)$ with the derivative at zero of $q(x)=f(x,1+x)$.

## Worked answers

1. The domain excludes $(0,0)$. The ordinary chain rule on each slice gives $f_x=2x/(x^2+y^2)$ and $f_y=2y/(x^2+y^2)$. At $(1,2)$ the common denominator is $5$, so the values are $2/5$ and $4/5$. The differential there sends $(h,k)$ to $(2h+4k)/5$.
2. Holding $y$ fixed gives $f_x=y^2$, so $f_x(0,1)=1$. The composition is $q(x)=x(1+x)^2=x+2x^2+x^3$, whose derivative at zero is also $1$. This equality is specific to the point: the chain rule gives $q'=f_x+f_y$ along the curve, and $f_y=2xy$ vanishes at $(0,1)$. At $x=1$, the values are $f_x(1,2)=4$ and $q'(1)=8$, so they differ.
