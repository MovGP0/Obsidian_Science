> [!TLDR]
> A multiple integral sums a function over a region; an iterated integral computes the sum through one coordinate at a time.

## From small rectangles to an integral

For a bounded region $R\subseteq\mathbb R^2$, divide a containing rectangle into small cells. Multiply the value of $f$ at a sample point by the area of each cell inside the region and add the products. When the sums have one limit as the mesh tends to zero, that limit is the double integral $\iint_R f\,dA$. For a continuous function on a compact region with a sufficiently regular boundary, this Riemann integral exists. The integral of $1$ is the region's area. Negative function values contribute negative terms.

On a rectangle, a continuous function satisfies Fubini's theorem:

$$
\iint_{[a,b]\times[c,d]}f(x,y)\,dA
=\int_a^b\left(\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\right)dx
=\int_c^d\left(\int_a^b f(x,y)\,dx\right)dy.
$$

In Lebesgue integration, absolute integrability also permits this interchange. Tonelli's theorem permits interchange for nonnegative measurable functions, even if the integral is infinite. For signed improper integrals without absolute convergence, do not assume that changing order preserves the result.

## Describe the region with slices

A region of the form

$$
R=\{(x,y):a\leq x\leq b,\ \phi(x)\leq y\leq\psi(x)\}
$$

has vertical slices. Its iterated integral integrates $y$ first, between the lower and upper curves, then $x$ across the horizontal range. The inner limits can depend on the outer variable. They cannot depend on the inner variable itself. A horizontal description instead takes $c\leq y\leq d$ and $\alpha(y)\leq x\leq\beta(y)$. A region may require several such pieces.

![[math-12-integration-region.svg|**Fig. 12.2** The region x² ≤ y ≤ x can be sliced vertically or horizontally, with different dependent limits.]]

The figure describes one set by two systems of inequalities. A vertical segment begins on the parabola and ends on the line. A horizontal segment begins on the line and ends on the square-root curve. A correct change of order changes the limits as well as the differential symbols.

## Worked example: reverse the order

Evaluate $I=\iint_R(x+y)\,dA$, where $R$ lies between $y=x^2$ and $y=x$. Solve $x^2=x$ to find $x=0,1$. On $0\leq x\leq1$, the line is the upper curve. Thus

$$
I=\int_0^1\int_{x^2}^{x}(x+y)\,dy\,dx.
$$

The inner antiderivative is $xy+y^2/2$. Its value between these limits is $3x^2/2-x^3-x^4/2$. Integration gives

$$
I=\frac12-\frac14-\frac1{10}=\frac3{20}.
$$

Now describe a fixed horizontal slice. Since $y\leq x$ and $x^2\leq y$ with $x\geq0$, it has $y\leq x\leq\sqrt y$. Its height ranges over $0\leq y\leq1$. Therefore

$$
I=\int_0^1\int_y^{\sqrt y}(x+y)\,dx\,dy
=\int_0^1\left(\frac y2+y^{3/2}-\frac32y^2\right)dy
=\frac14+\frac25-\frac12=\frac3{20}.
$$

The two calculations agree because they integrate a continuous function over the same compact region. A sketch helps establish that the regions are the same; equal numerical answers alone do not prove the limits correct.

## Three variables and practical order choices

Triple integrals use small boxes and the element $dV$. For the simplex $x,y,z\geq0$, $x+y+z\leq1$, one valid order is

$$
\iiint 1\,dV=\int_0^1\int_0^{1-x}\int_0^{1-x-y}1\,dz\,dy\,dx.
$$

Integrating first in $z$ gives $1-x-y$. Integrating in $y$ gives $(1-x)^2/2$, and the final integral is $1/6$. Each outer limit describes the projection left after an inner variable is removed. Choose an order that keeps slices connected and makes the inner antiderivative simple. [[12.08 Coordinate substitution]] provides another method when rectangular coordinates make the boundary difficult.

## Practice

1. Reverse the order of $\int_0^1\int_x^1 e^{y^2}\,dy\,dx$ and evaluate it.
2. Find the area between $y=x^2$ and $y=2x$.

## Worked answers

1. The original inequalities are $0\leq x\leq1$ and $x\leq y\leq1$. For fixed $y$, they become $0\leq x\leq y$, with $0\leq y\leq1$. The integral is $\int_0^1\int_0^y e^{y^2}\,dx\,dy=\int_0^1 y e^{y^2}\,dy$. Substitute $u=y^2$, $du=2y\,dy$, to get $(e-1)/2$. Reversal avoids a non-elementary inner antiderivative.
2. Intersections solve $x^2=2x$, so $x=0,2$. The line is above the parabola between them. Integrate $1$ over vertical slices: the area is $\int_0^2(2x-x^2)\,dx=[x^2-x^3/3]_0^2=4/3$. Reversing the boundary difference would give an incorrect negative area.
