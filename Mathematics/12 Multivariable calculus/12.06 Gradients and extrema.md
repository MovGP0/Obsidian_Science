> [!TLDR]
> An inner product turns the derivative of a scalar function into a gradient vector; the gradient and Hessian help locate and classify extrema.

## The derivative and its representing vector

For a differentiable scalar function $f$ on an open subset of $\mathbb R^n$, the derivative $Df(\mathbf a)$ is a linear map from vectors to scalars. Given an inner product $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$, the gradient is the unique vector satisfying

$$
Df(\mathbf a)\mathbf v=\langle\operatorname{grad}f(\mathbf a),\mathbf v\rangle
\quad\text{for every }\mathbf v.
$$

With the Euclidean inner product this vector is the column $\nabla f=(f_{x_1},\ldots,f_{x_n})^{\mathsf T}$. With a constant positive-definite symmetric matrix $M$ and inner product $\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v\rangle_M=\mathbf u^{\mathsf T}M\mathbf v$, it is $M^{-1}\nabla f$. The derivative is unchanged, but its representing vector depends on the inner product.

For Euclidean unit vectors, Cauchy–Schwarz gives $D_{\mathbf v}f=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf v\leq\|\nabla f\|$. When the gradient is nonzero, equality holds in its unit direction. Thus the gradient gives the greatest first-order increase. If a differentiable curve stays on a level set, differentiating the constant output gives $\nabla f\cdot\mathbf c'=0$. A nonzero gradient is normal to a smooth level curve.

The circular levels in [[12.01 Functions of several variables]] show this perpendicular relation for $f=x^2+y^2$, whose gradient is $(2x,2y)$.

## Interior stationary points

A local minimum has $f(\mathbf a+\mathbf h)\geq f(\mathbf a)$ for all sufficiently small allowed displacements; reverse the inequality for a local maximum. If the point is interior and $f$ is differentiable, each coordinate slice has an ordinary local extremum. Its derivative is zero, so $\nabla f(\mathbf a)=0$. Such a point is stationary. Stationarity is necessary here, not sufficient.

For a $C^2$ function at a stationary point, the first nonconstant Taylor term is $\tfrac12\mathbf h^{\mathsf T}H_f\mathbf h$. A positive-definite Hessian gives a strict local minimum, a negative-definite Hessian gives a strict local maximum, and an indefinite Hessian gives a saddle. These conclusions follow because the quadratic term dominates the smaller remainder. In two variables, write $\Delta=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2$. If $\Delta>0$, the sign of $f_{xx}$ distinguishes minimum from maximum; if $\Delta<0$, the point is a saddle. If $\Delta=0$, this test is inconclusive.

## Worked example: complete the square

Let $f(x,y)=x^2+2xy+3y^2-4x-8y$. Stationary points solve

$$
2x+2y-4=0,\qquad 2x+6y-8=0.
$$

Subtracting the first equation from the second gives $4y-4=0$, so $y=1$ and $x=1$. The Hessian is $\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\2&6\end{pmatrix}$, with determinant $8>0$ and first entry positive. This proves a strict local minimum. More strongly, set $h=x-1$ and $k=y-1$ to obtain

$$
f(x,y)=-6+h^2+2hk+3k^2=-6+(h+k)^2+2k^2.
$$

The last two terms are nonnegative and vanish together only at $h=k=0$. Hence this is the unique global minimum as well. A local Hessian test alone would not establish global behavior for a general function.

## Constrained extrema

For a regular constraint $g(\mathbf x)=c$, assume $\nabla g(\mathbf a)\ne0$. Allowed tangent directions satisfy $\nabla g\cdot\mathbf v=0$. At a constrained extremum, $Df$ vanishes on those tangent directions. Thus the two normals are parallel:

$$
\nabla f(\mathbf a)=\lambda\nabla g(\mathbf a),\qquad g(\mathbf a)=c.
$$

The scalar $\lambda$ is a Lagrange multiplier. For $f=x+y$ on $x^2+y^2=1$, the equations $(1,1)=\lambda(2x,2y)$ give $x=y$, hence $x=y=\pm1/\sqrt2$. Their values are $\pm\sqrt2$. Continuity and compactness of the circle ensure extrema exist; comparison identifies the global maximum and minimum. If the constraint gradient vanishes, this multiplier condition is not guaranteed. Boundary points and nonsmooth points need separate checks.

## Practice

1. Classify the origin for $f=x^2-y^2$ and for $q=x^4+y^4$.
2. For $f=x^2+y^2$ with inner product matrix $M=\operatorname{diag}(2,1)$, find the gradient at $(1,1)$ and verify its defining property on $(a,b)$.

## Worked answers

1. The first Hessian is $\operatorname{diag}(2,-2)$ and is indefinite. Along the axes the values have opposite signs, so the origin is a saddle. The Hessian of $q$ is zero at the origin and the quadratic test is inconclusive. But $q\geq0$, with equality only at zero, so the origin is a strict global minimum.
2. The Euclidean gradient is $(2,2)$. Multiplication by $M^{-1}$ gives the gradient $(1,2)$ for the stated inner product. Then $\langle(1,2),(a,b)\rangle_M=(1,2)\cdot(2a,b)=2a+2b$, exactly the directional derivative $Df(1,1)(a,b)$.
