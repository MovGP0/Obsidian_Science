> [!TLDR]
> The total derivative is the linear map that approximates every sufficiently small input displacement at once.

## The linear approximation criterion

Let $F:U\to\mathbb R^m$, where $U\subseteq\mathbb R^n$ is open. Use the Euclidean norm $\|\cdot\|$. The map is differentiable at $\mathbf a\in U$ if there is a linear map $A:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$ such that

$$
F(\mathbf a+\mathbf h)=F(\mathbf a)+A\mathbf h+\mathbf r(\mathbf h),
\qquad
\lim_{\mathbf h\to0}\frac{\|\mathbf r(\mathbf h)\|}{\|\mathbf h\|}=0.
$$

The derivative $DF(\mathbf a)$ is this linear map $A$. The condition concerns all small displacement vectors, not just one line at a time. The error is written $o(\|\mathbf h\|)$ and means that its ratio to the input length tends to zero. The constant output $F(\mathbf a)$ is part of the affine approximation, but is not part of the derivative.

The derivative is unique. If two linear maps worked, their difference applied to $t\mathbf v$, divided by $|t|$, would tend to zero for every fixed vector $\mathbf v$. Linearity makes that ratio equal to the fixed length of their difference on $\mathbf v$. Thus that length must be zero for every vector.

## The Jacobian matrix

In standard coordinates the derivative has an $m\times n$ matrix, called the Jacobian:

$$
J_F(\mathbf a)=
\begin{pmatrix}
\partial F_1/\partial x_1&\cdots&\partial F_1/\partial x_n\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\partial F_m/\partial x_1&\cdots&\partial F_m/\partial x_n
\end{pmatrix}_{\mathbf a}.
$$

Rows correspond to output components and columns to input coordinates. Each column describes the response to one coordinate direction. The matrix entries must be the partial derivatives if the total derivative exists. The existence of those entries alone does not prove differentiability; see [[12.02 Partial derivatives]]. Continuous first partial derivatives on a neighborhood do prove it.

## Worked example with an explicit error

Take $F(x,y)=(x^2y,x+y^2)$ at $\mathbf a=(1,2)$. First,

$$
F(1,2)=(2,5),\qquad J_F(1,2)=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\1&4\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Expand directly using $\mathbf h=(h,k)$:

$$
(1+h)^2(2+k)=2+4h+k+2h^2+2hk+h^2k,
$$

and $(1+h)+(2+k)^2=5+h+4k+k^2$. Hence

$$
F(1+h,2+k)=(2,5)+(4h+k,h+4k)
+(2h^2+2hk+h^2k,k^2).
$$

When $\|\mathbf h\|\leq1$, each quadratic term is bounded by a constant times $\|\mathbf h\|^2$, and the cubic term is no larger in order. Dividing the remainder norm by $\|\mathbf h\|$ therefore gives a quantity that tends to zero. This verifies the total derivative from its definition.

For $(h,k)=(0.01,-0.02)$ the linear approximation is $(2.02,4.93)$. The exact value is $(2.019798,4.9304)$. The difference consists precisely of the displayed quadratic and cubic remainder terms.

## Directional derivatives

For a vector $\mathbf v$, define the directional derivative by

$$
D_{\mathbf v}F(\mathbf a)=\lim_{t\to0}
\frac{F(\mathbf a+t\mathbf v)-F(\mathbf a)}t.
$$

If $F$ is differentiable, substituting $\mathbf h=t\mathbf v$ into its linear approximation gives $D_{\mathbf v}F=DF(\mathbf a)\mathbf v$. A unit vector measures change per unit input length. A general vector includes its magnitude in the rate. For the example and $\mathbf v=(3/5,4/5)$, matrix multiplication gives $(16/5,19/5)$.

Directional derivatives in every direction need not assemble into a linear derivative. For $g(x,y)=x^3/(x^2+y^2)$ away from the origin and $g(0,0)=0$, the directional derivative at zero is $v_1^3/(v_1^2+v_2^2)$ for nonzero $\mathbf v$. This exists in every direction but is not linear in $\mathbf v$, so no total derivative exists there.

## Practice

1. Find the Jacobian of $G(x,y,z)=(xy,yz)$ at $(1,2,3)$ and apply it to $(1,-1,2)$.
2. Find the first-order approximation to $f(x,y)=\sqrt{x+y}$ at $(1,3)$ and estimate $f(1.04,2.98)$.

## Worked answers

1. The rows are $(y,x,0)$ and $(0,z,y)$. At the point the matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}2&1&0\\0&3&2\end{pmatrix}$. Applied to the displacement it gives $(2-1,-3+4)=(1,1)$. The domain has dimension three, while the output and directional derivative have dimension two.
2. At the base point $f=2$ and both partial derivatives equal $1/(2\sqrt4)=1/4$. Thus $f(1+h,3+k)\approx2+(h+k)/4$. With $h=0.04$ and $k=-0.02$, the estimate is $2.005$. Differentiability holds because the square root is smooth where $x+y>0$, an open neighborhood of the base point.
