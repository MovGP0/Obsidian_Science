> [!TLDR]
> The Hessian records second-order change; continuous second partial derivatives permit their interchange and give a quadratic Taylor approximation.

## Derivatives of derivatives

Let $f:U\subseteq\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R$. A second partial derivative differentiates a first partial derivative. To avoid an order ambiguity, this article uses $f_{xy}=\partial_y(\partial_x f)$: first differentiate in $x$, then in $y$. The pure partials $f_{xx}$ and $f_{yy}$ use one coordinate twice. A mixed partial uses different coordinates.

If all second partial derivatives exist and are continuous on an open neighborhood of a point, then the mixed partials agree there: $f_{xy}=f_{yx}$. This is a sufficient version of the Schwarz or Clairaut theorem. Merely having both mixed partials at one point does not justify interchanging them.

One way to see why continuity matters is to consider the rectangular difference

$$
f(a+h,b+k)-f(a+h,b)-f(a,b+k)+f(a,b).
$$

For continuous mixed derivatives, the fundamental theorem of calculus writes this as the double integral of $f_{xy}$ over the small rectangle. Reversing the integration order writes the same quantity with $f_{yx}$. Divide by $hk$ and let the rectangle shrink. Continuity makes each average converge to its value at $(a,b)$.

## Hessian and Taylor polynomial

The Hessian matrix is $H_f=(\partial_{x_j}\partial_{x_i}f)_{i,j}$. For a $C^2$ function, meaning that all partial derivatives through order two are continuous, it is symmetric. The quadratic form $\mathbf h^{\mathsf T}H_f\mathbf h$ does not depend on how the same scalar cross terms are grouped.

At a point $\mathbf a$, the second-order Taylor approximation is

$$
f(\mathbf a+\mathbf h)=f(\mathbf a)+Df(\mathbf a)\mathbf h
+\frac12\mathbf h^{\mathsf T}H_f(\mathbf a)\mathbf h
+o(\|\mathbf h\|^2).
$$

For two variables, its quadratic part is

$$
\frac12 f_{xx}h^2+f_{xy}hk+\frac12 f_{yy}k^2.
$$

The cross term has no factor $1/2$ after simplification: the matrix product contains both $f_{xy}hk$ and $f_{yx}kh$. This approximation follows by applying the one-variable Taylor theorem to $g(t)=f(\mathbf a+t\mathbf h)$. The chain rule gives $g'(0)=Df(\mathbf a)\mathbf h$ and $g''(0)=\mathbf h^{\mathsf T}H_f(\mathbf a)\mathbf h$. Continuity of the Hessian controls the remainder uniformly as $\mathbf h$ tends to zero.

## Worked example with a controlled remainder

Let $f(x,y)=e^x\cos y$ and expand about $(0,0)$. At that point $f=1$, $f_x=1$, and $f_y=0$. The second derivatives are $f_{xx}=e^x\cos y$, $f_{xy}=-e^x\sin y$, and $f_{yy}=-e^x\cos y$. Thus

$$
H_f(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
T_2(x,y)=1+x+\frac{x^2-y^2}{2}.
$$

At $(0.1,0.2)$ the approximation gives $1+0.1+(0.01-0.04)/2=1.085$. All third partial derivatives are bounded near zero, so the remainder is $O(\|(x,y)\|^3)$ there. This sharper bound uses third derivatives; a $C^2$ assumption alone gives the stated small-o quadratic remainder without a uniform cubic bound.

## Mixed derivatives can differ

Set $u(0,0)=0$ and, elsewhere, $u(x,y)=xy(x^2-y^2)/(x^2+y^2)$. Taking the first derivative from the definition on the axes gives $u_x(0,y)=-y$ and $u_y(x,0)=x$. Therefore $u_{xy}(0,0)=-1$ but $u_{yx}(0,0)=1$. The function does not meet the continuity hypothesis for second partials near zero. The theorem has no contradiction.

The Hessian also helps classify stationary points in [[12.06 Gradients and extrema]]. A zero eigenvalue means that the quadratic approximation alone may not decide the classification.

## Practice

1. Find the Hessian of $f(x,y)=x^2y+y^3$ and its Taylor polynomial of degree two about $(1,1)$.
2. For $f(x,y)=\log(1+x+y)$, find the quadratic Taylor polynomial at zero and state a domain restriction.

## Worked answers

1. The gradient is $(2xy,x^2+3y^2)$ and the Hessian is $\begin{pmatrix}2y&2x\\2x&6y\end{pmatrix}$. At $(1,1)$, $f=2$, the gradient is $(2,4)$, and the Hessian entries are $2,2,6$. With $h=x-1$, $k=y-1$, the polynomial is $2+2h+4k+h^2+2hk+3k^2$. The omitted terms are cubic.
2. The real domain is $x+y>-1$. At zero, both first derivatives are $1$ and all Hessian entries are $-1$. The polynomial is $x+y-(x+y)^2/2$. It approximates the function for small $(x,y)$ inside the domain, and follows also from the one-variable expansion of $\log(1+s)$ with $s=x+y$.
