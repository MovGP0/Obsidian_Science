title: "Local inversion"

> [!TLDR]
> A continuously differentiable map with a nonsingular Jacobian has a differentiable inverse on sufficiently small neighborhoods, but need not have one globally.

## The inverse-function theorem

Let $U\subseteq\mathbb R^n$ be open and let $F:U\to\mathbb R^n$ be continuously differentiable. Choose $\mathbf a\in U$. If $\det DF(\mathbf a)\ne0$, there are open neighborhoods $U_0$ of $\mathbf a$ and $V_0$ of $F(\mathbf a)$ such that the restriction $F:U_0\to V_0$ is a bijection with a continuously differentiable inverse. For $\mathbf y\in V_0$,

$$
D(F^{-1})(\mathbf y)=\left[DF(F^{-1}(\mathbf y))\right]^{-1}.
$$

In particular, $D(F^{-1})(F(\mathbf a))=[DF(\mathbf a)]^{-1}$. Square brackets on the right mean matrix inverse, not componentwise reciprocal. The theorem requires equal input and output dimensions. A nonsquare Jacobian can have full rank, but its consequences belong to the rank and implicit-function theorems.

## Meaning of the condition

The derivative approximates the map near the point. A nonsingular derivative maps different infinitesimal directions to different directions and has no nonzero vector in its kernel. By continuity, the derivative stays close to that invertible linear map on a small neighborhood. The inverse-function theorem controls the nonlinear remainder enough to give genuine local existence and uniqueness of preimages.

This paragraph explains the mechanism; it is not a complete proof of the existence theorem. The inverse derivative formula does follow directly from [[12.04 Multivariable chain rules]] once existence and differentiability of the inverse are known. Differentiate $F^{-1}\circ F=\operatorname{id}$ to get $D(F^{-1})(F(\mathbf a))DF(\mathbf a)=I$, then multiply by the matrix inverse.

## Worked example: recover nearby inputs

Let $F(x,y)=(x+y,xy)$ and choose $\mathbf a=(1,2)$. Its output is $(3,2)$ and

$$
DF(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\y&x\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
DF(1,2)=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\2&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The determinant is $-1$, so a local inverse exists. Matrix inversion gives

$$
[DF(1,2)]^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\2&-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

For a small output change $(\delta s,\delta p)$, the input change is therefore approximately $(-\delta s+\delta p,2\delta s-\delta p)$. If the output changes from $(3,2)$ to $(3.01,2.02)$, the estimate changes $(x,y)$ by $(0.01,0)$, giving $(1.01,2)$. In this selected case the estimate is exact: the sum is $3.01$ and the product is $2.02$.

There is also an explicit branch. If $s=x+y$ and $p=xy$, then $x$ and $y$ are roots of $t^2-st+p=0$. Near $(1,2)$, choose

$$
x=\frac{s-\sqrt{s^2-4p}}2,\qquad
y=\frac{s+\sqrt{s^2-4p}}2.
$$

The discriminant is positive near $(s,p)=(3,2)$, so this branch is differentiable there. Interchanging the two roots gives a second branch near $(2,1)$. Thus the original map is not globally one-to-one. The theorem keeps the input neighborhood small enough to select one branch.

## Local and global are different conclusions

The map $G(u,v)=(e^u\cos v,e^u\sin v)$ has determinant $e^{2u}>0$ everywhere. Nevertheless, $G(u,v+2\pi)=G(u,v)$. Each point has a suitable local inverse, but there is no inverse on the whole domain. Restricting the angular coordinate selects a branch, as in [[12.08 Coordinate substitution]]. A nonzero determinant everywhere is not a global injectivity test.

A zero determinant does not always forbid an inverse as a set map. The real function $q(x)=x^3$ is a bijection, but its inverse $y^{1/3}$ is not differentiable at zero. If a differentiable inverse existed there, the chain rule would require $(q^{-1})'(0)q'(0)=1$, impossible because $q'(0)=0$. Thus the nonsingular condition is necessary for a differentiable inverse at the corresponding point, while set-theoretic inversion is weaker.

## Practice

1. For $F(x,y)=(x^2-y^2,2xy)$, determine where the theorem applies and find the inverse derivative at the output of $(1,0)$.
2. Explain why $H(x,y)=(x,y^2)$ has a smooth local inverse near $(0,1)$ but has no one-to-one restriction to any open neighborhood of $(0,0)$.

## Worked answers

1. The Jacobian is $\begin{pmatrix}2x&-2y\\2y&2x\end{pmatrix}$ with determinant $4(x^2+y^2)$. The theorem applies at every point except the origin. At $(1,0)$ the derivative is $2I$, so the inverse derivative at $F(1,0)=(1,0)$ is $I/2$. The two inputs $(x,y)$ and $(-x,-y)$ have the same output, so this does not supply a global inverse on the punctured plane.
2. The Jacobian is $\operatorname{diag}(1,2y)$ and is nonsingular at $(0,1)$. Select the branch $(s,t)\mapsto(s,\sqrt t)$ for positive $t$ nearby. Every open neighborhood of $(0,0)$ contains $(0,\varepsilon)$ and $(0,-\varepsilon)$ for some positive $\varepsilon$. They have the same output, so no such neighborhood can make $H$ one-to-one.
