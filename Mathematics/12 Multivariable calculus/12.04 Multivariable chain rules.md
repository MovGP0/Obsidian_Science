> [!TLDR]
> Differentiate a composition by multiplying its derivative maps in the order that inputs pass through the functions.

## Composition of linear approximations

Let $G:U\subseteq\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^p$ be differentiable at $\mathbf a$, and let $F:V\subseteq\mathbb R^p\to\mathbb R^m$ be differentiable at $G(\mathbf a)$. Assume $G(U)\subseteq V$, or restrict to a suitable neighborhood. The chain rule is

$$
D(F\circ G)(\mathbf a)=DF(G(\mathbf a))\,DG(\mathbf a).
$$

The right-hand map acts first. An input displacement is sent through $DG$ and then through $DF$. The matrix dimensions are $(m\times p)(p\times n)=m\times n$, which is a useful check. The evaluation point in $DF$ is the output of $G$, not the original input point.

To understand the rule, write $G(\mathbf a+\mathbf h)=G(\mathbf a)+B\mathbf h+o(\|\mathbf h\|)$. This change is $O(\|\mathbf h\|)$. Substitution into the linear approximation for $F$ gives a first-order term $AB\mathbf h$. Its remaining terms are $o(\|\mathbf h\|)$ because linear maps are bounded in finite dimensions and the second approximation error is small compared with its own input change. This is the same derivative criterion used in [[12.03 Total derivatives]].

## Keep every route of dependence

For $z=f(u,v)$ with $u=u(s,t)$ and $v=v(s,t)$, each independent input has two routes to the output:

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial s}=f_u u_s+f_v v_s,
\qquad
\frac{\partial z}{\partial t}=f_u u_t+f_v v_t.
$$

Here $f_u$ and $f_v$ are evaluated at $(u(s,t),v(s,t))$. If $z=f(t,u(t),v(t))$ also depends directly on $t$, then

$$
\frac{dz}{dt}=f_t+f_u u'(t)+f_v v'(t).
$$

The direct term is essential. Drawing the dependencies on paper can prevent omission of one route.

## Worked example: verify by expansion

Let $f(u,v)=u^2+uv$, $u=s+t$, and $v=st$. Then $f_u=2u+v$ and $f_v=u$. Because $u_s=1$ and $v_s=t$,

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial s}=2(s+t)+st+(s+t)t.
$$

Similarly, $u_t=1$ and $v_t=s$ give

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial t}=2(s+t)+st+(s+t)s.
$$

At $(s,t)=(1,2)$ these are $14$ and $11$. Check them independently: the composition expands to $z=s^2+2st+t^2+s^2t+st^2$. Its derivatives are $2s+2t+2st+t^2$ and $2s+2t+s^2+2st$, which give the same values. The agreement tests the dependency calculation, not just arithmetic at one point.

## Coordinate transformations

For polar coordinates $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$, define $q(r,\theta)=f(x,y)$. On a local coordinate patch with $r>0$,

$$
q_r=f_x\cos\theta+f_y\sin\theta,
\qquad
q_\theta=-r f_x\sin\theta+r f_y\cos\theta.
$$

Solving this two-by-two linear system gives

$$
f_x=\cos\theta\,q_r-\frac{\sin\theta}{r}q_\theta,
\qquad
f_y=\sin\theta\,q_r+\frac{\cos\theta}{r}q_\theta.
$$

The factors $1/r$ require $r>0$. The angular coordinate fails at the origin. These formulas express derivative operators in different coordinates; they are not obtained by replacing each $x$ symbol with $r$.

For a second derivative, differentiate the coefficients as well as the function. For instance, differentiating $f(x(t),y(t))$ twice produces both Hessian terms and terms involving $x''$ and $y''$. A first-order chain rule cannot be applied twice while treating changing coefficients as constants.

## Practice

1. Let $F(u,v)=(u+v,uv)$ and $G(t)=(t^2,e^t)$. Compute $(F\circ G)'(0)$ by matrix multiplication.
2. For $f(x,y)=x^2+y^2$, compute $q_r$ and $q_\theta$ in polar coordinates by both substitution and the chain rule.

## Worked answers

1. At zero, $G(0)=(0,1)$ and $G'(0)=(0,1)^{\mathsf T}$. The Jacobian of $F$ at $(0,1)$ is $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$. Its product with that column is $(1,0)^{\mathsf T}$. Directly, the composition is $(t^2+e^t,t^2e^t)$, whose derivative at zero is $(1,0)$.
2. Substitution gives $q=r^2$, so $q_r=2r$ and $q_\theta=0$. With $f_x=2x$ and $f_y=2y$, the chain formulas give $2r(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta)=2r$ and $-2r^2\cos\theta\sin\theta+2r^2\sin\theta\cos\theta=0$. The zero angular derivative agrees with the circular level sets of this function.
