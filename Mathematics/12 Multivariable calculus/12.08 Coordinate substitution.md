> [!TLDR]
> A coordinate substitution changes the region and the integrand; the absolute Jacobian determinant supplies its local area or volume scale.

## Why a determinant appears

Let $T:U\subseteq\mathbb R^n\to V\subseteq\mathbb R^n$ be a continuously differentiable bijection between open sets, with continuously differentiable inverse. Such a map is a $C^1$ diffeomorphism. For an integrable scalar function and an appropriate region $S\subset U$, the change-of-variables formula is

$$
\int_{T(S)}f(\mathbf x)\,d\mathbf x
=\int_S f(T(\mathbf u))\,|\det DT(\mathbf u)|\,d\mathbf u.
$$

For ordinary bounded regions, assume boundaries have measure zero and the functions are continuous, or apply the corresponding measurable version. If a coordinate map misses or repeats a boundary set of measure zero, the integral can still be computed on the interior patch. Repeated coverage of a region with positive area needs additional multiplicity accounting.

Near $\mathbf u$, the derivative maps a small coordinate rectangle to an approximate parallelogram. Its columns are the images of the coordinate edge vectors. The determinant of those columns is the signed area scale; in higher dimensions it is the signed volume scale. [[12.03 Total derivatives]] explains the underlying linear approximation.

![[math-12-jacobian-area.svg|**Fig. 12.3** The map (u, v) ↦ (2u + v, u + 2v) sends a unit square to a parallelogram of area 3.]]

The two shown edge vectors are $(2,1)$ and $(1,2)$. Their determinant is $4-1=3$. The picture makes the determinant's area meaning visible without requiring either edge to remain perpendicular to the other.

## Orientation and ordinary volume

A positive determinant preserves orientation, and a negative determinant reverses it. An ordinary scalar area integral uses the absolute value because a reflected region still has positive area. For $T(u,v)=(v,u)$, the determinant is $-1$, but the area factor is $1$. Oriented integrals of differential forms retain the determinant's sign; that is a different integration convention.

The determinant can be taken from the forward or inverse map, but the factors are reciprocal. If $\mathbf x=T(\mathbf u)$, then the displayed formula uses $|\det\partial\mathbf x/\partial\mathbf u|$. Using $|\det\partial\mathbf u/\partial\mathbf x|$ at that location would reverse the scale incorrectly.

## Worked example in polar coordinates

Set $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$. The derivative and determinant are

$$
DT=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&r\cos\theta\end{pmatrix},
\qquad \det DT=r.
$$

For $r>0$ and an angular interval of length at most $2\pi$ with a suitable cut, the map is one-to-one on an open patch. The origin and the cut have area zero, so they do not affect disk integrals. With $r\geq0$, the area element is $dA=r\,dr\,d\theta$.

For the disk $D:x^2+y^2\leq4$, compute

$$
\iint_D(x^2+y^2)\,dA
=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^2 r^2\,r\,dr\,d\theta.
$$

The inner integral is $[r^4/4]_0^2=4$. The outer integral gives $8\pi$. Omitting the factor $r$ would give $16\pi/3$, which integrates against the wrong area scale. The radial integrand substitution and the area-factor substitution are separate steps.

## Cylindrical coordinates

In three dimensions, $T(r,\theta,z)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,z)$ leaves the third coordinate unchanged. Its Jacobian determinant is again $r$, so $dV=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz$ in any consistent integration order. For $0\leq z\leq3$ and $x^2+y^2\leq1$, the volume is

$$
\int_0^3\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1 r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz
=3\cdot2\pi\cdot\frac12=3\pi.
$$

At $r=0$ the derivative is singular because the angle is not a distinct coordinate there. A singular coordinate description need not mean that the original region or function is singular. [[12.09 Local inversion]] states when coordinates have a local inverse.

## Practice

1. Find the area of the ellipse $x^2/9+y^2/4\leq1$ with $x=3u$, $y=2v$.
2. Evaluate $\iint_R(x-y)\,dA$ for the parallelogram image of $0\leq u,v\leq1$ under $x=2u+v$, $y=u+2v$.

## Worked answers

1. The substitution gives the unit disk $u^2+v^2\leq1$. Its determinant is $6$, so the ellipse area is $6$ times the unit disk area, or $6\pi$. Both semiaxis scales contribute; using only one would miss part of the area change.
2. The determinant is $3$ and the transformed integrand is $u-v$. Thus the integral is $3\int_0^1\int_0^1(u-v)\,dv\,du=3\int_0^1(u-1/2)\,du=0$. The zero result follows from the symmetry between $u$ and $v$; the signed integrand has positive and negative parts even though the area factor is positive.
