> [!TLDR]
> A path homotopy deforms one path into another while retaining stated endpoint conditions; based loops form a group after such deformations are identified.

## Paths and endpoint conditions

Let $X$ be a topological space and let $I=[0,1]$. A path is a continuous map $\gamma:I\to X$. A loop based at $x_0\in X$ satisfies $\gamma(0)=\gamma(1)=x_0$. A loop can cross itself or repeat part of its image: the map, including its order of traversal, matters.

Two paths $\gamma_0,\gamma_1$ from $a$ to $b$ are **homotopic relative to endpoints** if a continuous map $H:I\times I\to X$ satisfies

$$
H(t,0)=\gamma_0(t),\quad H(t,1)=\gamma_1(t),\quad
H(0,s)=a,\quad H(1,s)=b.
$$

The parameter $t$ runs along a path; $s$ runs through the deformation. All four conditions matter. Moving the endpoints is a different problem. A based loop is null-homotopic if it is homotopic with its basepoint fixed to the constant loop $c(t)=x_0$.

A space is **contractible** if its identity map can be deformed to a constant map: there are $p\in X$ and continuous $C:X\times I\to X$ with $C(x,0)=x$ and $C(x,1)=p$. This contracts the whole space coherently. A loop being contractible does not mean that the space containing it is contractible.

## Worked example: a valid linear deformation

Suppose $X\subseteq\mathbb R^n$ is convex, so every line segment joining two points of $X$ stays in $X$. For two paths with the same endpoints define

$$
H(t,s)=(1-s)\gamma_0(t)+s\gamma_1(t).
$$

The formula is continuous because addition and scalar multiplication are continuous. Convexity keeps its image in $X$. At $s=0$ it is $\gamma_0$, and at $s=1$ it is $\gamma_1$. At the initial endpoint,

$$
H(0,s)=(1-s)a+sa=a,
$$

and the same calculation gives $H(1,s)=b$. This verifies the full homotopy definition. Taking $\gamma_1(t)=x_0$ contracts every based loop in a convex space. Taking $C(x,s)=(1-s)x+sp$ contracts the whole convex space to $p$.

For example, the upper semicircle path $\gamma_0(t)=(\cos\pi t,\sin\pi t)$ and the diameter path $\gamma_1(t)=(1-2t,0)$ have common endpoints $(1,0)$ and $(-1,0)$. Their linear homotopy lies in the closed unit disk, which is convex. The same formula is not a homotopy in the circle: at $t=s=1/2$ it gives $(0,1/2)$, which is not on the circle. The permitted target space must be checked throughout a deformation.

## The fundamental-group idea

Take all based loops at $x_0$ and identify loops homotopic relative to the basepoint. Their equivalence classes form $\pi_1(X,x_0)$, the **fundamental group**. To multiply classes, first follow $\gamma$ and then $\eta$:

$$
(\gamma*\eta)(t)=
\begin{cases}
\gamma(2t),&0\leq t\leq1/2,\\
\eta(2t-1),&1/2\leq t\leq1.
\end{cases}
$$

The formulas agree at $1/2$ because both paths meet at $x_0$. The constant loop is the identity class. The reversed loop $\overline\gamma(t)=\gamma(1-t)$ supplies the inverse. Concatenation is associative on homotopy classes, although different parenthesizations allocate different parameter times to the original paths. A continuous change of speed gives the required homotopy.

On $S^1$, the loops $\gamma_n(t)=e^{2\pi int}$ have integer winding $n$. Continuous lifted angles show that winding is unchanged by based homotopy, that every based circle loop has one of these classes, and that concatenation adds the integers. Thus $\pi_1(S^1,1)\cong\mathbb Z$; [[27.05 Winding numbers]] supplies the calculation. In contrast, a convex space has the trivial fundamental group.

A path-connected space with trivial fundamental group is called simply connected. Contractible spaces are simply connected, but trivial fundamental group alone need not imply contractibility. For example, the two-dimensional sphere is simply connected and is not contractible; proving the second assertion needs an invariant beyond the fundamental group. No such general converse is assumed here.

## Practice

1. Contract the based loop $\gamma(t)=(\cos2\pi t,\sin2\pi t)$ in $\mathbb R^2$ to $(1,0)$ with its basepoint fixed. Why does the same contraction fail in $\mathbb R^2\setminus\{0\}$?
2. If $\gamma_n(t)=e^{2\pi int}$, what classes are represented by $\gamma_2*\gamma_{-3}$ and by the reverse of $\gamma_2$?

## Worked answers

1. Set $H(t,s)=(1-s)\gamma(t)+s(1,0)$. Its endpoints stay at $(1,0)$, and it is a continuous contraction in the plane. At $(t,s)=(1/2,1/2)$ it equals $(0,0)$, so it leaves the punctured plane. In fact a winding-one loop cannot contract there, because its winding would have to change to zero.
2. Concatenation gives winding $2+(-3)=-1$, so the first class is $[\gamma_{-1}]$. Reversal changes the signed angle increment from $4\pi$ to $-4\pi$, so the second class is $[\gamma_{-2}]$. Equality here means equality of homotopy classes, not equality of parametrized paths.
