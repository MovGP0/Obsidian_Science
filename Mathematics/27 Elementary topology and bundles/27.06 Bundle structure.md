> [!TLDR]
> A fibre bundle is a space that looks locally like a product over its base, although those local products need not combine into one global product.

## Base, total space, and fibres

A **fibre bundle** consists of a total space $E$, a base space $B$, a continuous surjection $\pi:E\to B$, and a model fibre $F$. The fibre above $b\in B$ is

$$
E_b=\pi^{-1}(\{b\}).
$$

The map $\pi$ records the base point of each total-space point. The decisive condition is **local triviality**: every base point lies in an open set $U\subseteq B$ with a homeomorphism

$$
\Phi_U:\pi^{-1}(U)\longrightarrow U\times F,
\qquad \operatorname{pr}_1\circ\Phi_U=\pi.
$$

Here $\operatorname{pr}_1(b,f)=b$ is projection to the first coordinate. The last equation says that the chart preserves the base point. It is insufficient for $\pi^{-1}(U)$ to be homeomorphic to a product in a way that mixes base and fibre arbitrarily. Each restriction $\Phi_U:E_b\to\{b\}\times F$ identifies a fibre with its model.

The product bundle is $E=B\times F$ with $\pi(b,f)=b$. A bundle is globally trivial if it has one such trivialization over all of $B$. Local triviality is part of the definition; global triviality is an additional property. A map with fibres that all happen to be homeomorphic need not satisfy local triviality.

## A section chooses one point per fibre

A **section** is a continuous map $s:B\to E$ such that

$$
\pi\circ s=\operatorname{id}_B.
$$

Thus $s(b)\in E_b$ for each $b$. In a local trivialization it has the form $\Phi_U(s(b))=(b,f_U(b))$ for a continuous map $f_U:U\to F$. Local functions must agree through the change of fibre coordinates on overlaps; they cannot be selected independently.

A section is a right inverse of $\pi$, not necessarily its two-sided inverse. For example, in $B\times\mathbb R$ the section $s(b)=(b,0)$ satisfies $\pi s=\operatorname{id}_B$, but $s\pi(b,v)=(b,0)$ usually differs from $(b,v)$. Existence of one section does not generally make a fibre bundle trivial. A vector bundle always has its zero section, including nontrivial vector bundles.

## Worked example: two points above each circle point

Let $E=B=S^1$ and set $\pi(z)=z^2$, with model fibre $F=\{+1,-1\}$ carrying the discrete topology. If $b=e^{i\theta}$, its fibre is

$$
E_b=\{e^{i\theta/2},-e^{i\theta/2}\}.
$$

On an open arc $U$ with a continuous real angle $\theta_U$, each $z\in\pi^{-1}(U)$ has a unique sign $\epsilon\in F$ such that $z=\epsilon e^{i\theta_U(z^2)/2}$. Define

$$
\Phi_U(z)=(z^2,\epsilon),\qquad
\Phi_U^{-1}(b,\epsilon)=\epsilon e^{i\theta_U(b)/2}.
$$

Both maps are continuous on the two separate sheets, and $\operatorname{pr}_1\Phi_U(z)=z^2$. This verifies local triviality. A circle cannot have one continuous global angle, so the local formulas require several arcs.

This bundle has no section. Suppose $s:S^1\to S^1$ were continuous with $s(b)^2=b$. Along $b(t)=e^{2\pi it}$, choose a continuous lifted angle $\alpha(t)$ for $s(b(t))$. The equation gives

$$
2\alpha(t)-2\pi t\in2\pi\mathbb Z.
$$

The left side is continuous, so its integer value is constant. Subtract endpoint values to get $2[\alpha(1)-\alpha(0)]=2\pi$, hence $\alpha(1)-\alpha(0)=\pi$. But $b(0)=b(1)$ forces $s(b(0))=s(b(1))$, whose lifted angle difference must be a multiple of $2\pi$. This contradiction rules out a section. The lifting idea is explained in [[27.05 Winding numbers]].

Also, the total circle is connected, while $S^1\times\{+1,-1\}$ has two connected components. Thus this locally product bundle is not a global product. Connectivity provides a second obstruction independent of the section calculation.

## Practice

1. In the product bundle $\mathbb R^2\times\mathbb R\to\mathbb R^2$, write a section whose fibre coordinate is $x^2+y^2$. Verify the section equation.
2. In the square-map bundle, calculate the fibre over $-1$. Explain why choosing the square root with argument in $[0,\pi)$ does not construct a continuous global section.

## Worked answers

1. Define $s(x,y)=((x,y),x^2+y^2)$. It is continuous because all its coordinates are polynomials. Projection removes the last coordinate, so $\pi(s(x,y))=(x,y)$, as required.
2. The solutions of $z^2=-1$ are $i$ and $-i$. A chosen angular range can select one root at every base point as a set-theoretic rule. When the base angle passes the seam from nearly $2\pi$ to $0$, the selected root changes from nearly $-1$ to $1$. These values do not approach the same point. The rule is discontinuous, consistent with the general no-section proof.
