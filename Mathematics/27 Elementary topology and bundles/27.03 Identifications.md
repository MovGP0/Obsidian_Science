> [!TLDR]
> Identifying the endpoints of an interval produces a circle, whose angular coordinate is periodic and cannot be one global real coordinate.

## From an interval to a quotient space

Fix a real number $L>0$. On $I=[0,L]$, declare $0\sim L$ and leave every interior point equivalent only to itself. This is an equivalence relation. Its classes are $\{t\}$ for $0<t<L$ and the single class $\{0,L\}$. Write $I/\!\sim$ for the set of classes and

$$
q:I\longrightarrow I/\!\sim,\qquad q(t)=[t]
$$

for the quotient map. The **quotient topology** declares $V\subseteq I/\!\sim$ open exactly when $q^{-1}(V)$ is open in the interval's subspace topology. This ensures that neighbourhoods of the joined point contain pieces near both ends of the original interval.

Define $F:I\to S^1\subseteq\mathbb C$ by

$$
F(t)=\exp(2\pi i t/L)=\cos(2\pi t/L)+i\sin(2\pi t/L).
$$

Here $i^2=-1$, and $S^1=\{z\in\mathbb C:|z|=1\}$. The map has equal values at $0$ and $L$, and otherwise takes distinct values. It therefore defines a bijection $\overline F:I/\!\sim\to S^1$ with $\overline F([t])=F(t)$. A basic quotient property says that $h$ on the quotient is continuous exactly when $h\circ q$ is continuous. Applying it to $\overline F$ proves continuity.

The domain is compact, as the continuous image under $q$ of $[0,L]$. The circle is Hausdorff: two different points have disjoint sufficiently small Euclidean balls. A continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism. Indeed, closed subsets of a compact space are compact, their images are compact, and compact subsets of a Hausdorff space are closed. Thus the bijection is closed and its inverse is continuous. This proves that the quotient is a circle, not just that it looks like one.

## Periodic coordinates and the seam

The equivalent description $\mathbb R/L\mathbb Z$ identifies $t$ and $t+kL$ for all integers $k$. Here $L\mathbb Z$ means $\{kL:k\in\mathbb Z\}$. A periodic coordinate records a class $[t]$, not a unique real number. Addition of classes is well defined, since changing either representative changes their sum by an integer multiple of $L$.

Choosing representatives in $[0,L)$ gives a bijective labelling, but it does not give a homeomorphism from that ordinary subspace interval to the circle. The points $t_n=L-1/n$ for sufficiently large $n$ approach the joined point on the circle. Their chosen representatives do not approach the representative $0$. This discontinuity is the coordinate seam. Local angular coordinates can be continuous on open arcs, but a single real-valued angle on the entire circle cannot be continuous and undo the quotient map.

## Worked example: functions that descend

A continuous function $f:[0,L]\to\mathbb R$ defines a continuous function $\widetilde f$ on the quotient precisely when $f(0)=f(L)$. Necessity follows because both endpoints are the same class. For sufficiency, define $\widetilde f([t])=f(t)$; the endpoint condition makes this independent of the representative, and the quotient property gives continuity.

With $L=2\pi$, the function $f(t)=\cos t$ descends, since $f(0)=f(2\pi)=1$. The function $g(t)=t$ does not: it would assign both $0$ and $2\pi$ to one point. The function $h(t)=t(2\pi-t)$ does descend continuously because both endpoint values are zero. It does not define a smooth function across the seam in the standard smooth circle structure: the one-sided derivatives $h'(0)=2\pi$ and $h'(2\pi)=-2\pi$ do not match. Continuity requires value matching; smooth periodic functions require matching derivatives too.

The same distinction explains why a loop can return to its starting circle point while its continuous lifted angle changes by $2\pi n$. The integer $n$ is studied in [[27.05 Winding numbers]].

## Practice

1. Which functions on $[0,1]$ descend continuously to the endpoint quotient: $t^2$, $\sin(2\pi t)$, and $t(1-t)$?
2. On $\mathbb R/5\mathbb Z$, compute $[4]+[3]$ and determine whether $[1]=[6]$. Does $t\mapsto t^2$ define a real-valued function of a class?

## Worked answers

1. $t^2$ has endpoint values $0$ and $1$, so it fails. The sine function has zero at both ends, so it descends. The product also has zero at both ends, so it descends continuously. Its derivatives $1$ and $-1$ do not match, so the descended function is not smooth at the join.
2. $[4]+[3]=[7]=[2]$, since $7-2=5$. Also $[1]=[6]$, because $6-1=5$. Squaring is not representative independent as a real-valued rule: the same class would give $1^2=1$ and $6^2=36$. Therefore it defines no such function.
