title: "Compactness"

> [!TLDR]
> Compactness means that every open cover has a finite subcover; it is independent of connectedness and of manifold boundary.

## The open-cover definition

An **open cover** of a space $X$ is a family $\{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in I}$ of open subsets of $X$ with $X=\bigcup_{\alpha\in I}U_\alpha$. A **finite subcover** is a finite choice $U_{\alpha_1},\ldots,U_{\alpha_m}$ whose union still contains all of $X$. The space is compact if every open cover has a finite subcover. The sets in a subcover must come from the given cover; replacing them with one larger set does not meet the definition.

For subsets of Euclidean space $\mathbb R^n$, the **Heine–Borel theorem** states that compactness is equivalent to being closed and bounded. Here closed and bounded refer to the ambient Euclidean space. This test must not be extended to arbitrary topological or metric spaces without further hypotheses. Compactness itself uses only the open sets from [[27.01 Topological language]].

For example, $[0,1]$ is compact. One proof of the interval case uses bisection. If a given cover has no finite subcover, at least one of the two closed half-intervals also has no finite subcover. Repeat the step to obtain nested closed intervals of lengths $2^{-n}$. Completeness of $\mathbb R$ gives a common point $p$. Some cover member contains $p$ and therefore contains a relative interval about $p$. A sufficiently short nested interval lies within this member, a contradiction. The argument uses both completeness and the inclusion of the limiting endpoints.

## A worked noncompact example

Consider $X=(0,1)$ and the relatively open sets

$$
U_n=(1/n,2)\cap X,\qquad n=2,3,\ldots.
$$

They cover $X$: given $x>0$, choose $n>1/x$, so $1/n<x$. Now select any finite list of indices and let $N$ be its largest index. These sets are nested, so their union is $U_N$. The point $x=1/(2N)$ belongs to $X$ but not to $U_N$. No finite selection covers $X$, and $X$ is noncompact.

The missing point $0$ is not itself required to be covered. Its role is that points of $X$ can approach it without a limit in $X$. The sequence $x_n=1/n$, for $n\geq2$, makes the same obstruction visible. In a metric space compactness is equivalent to every sequence having a subsequence that converges to a point of the space. The open-cover definition also applies where sequence tests are insufficient.

## Three independent distinctions

Compactness is not connectedness. A finite union of compact sets is compact: take a finite subcover for each member and combine the finite lists. Thus $[0,1]\cup[2,3]$ is compact but disconnected. The real line is connected but noncompact. The circle $S^1=\{(x,y):x^2+y^2=1\}$ is both compact and connected.

Compactness also does not mean that a manifold has a boundary. A manifold boundary point has a neighbourhood modelled on a half-space rather than all of Euclidean space. The following examples separate the concepts:

| Space | Compact? | Connected? | Manifold boundary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $[0,1]$ | Yes | Yes | $\{0,1\}$ |
| $S^1$ | Yes | Yes | Empty |
| $\mathbb R$ | No | Yes | Empty |
| $[0,\infty)$ | No | Yes | $\{0\}$ |

Do not confuse manifold boundary with the boundary of a subset in a chosen ambient space. As a subset of $\mathbb R^2$, the circle has empty interior and its ambient boundary is the entire circle. As a one-dimensional manifold it has no boundary points.

Continuous images of compact spaces are compact: pull an open cover back to the domain, choose a finite subcover there, then use the corresponding target sets. This proves that the circle is compact from its continuous parametrization by $[0,2\pi]$. A continuous real-valued function on a nonempty compact space attains a maximum and minimum, since its image is a nonempty compact subset of $\mathbb R$. These are global conclusions from the cover property.

## Practice

1. Classify $K=\{0\}\cup\{1/n:n\in\mathbb N\}$ as compact or noncompact in $\mathbb R$. What changes when $0$ is removed?
2. Give a compact disconnected space with no manifold boundary. Explain all three claims.

## Worked answers

1. $K$ is bounded. Its only possible accumulation point outside its isolated points is $0$, which is included, so $K$ is closed. Heine–Borel gives compactness. In $K\setminus\{0\}$ the sequence $1/n$ has no subsequence converging inside the set, because every subsequence still tends to $0$. Hence the smaller set is noncompact.
2. Use two disjoint circles in the plane. Each is closed and bounded, and their finite union is compact. The two components are disjoint nonempty relatively open sets, so they separate the union. Every point has a neighbourhood homeomorphic to an open interval; there is no half-interval endpoint. Thus the one-dimensional manifold boundary is empty.
