title: "Topological language"

> [!TLDR]
> A topology specifies open sets and makes continuity, connectedness, and equivalence of spaces precise without a distance formula.

## Open sets and neighbourhoods

A **topological space** is a set $X$ with a family $\mathcal T$ of subsets called open sets. The family must contain $\varnothing$ and $X$, every union of members of $\mathcal T$, and every finite intersection of members of $\mathcal T$. Infinite intersections need not be open. In the usual topology on $\mathbb R$, each interval $(a,b)$ is open, but

$$
\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1/n,1/n)=\{0\}
$$

is not open. No positive interval about $0$ fits inside a singleton.

A **neighbourhood** of $x\in X$ is a set $N$ that contains an open set $U$ with $x\in U$. In this convention a neighbourhood need not itself be open. A set is closed if its complement in $X$ is open. Closed is not the opposite of open: $X$ and $\varnothing$ are both open and closed.

If $A\subseteq X$, its **subspace topology** has open sets $A\cap U$, where $U$ is open in $X$. Thus $[0,1/2)$ is open in $[0,1]$, since it equals $[0,1]\cap(-1,1/2)$. It is not open in $\mathbb R$. Always state the space relative to which a set is open.

## Continuity and homeomorphisms

A map $f:X\to Y$ is continuous if $f^{-1}(V)$ is open in $X$ for every open $V\subseteq Y$. The inverse-image notation means the set $\{x:f(x)\in V\}$; it does not require an inverse function. This definition implies the usual epsilon–delta condition for metric spaces.

A **homeomorphism** is a bijection $f$ for which both $f$ and $f^{-1}$ are continuous. Homeomorphic spaces have the same topological structure. A drawing can suggest a homeomorphism, but a continuous bijection alone is insufficient. [[27.03 Identifications]] gives a useful failure at the endpoint of an angular interval.

For a worked example, let $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ be $f(x)=x^3$. It is continuous because polynomials are continuous. If $x<y$, then $x^3<y^3$, so it is injective. Every real number $y$ has the real cube root $\sqrt[3]{y}$, so it is surjective. The inverse $g(y)=\sqrt[3]{y}$ is continuous. One can check this without differentiation: a strictly increasing bijection sends an interval $(a,b)$ to $(a^3,b^3)$, so inverse images under $g$ of open intervals are open. Therefore $f$ is a homeomorphism, even though $g$ has no finite derivative at $0$. Topological equivalence does not require differentiability.

## Connectedness

A **separation** of $X$ is a representation $X=U\cup V$ by disjoint nonempty subsets that are open in $X$. A space is connected if it has no separation. For example,

$$
X=(-2,-1)\cup(1,2)
$$

is disconnected: its two displayed intervals form a separation. An interval in $\mathbb R$ is connected. For a proof, suppose an interval $J$ has a separation $U,V$. Choose $u\in U$ and $v\in V$, ordered as $u<v$ after interchanging the parts if needed. Let $c=\sup(U\cap[u,v])$, which lies in $[u,v]\subseteq J$. If $c\in V$, openness of $V$ leaves a short interval about $c$ with no points of $U$, contrary to the supremum property; $c=u$ is excluded because $u\in U$. If $c\in U$, then $c<v$ and openness of $U$ supplies a point of $U\cap[u,v]$ larger than $c$, also a contradiction. Thus no separation exists.

A path from $a$ to $b$ is a continuous map $\gamma:[0,1]\to X$ with $\gamma(0)=a$ and $\gamma(1)=b$. A space is **path connected** if every pair admits such a path. Path connectedness implies connectedness: a separation would pull back along a joining path to a separation of $[0,1]$. The converse can fail in general spaces; no converse is needed for the interval and circle examples here.

Continuous images of connected spaces are connected. Indeed, a separation of $f(X)$ would have disjoint nonempty open inverse images that separate $X$. This gives a practical test: no continuous map from an interval can have an image consisting of exactly two distinct points. See [[27.02 Compactness]] for another property preserved by continuous maps.

## Practice

1. Let $X=\{a,b\}$ have the discrete topology, in which every subset is open. Is $X$ connected? Is every map from $X$ to an arbitrary topological space continuous?
2. In $A=[0,2]$, decide whether $[0,1)$ and $\{0\}$ are open. Then explain why $\gamma(t)=2t$ proves that $A$ is connected.

## Worked answers

1. The sets $\{a\}$ and $\{b\}$ form a separation, so $X$ is disconnected. Every inverse image of an open target set is a subset of $X$, and every such subset is open. Thus every map with this domain is continuous. Continuity does not make a disconnected domain connected.
2. We have $[0,1)=A\cap(-1,1)$, so it is open in $A$. If $\{0\}=A\cap U$ with $U$ open in $\mathbb R$ and $0\in U$, then some interval $(-\epsilon,\epsilon)$ lies in $U$. This also includes positive points of $A$, a contradiction. Hence $\{0\}$ is not open. The map $\gamma$ is continuous and maps the connected interval $[0,1]$ onto $A$. Its image is therefore connected.
