> [!TLDR]
> Product bundles have a global product description, vector bundles carry linear fibres, and principal bundles carry fibres on which a group acts freely and transitively.

## Product and vector bundles

The **product bundle** with base $B$ and fibre $F$ is $B\times F\to B$, $(b,f)\mapsto b$. A general bundle is called trivial when it is isomorphic over $B$ to such a product. An isomorphism over $B$ must preserve projection; an arbitrary homeomorphism between total spaces is insufficient.

A rank-$r$ real **vector bundle** has a real vector space $E_b$ of dimension $r$ above each $b$. Its local trivializations identify $E_b$ linearly with $\mathbb R^r$. Therefore transition maps lie in $\mathrm{GL}(r,\mathbb R)$, the group of invertible real matrices. For complex vector bundles replace $\mathbb R$ by $\mathbb C$. The rank is fixed here; more generally it is locally constant.

Every vector bundle has the zero section $b\mapsto0_b$. This section does not select a basis. A **global frame** is a list of $r$ continuous sections $e_1,\ldots,e_r$ that form a basis in every fibre. A frame gives a vector-bundle isomorphism

$$
B\times\mathbb R^r\longrightarrow E,\qquad
(b,(a_1,\ldots,a_r))\longmapsto\sum_{j=1}^r a_je_j(b).
$$

Local frames show that this map and its inverse are continuous. Conversely, a vector-bundle trivialization supplies a global frame by taking the standard basis vectors. Thus a vector bundle is trivial precisely when it admits a global frame. For rank one, this means one nowhere-zero section.

## Worked example: the Möbius line bundle

Define

$$
E=([0,1]\times\mathbb R)/((0,v)\sim(1,-v)),
\qquad \pi([t,v])=e^{2\pi it}.
$$

Each fibre has a real line structure, and local intervals on the base give linear trivializations. A section written on $[0,1]$ as $s(e^{2\pi it})=[t,f(t)]$ must satisfy

$$
f(1)=-f(0).
$$

If $f(0)=0$, the section already vanishes. If $f(0)>0$, then $f(1)<0$, so the intermediate value theorem forces a zero. If $f(0)<0$, reverse the signs to obtain the same result. Every continuous section therefore vanishes somewhere. No global frame exists, so this line bundle is nontrivial. The zero section exists and satisfies the endpoint equation, which illustrates why existence of a section alone is too weak.

The gluing figure in [[27.07 Transition functions]] shows the endpoint rule. With $(0,v)\sim(1,v)$, the constant $f(t)=1$ would be a nowhere-zero section and would trivialize the product line bundle.

## Principal bundles

Let $G$ be a topological group. A **principal $G$-bundle** $P\to B$ has a continuous right action $p\cdot g$ that preserves each fibre. The action is **free** if $p\cdot g=p$ implies $g=1$. It is **transitive on each fibre** if for any $p,q$ above the same base point there is a $g$ with $q=p\cdot g$. Together these conditions give exactly one such $g$. A principal bundle also has local equivariant trivializations with model fibre $G$ and its right multiplication action.

A fibre of a principal bundle resembles $G$, but has no distinguished identity element until one point is chosen. This is different from a vector fibre, which has a canonical zero. A global section $s:B\to P$ does trivialize a principal bundle:

$$
B\times G\longrightarrow P,\qquad (b,g)\longmapsto s(b)\cdot g.
$$

Freeness and transitivity give a unique inverse in each fibre; local trivializations give continuity. Therefore the section test has a stronger conclusion for principal bundles than for arbitrary bundles.

A concrete example is $P=S^1$, $B=S^1$, $\pi(z)=z^2$, and $G=\{+1,-1\}$ acting by multiplication. Each fibre is a pair of roots. Multiplication by $-1$ exchanges them, so the action is free and transitive. The no-section proof in [[27.06 Bundle structure]] shows this principal bundle is nontrivial.

For any rank-$r$ vector bundle, the ordered bases of each fibre form its **frame bundle**. The group $\mathrm{GL}(r,\mathbb R)$ acts freely and transitively by change of basis. This principal bundle records choices of frames; its associated vector bundle records vectors. The fibre types and roles differ even when the two constructions describe related information.

## Practice

1. Show that $s(b)=(b,(1,1))$ is a nowhere-zero section of $B\times\mathbb R^2$. Does this one section alone form a frame?
2. Why is ordinary scalar multiplication by $\mathbb R^\times$ on all of $\mathbb R$ not a principal action? What changes if the zero vector is removed from a one-dimensional real vector space?

## Worked answers

1. Projection gives $\pi s(b)=b$, and $(1,1)$ is never zero, so it is a nowhere-zero section. A rank-two frame needs two linearly independent vectors in every fibre. This single section supplies only one. Adding $(b,(1,-1))$ gives a frame, since the determinant of the matrix with these columns is $-2\ne0$.
2. Every nonzero scalar fixes $0$, so freeness fails at zero. The action also cannot send $0$ to a nonzero point, so transitivity fails. In a one-dimensional vector space with zero removed, every pair of nonzero vectors differs by exactly one nonzero scalar. The action there is free and transitive, giving the fibre of its principal frame bundle.
