title: "Connections and holonomy"

> [!TLDR]
> A connection defines transport along paths; curvature detects local transport around small loops, while flat connections can still have nontrivial transport around noncontractible loops.

## A concrete connection convention

Work first on a trivial complex line bundle $B\times\mathbb C$ over a smooth manifold $B$. A section is represented by a complex-valued function $v$. Let $A$ be a real smooth one-form on $B$ and choose the covariant derivative

$$
\nabla v=dv+iAv.
$$

Along a piecewise smooth path $\gamma:[0,1]\to B$, parallel transport requires the fibre value $v(t)$ to satisfy

$$
\frac{dv}{dt}+iA_{\gamma(t)}(\dot\gamma(t))v(t)=0.
$$

Solving this scalar linear equation gives

$$
v(1)=\exp\left(-i\int_\gamma A\right)v(0).
$$

The minus sign follows from the stated $\nabla=d+iA$ convention. For a closed loop based at $b_0$, this transport is an automorphism of the same fibre. Its multiplier $\operatorname{Hol}_\gamma=\exp(-i\oint_\gamma A)$ is its **holonomy**. An open path also has transport, but its endpoints lie in different fibres, so calling its multiplier holonomy requires additional frame choices.

For a general vector bundle, a connection differentiates sections and obeys the Leibniz rule $\nabla(fs)=df\otimes s+f\nabla s$. Local connection matrices produce linear transport equations. In higher rank the matrices may not commute, so the simple exponential of an integral must be replaced by the solution of that matrix equation. The line-bundle case lets us see the topology without this additional algebra.

## Curvature and contractible loops

For the chosen line connection, the curvature operator is $\nabla^2=i\,dA$. Write $F=dA$ for the real curvature two-form. If $\gamma$ bounds an oriented smooth disk $D$ on which $A$ is defined, Stokes' theorem gives

$$
\operatorname{Hol}_\gamma
=\exp\left(-i\int_D F\right).
$$

The orientation of $\gamma$ is the boundary orientation of $D$. A contractible loop can have nontrivial holonomy when curvature is nonzero. Contractibility alone does not force transport to return a vector unchanged.

For a worked example on $\mathbb R^2$, let $A=cx\,dy$, with real constant $c$. Then $F=c\,dx\wedge dy$. Traverse the rectangle $[0,a]\times[0,b]$ counterclockwise. The bottom and top edges have $dy=0$, and the left edge has $x=0$. Only the right edge contributes:

$$
\oint_\gamma A=\int_0^b ca\,dy=cab,
\qquad \operatorname{Hol}_\gamma=e^{-icab}.
$$

For $a=b=1$ and $c=\pi$, the multiplier is $-1$. The rectangle loop is contractible in the plane, yet its holonomy is nontrivial. Shrinking the rectangle makes its area and integral tend to zero.

## Flat connections and global transport

A connection is **flat** when its curvature vanishes. For a smooth flat connection, transport along paths with fixed endpoints is unchanged by homotopy; consequently its holonomy on a contractible loop is identity. In the present line-bundle model, pull $A$ back along a smooth homotopy square. Stokes' theorem makes the boundary integral equal to the integral of $dA=0$. The two fixed-endpoint edges contribute zero, so the two path integrals agree. Piecewise smooth paths give the same conclusion by subdivision. This statement depends on flatness and does not apply to the rectangle example above.

Now set $B=S^1$ and let $\alpha$ be the globally defined angular one-form. In a local angle coordinate $\theta$ it is $d\theta$; on overlaps angle branches differ by constant multiples of $2\pi$, so their differentials agree. Take $A=a\alpha$, where $a\in\mathbb R$. Since the base is one-dimensional, $F=dA=0$. A loop with winding $n$ nevertheless has

$$
\oint_\gamma A=2\pi an,\qquad
\operatorname{Hol}_\gamma=e^{-2\pi ian}.
$$

For $a=1/2$ and $n=1$, this is $-1$. For $n=0$, it is $1$. Thus zero local curvature does not imply trivial global holonomy. Winding, described in [[27.05 Winding numbers]], distinguishes the loops. In this flat example the rule $n\mapsto e^{-2\pi ian}$ is a group homomorphism from $\pi_1(S^1)\cong\mathbb Z$ to $U(1)=\{z:|z|=1\}$.

![[math-27-flat-holonomy.svg|**Fig. 27.3** A parallel vector for A = ½ dθ changes by a factor −1 after one circle turn, although the connection has zero curvature. The fibre phases are drawn in separate local copies of the complex line.]]

The total bundle in this example is a product. Its nontrivial holonomy belongs to the chosen connection, not to a failure of the underlying bundle to be trivial. Bundle topology, connection curvature, and global transport are different data.

## Practice

1. With $A=cx\,dy$, compute holonomy around the same rectangle with clockwise orientation. For which real values of $cab$ is it identity?
2. For $A=\tfrac13\alpha$ on $S^1$, calculate holonomy at winding $1$ and $3$. Can winding $3$ be contracted because its holonomy is identity?

## Worked answers

1. Reversing orientation negates the line integral, giving $e^{icab}$, the inverse of the counterclockwise value. Either value is $1$ exactly when $cab\in2\pi\mathbb Z$. Nonzero curvature can therefore produce identity holonomy for some particular loops.
2. The values are $e^{-2\pi i/3}$ and $e^{-2\pi i}=1$. Winding $3$ remains nonzero, so the loop is not contractible on the circle. This connection sends several different loop classes to the same holonomy and therefore does not detect all topological distinctions.
