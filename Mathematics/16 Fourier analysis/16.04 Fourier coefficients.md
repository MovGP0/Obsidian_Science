title: "Fourier coefficients"

> [!TLDR]
> Fourier coefficients are integral projections onto periodic modes; orthogonality isolates one coefficient at a time.

## State the conventions first

Fix $T>0$, set $\omega_0=2\pi/T$, and assume $f$ is square-integrable over one period. The complex coefficient is

$$
c_n=\frac1T\int_a^{a+T}f(x)e^{-in\omega_0x}\,dx,
\qquad n\in\mathbb Z.
$$

The exponential has a negative sign because the projection inner product conjugates its second argument. The corresponding formal expansion uses a positive sign, $\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}c_ne^{in\omega_0x}$. These two signs belong together. The zero coefficient $c_0$ is the average of $f$ over a period.

To derive the formula, first suppose $f$ is a finite sum $\sum_m c_me^{im\omega_0x}$. Multiply by $e^{-in\omega_0x}$, integrate, and divide by $T$. Every term with $m\ne n$ has zero integral, while the $m=n$ term contributes exactly $c_n$. For a general $L^2$ function, the same integral defines its orthogonal projection coefficient without assuming that a pointwise infinite expansion already exists.

The real form is

$$
f(x)\sim\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}
\left[a_n\cos(n\omega_0x)+b_n\sin(n\omega_0x)\right].
$$

The symbol $\sim$ records the assigned Fourier expansion; its convergence is a separate question. Since the nonconstant sine and cosine modes have squared norm $1/2$ under the normalized inner product, their projection formulas are

$$
a_n=\frac2T\int_a^{a+T}f(x)\cos(n\omega_0x)\,dx\quad(n\ge0),
$$

$$
b_n=\frac2T\int_a^{a+T}f(x)\sin(n\omega_0x)\,dx\quad(n\ge1).
$$

For $n=0$, the first formula gives twice the average, as required by the constant term $a_0/2$. There is no $b_0$ term because $\sin0=0$. See [[16.03 Function inner products]] for the normalization argument.

## Worked example: a periodic linear function

Define $f(x)=x$ on $[-\pi,\pi)$ and extend it with period $2\pi$. The function is odd except at irrelevant endpoint choices, so its average and cosine coefficients vanish. For $n\ge1$,

$$
b_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}x\sin(nx)\,dx
=\frac2\pi\int_0^{\pi}x\sin(nx)\,dx.
$$

The product of two odd functions is even, which explains the second equality. Integration by parts gives

$$
\int_0^{\pi}x\sin(nx)\,dx
=\left[-\frac{x\cos(nx)}n\right]_0^{\pi}
+\frac1n\int_0^{\pi}\cos(nx)\,dx
=-\frac{\pi(-1)^n}{n}.
$$

The remaining integral is zero because $\sin(n\pi)=\sin0=0$. Therefore

$$
b_n=\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}n,\qquad
x\sim2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}n\sin(nx).
$$

The complex coefficients are $c_0=0$ and $c_n=i(-1)^n/n$ for every nonzero integer $n$. For positive $n$, this follows from $c_n=-ib_n/2$; the negative indices satisfy the same formula and the required conjugate relation.

This calculation determines coefficients, but it does not justify substituting $x=\pi$ and expecting the value $\pi$. The periodic extension jumps there, and every sine partial sum is zero there. [[16.06 Convergence]] explains the midpoint value.

## Practical checks

Check the constant separately, use a complete interval of length $T$, and retain boundary terms in integration by parts. A symmetry argument can remove an integral only when the interval and function have the stated symmetry. A shifted interval may hide that symmetry even though it gives the same final coefficients.

## Practice

1. Find all real Fourier coefficients of the $2\pi$-periodic extension of $|x|$ from $[-\pi,\pi]$.
2. Find the sine coefficients of $s(x)=1$ on $(0,\pi)$ and $s(x)=-1$ on $(-\pi,0)$, extended periodically.

## Worked answers

1. Evenness gives $b_n=0$. Also $a_0=(2/\pi)\int_0^\pi x\,dx=\pi$. For $n\ge1$, integration by parts gives $\int_0^\pi x\cos(nx)\,dx=\bigl((-1)^n-1\bigr)/n^2$. Hence $a_n=2\bigl((-1)^n-1\bigr)/(\pi n^2)$: it is zero for even $n$ and $-4/(\pi n^2)$ for odd $n$.
2. Oddness gives $a_n=0$, including $a_0$. The sine coefficient is $(2/\pi)\int_0^\pi\sin(nx)\,dx=2\bigl(1-(-1)^n\bigr)/(\pi n)$. Thus $b_n=4/(\pi n)$ for odd $n$ and zero for even $n$.
