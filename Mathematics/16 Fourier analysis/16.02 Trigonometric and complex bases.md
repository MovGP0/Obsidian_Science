title: "Trigonometric and complex bases"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Sine and cosine pairs contain the same information as pairs of positive and negative complex-exponential modes.

## Two forms of the same expansion

Fix a period $T>0$ and define $\omega_0=2\pi/T$. The integer $n$ labels the mode, and $x$ is the real independent variable. A finite trigonometric expansion has the form

$$
p(x)=\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left[a_n\cos(n\omega_0x)+b_n\sin(n\omega_0x)\right].
$$

The factor $1/2$ in the constant term is a convention. It makes later integral formulas for all $a_n$ use the same factor. An equivalent complex expansion is

$$
p(x)=\sum_{n=-N}^{N}c_ne^{in\omega_0x}.
$$

Here $i^2=-1$. Each exponential has period $T$ because increasing $x$ by $T$ multiplies it by $e^{2\pi in}=1$. The zero mode is the constant $e^0=1$. Positive and negative labels are distinct basis functions, even when their combination produces a real function.

For infinite sums, first define the symmetric partial sum with $-N\le n\le N$. The conversion below holds for every finite partial sum. Statements about its limit require the hypotheses discussed in [[16.06 Convergence]]. Algebraic conversion alone does not establish convergence.

## Derive the coefficient conversion

Euler's identities give

$$
\cos\theta=\frac{e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}}{2},\qquad
\sin\theta=\frac{e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}}{2i}.
$$

Insert these identities into one sine–cosine pair and collect equal exponentials:

$$
a_n\cos\theta+b_n\sin\theta
=\frac{a_n-ib_n}{2}e^{i\theta}
+\frac{a_n+ib_n}{2}e^{-i\theta}.
$$

Thus, for each $n\ge1$,

$$
c_n=\frac{a_n-ib_n}{2},\qquad
c_{-n}=\frac{a_n+ib_n}{2},\qquad c_0=\frac{a_0}{2}.
$$

Adding the first two equations gives $a_n=c_n+c_{-n}$. Subtracting them gives $c_n-c_{-n}=-ib_n$, so

$$
b_n=i(c_n-c_{-n}).
$$

These equations also apply to complex-valued functions, for which $a_n$ and $b_n$ need not be real. If the function is real, the usual coefficient condition is $c_{-n}=\overline{c_n}$ and $c_0$ real. Under this condition the pair is

$$
c_ne^{in\omega_0x}+\overline{c_n}e^{-in\omega_0x}
=2\operatorname{Re}\!\left(c_ne^{in\omega_0x}\right).
$$

If $c_n=re^{i\phi}$ with $r\ge0$, the same pair is $2r\cos(n\omega_0x+\phi)$. This explains how the size and argument of a complex coefficient determine a real cosine's amplitude and shift. The sign in the phase follows from the chosen positive exponential convention.

## Worked example

On a $2\pi$ interval, let

$$
p(x)=3+2\cos2x-4\sin x.
$$

The constant is $a_0/2=3$, so $a_0=6$ and $c_0=3$. At $n=1$, $a_1=0$ and $b_1=-4$, giving $c_1=2i$ and $c_{-1}=-2i$. At $n=2$, $a_2=2$ and $b_2=0$, giving $c_2=c_{-2}=1$. Therefore

$$
p(x)=3+2ie^{ix}-2ie^{-ix}+e^{2ix}+e^{-2ix}.
$$

To check the sign, combine the first nonconstant pair: $2i(e^{ix}-e^{-ix})=2i(2i\sin x)=-4\sin x$. All other coefficients vanish. No integration is needed because the function is already expressed in the basis.

## Common errors

Do not identify $c_n$ with $a_n-ib_n$; the missing factor $1/2$ doubles the function. Do not discard the negative modes for a real function. They supply the conjugate terms that make its value real. Finally, the basis depends on the chosen period: $e^{inx}$ assumes $T=2\pi$, while a general period requires $e^{2\pi inx/T}$.

## Practice

1. Convert $2-3\cos x+6\sin x$ to complex coefficients.
2. Suppose $c_2=1-2i$, $c_{-2}=1+2i$, and all other coefficients vanish. Find the real trigonometric form.

## Worked answers

1. Here $c_0=2$, $c_1=(-3-6i)/2=-3/2-3i$, and $c_{-1}=-3/2+3i$. Their sum produces the cosine term, and their difference produces the sine term. The trigonometric constant convention would use $a_0=4$.
2. Add the two coefficients to obtain $a_2=2$. Their difference is $-4i$, so $b_2=i(-4i)=4$. The function is $2\cos2x+4\sin2x$. The conjugate pairing confirms that it is real.
