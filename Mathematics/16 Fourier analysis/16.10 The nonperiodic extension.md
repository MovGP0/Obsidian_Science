title: "The nonperiodic extension"

> [!TLDR]
> The Fourier transform replaces the discrete mode sum of a periodic function with an integral over a continuous real parameter.

## From a circle to the real line

A Fourier series describes a function with a selected period $T$. Its allowed parameters are $k_n=2\pi n/T$, indexed by integers. A function on the whole real line need not repeat. The **Fourier transform** assigns a coefficient to every real number $k$, using the convention

$$
\widehat f(k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)e^{-ikx}\,dx.
$$

Initially assume $f\in L^1(\mathbb R)$, which means $\int_{\mathbb R}|f(x)|\,dx<\infty$. Then the integral exists absolutely for every real $k$, and $|\widehat f(k)|\le\|f\|_1$. The variable $k$ is a continuous wave number in mathematical terminology: it is the coefficient of $x$ in the exponential. It does not carry a physical interpretation here.

There is no fundamental period and no least positive mode spacing on the real line. The functions $e^{ikx}$ are not square-integrable over $\mathbb R$, so they are not an ordinary orthonormal list of $L^2(\mathbb R)$ vectors. The integral representation must not be justified by pretending that their infinite-interval norms equal one.

## The inverse convention

A useful sufficient form of the inversion theorem is this: if both $f$ and $\widehat f$ are integrable, then

$$
f(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat f(k)e^{ikx}\,dk
$$

at every point where the chosen representative $f$ is continuous, and almost everywhere in general. The negative sign belongs in the forward transform and the positive sign in the inverse. Other conventions distribute the $2\pi$ factor differently; formulas cannot be copied between conventions without conversion.

For functions that are square-integrable but not integrable, the transform can be extended through $L^2$ limits. Its norm identity is then

$$
\int_{\mathbb R}|f(x)|^2\,dx
=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}|\widehat f(k)|^2\,dk.
$$

This is the real-line counterpart of [[16.07 Energy of an expansion]]. Defining that extension and proving inversion require separate analysis; the integral formula above is used directly only under its stated hypotheses.

## Why a sum becomes an integral

Take a large interval $[-T/2,T/2]$ and periodically extend the restriction of $f$ to it. Define its truncated transform by

$$
\widehat f_T(k)=\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}f(x)e^{-ikx}\,dx.
$$

Its series coefficients satisfy $c_n=\widehat f_T(k_n)/T$. Since consecutive values of $k_n$ differ by $\Delta k=2\pi/T$, its series has the form

$$
\sum_n c_ne^{ik_nx}
=\frac1{2\pi}\sum_n\widehat f_T(k_n)e^{ik_nx}\,\Delta k.
$$

This expression has the shape of a Riemann sum for the inverse transform. As $T$ grows, the mode spacing decreases. This calculation explains the normalization and the continuous parameter. It is a limiting motivation, not by itself a proof that every interchange of limits, sums, and integrals is valid.

## Worked example: exponential decay

Let $f(x)=e^{-|x|}$. This function is integrable, even, and continuous. Split the transform at zero and substitute $u=-x$ in its negative half:

$$
\widehat f(k)=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-x}e^{-ikx}\,dx
+\int_0^{\infty}e^{-x}e^{ikx}\,dx.
$$

For a complex number $z$ with positive real part, $\int_0^\infty e^{-zx}\,dx=1/z$. Therefore

$$
\widehat f(k)=\frac1{1+ik}+\frac1{1-ik}
=\frac{2}{1+k^2}.
$$

The transform is real and even, as symmetry predicts. It is also integrable, so inversion applies at every point. At $x=0$ it gives

$$
1=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac2{1+k^2}\,dk
=\frac1\pi\left[\arctan k\right]_{-\infty}^{\infty}.
$$

This checks the $2\pi$ factor directly. A periodic nonzero constant would not satisfy the integrability assumption, so this definition cannot be applied to it by the same absolute-integral argument.

## Practice

1. For $a>0$, calculate the transform of the indicator function of $[-a,a]$, including its value at $k=0$.
2. If $g(x)=f(x-b)$ for an integrable function $f$ and real $b$, derive the relation between their transforms.

## Worked answers

1. For $k\ne0$, integrate to obtain $\widehat f(k)=[e^{-ikx}/(-ik)]_{-a}^{a}=2\sin(ak)/k$. At $k=0$ the defining integral is $2a$, also the limit of this formula because $\sin(ak)/(ak)\to1$. The resulting transform is not absolutely integrable, so the sufficient absolute-inversion theorem stated above does not apply without a different convergence argument.
2. Substitute $y=x-b$, so $x=y+b$ and $dx=dy$. Then $\widehat g(k)=\int f(y)e^{-ik(y+b)}\,dy=e^{-ikb}\widehat f(k)$. Translation changes the complex phase but leaves the magnitude unchanged.
