title: "Function inner products"

> [!TLDR]
> An integral inner product measures function size and orthogonality, and its normalization fixes the factors in Fourier coefficients.

## Functions as vectors

Let $T>0$ and let $L^2([a,a+T])$ denote the complex functions whose squared magnitude has a finite integral. Functions that differ only on a set of measure zero represent the same vector. This distinction matters: changing one isolated value changes a pointwise function but does not change its integral norm.

Use the normalized inner product

$$
\langle f,g\rangle=\frac1T\int_a^{a+T}f(x)\overline{g(x)}\,dx.
$$

This convention is linear in the first argument and conjugate-linear in the second. Its associated norm is

$$
\|f\|_2=\sqrt{\langle f,f\rangle}
=\left(\frac1T\int_a^{a+T}|f(x)|^2\,dx\right)^{1/2}.
$$

The bar denotes complex conjugation. It ensures that $\langle f,f\rangle$ is real and nonnegative. For example, if $f(x)=i$, the squared magnitude is $i\overline i=1$, not $i^2=-1$.

Two functions are **orthogonal** if their inner product is zero. A family is **orthonormal** if each member has norm one and distinct members are orthogonal. These are statements about an integral over an interval. Two orthogonal functions can both be nonzero at the same point.

## Exponential orthogonality

Set $\omega_0=2\pi/T$ and $e_n(x)=e^{in\omega_0x}$ for integers $n$. Then

$$
\langle e_n,e_m\rangle
=\frac1T\int_a^{a+T}e^{i(n-m)\omega_0x}\,dx.
$$

If $n=m$, the integrand is one and the result is one. If $n\ne m$, evaluate the antiderivative:

$$
\frac{e^{i(n-m)\omega_0(a+T)}-e^{i(n-m)\omega_0a}}
{i(n-m)\omega_0T}=0.
$$

The numerator vanishes because $e^{2\pi i(n-m)}=1$. Thus the exponentials form an orthonormal family on any full period interval. Using less than one full period generally destroys this identity.

With the same normalized inner product, the real basis has different norms:

$$
\|1\|_2^2=1,\qquad
\|\cos(n\omega_0x)\|_2^2=\|\sin(n\omega_0x)\|_2^2=\frac12
\quad(n\ge1).
$$

The identity $\cos^2u=(1+\cos2u)/2$ gives the cosine result. The integral of the doubled mode is zero. The sine calculation uses $\sin^2u=(1-\cos2u)/2$. Product-to-sum identities show that distinct sine and cosine modes are orthogonal. Multiplication by $\sqrt2$ makes each nonconstant real mode have norm one.

## Projection and the best finite approximation

For an orthonormal finite family $e_{-N},\ldots,e_N$, set

$$
P_Nf=\sum_{n=-N}^{N}\langle f,e_n\rangle e_n.
$$

Taking the inner product with any included $e_m$ gives $\langle f-P_Nf,e_m\rangle=0$. Therefore the residual is orthogonal to every candidate $q$ in the same span. Expanding the norm of $(f-P_Nf)+(P_Nf-q)$ gives

$$
\|f-q\|_2^2=\|f-P_Nf\|_2^2+\|P_Nf-q\|_2^2.
$$

The second term cannot be negative, so the projection is the unique best approximation in this finite span. This is the reason Fourier coefficients use projections; it is not merely a convenient integral trick. See [[16.04 Fourier coefficients]].

## Worked example

Take $T=2\pi$ and $f(x)=1+2\cos x+\sin2x$. Orthogonality removes every cross term from its squared norm:

$$
\|f\|_2^2=1^2+2^2\left(\frac12\right)+1^2\left(\frac12\right)=\frac72.
$$

The best approximation from the span of $1,\cos x,\sin x$ is $q=1+2\cos x$. Its residual is $\sin2x$, with squared norm $1/2$. Any extra constant or first-mode change adds a positive squared norm to that error.

## Practice

1. Use the unnormalized inner product $\int_0^T f\overline g\,dx$. What are the squared norms of $e_n$ and $\cos(n\omega_0x)$?
2. Find the best constant approximation to $f(x)=x$ on $[0,2]$ with the normalized inner product, and calculate its squared error.

## Worked answers

1. Removing $1/T$ multiplies all inner products by $T$. The squared norms become $T$ and $T/2$, respectively. An orthonormal exponential is then $e_n/\sqrt T$. Orthogonality itself does not change.
2. The constant basis vector has norm one, so its coefficient is $\langle x,1\rangle=(1/2)\int_0^2x\,dx=1$. The error is $(1/2)\int_0^2(x-1)^2\,dx=(1/2)(2/3)=1/3$. A different constant adds the square of its distance from one to this error.
