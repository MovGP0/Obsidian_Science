> [!TLDR]
> Fourier sums converge in mean square for square-integrable functions; pointwise values and values at jumps require separate convergence statements.

## Specify what convergence means

For a $2\pi$-periodic function with coefficients $c_n$, define its symmetric partial sum by

$$
S_Nf(x)=\sum_{n=-N}^{N}c_ne^{inx}.
$$

**Pointwise convergence** to $f$ at $x$ means $S_Nf(x)\to f(x)$ as $N\to\infty$. This statement concerns a fixed input. **Uniform convergence** means $\sup_x|S_Nf(x)-f(x)|\to0$, with one error bound valid at every input. Uniform convergence implies pointwise convergence, but the converse fails.

**Mean-square convergence** means

$$
\|f-S_Nf\|_2^2
=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|f(x)-S_Nf(x)|^2\,dx\longrightarrow0.
$$

This statement measures total squared error. It does not directly control a value at a specified point. Indeed, changing $f$ at one point leaves every coefficient and this norm unchanged. For every $f\in L^2$, the trigonometric system is complete, and its Fourier partial sums converge to $f$ in this norm. Completeness is a theorem about density of trigonometric polynomials; it is stated here rather than proved from the finite projection calculation.

## A useful pointwise theorem

Suppose a periodic function is piecewise continuously differentiable with a finite partition and finite one-sided limits at each break. At every input $x$, its Fourier sums satisfy

$$
S_Nf(x)\longrightarrow\frac{f(x^-)+f(x^+)}2.
$$

At a point of continuity the two limits equal $f(x)$, so the sum converges to the function. At a jump it converges to the midpoint of the side limits, regardless of the separately assigned value at the jump. At an endpoint of a period interval, the side limits must be taken in the periodic extension.

One way to understand why the two sides matter is the Dirichlet-kernel formula

$$
S_Nf(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x-y)D_N(y)\,dy,
\qquad
D_N(y)=\frac{\sin((N+1/2)y)}{\sin(y/2)}.
$$

The kernel is the finite sum $\sum_{n=-N}^{N}e^{iny}$, obtained from a geometric progression. It is even and has integral $2\pi$. Near a jump it samples both sides symmetrically. The cancellation estimates needed for the stated pointwise theorem are not supplied by evenness alone; the piecewise regularity hypotheses provide that control.

## Worked example: a jump at the joined endpoint

For $f(x)=x$ on $(-\pi,\pi)$, [[16.04 Fourier coefficients]] gives

$$
S_Nf(x)=2\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}n\sin(nx).
$$

At $x=\pi/2$ the first nonzero terms give

$$
\lim_{N\to\infty}S_Nf(\pi/2)
=2\left(1-\frac13+\frac15-\cdots\right)=\frac\pi2.
$$

The convergence theorem applies because this input lies inside a smooth piece. At $x=\pi$, every sine is zero, so every partial sum is zero. The side limits of the periodic extension are $\pi$ and $-\pi$, whose average is zero. There is no contradiction: the endpoint lies at a jump.

## Overshoot and its limitation

Near a jump, Fourier partial sums oscillate and can exceed both nearby constant values. This is the **Gibbs phenomenon**. The region of overshoot narrows as the number of modes increases, but its relative height does not tend to zero. The limiting value exactly at the jump is still the midpoint.

![[math-16-jump-convergence.svg|**Fig. 16.2** Odd sine partial sums of a periodic step become more accurate away from the jump, while a narrow overshoot remains near zero. All shown sums are zero at the jump.]]

A discontinuous function cannot be the uniform limit of continuous trigonometric polynomials. Therefore a step's mean-square convergence does not imply uniform convergence on a full period. On the other hand, if $\sum_n|c_n|<\infty$, the Fourier series converges uniformly to a continuous representative. This is a useful sufficient condition, not a necessary condition for every type of convergence.

## Practice

1. A periodic step has side limits $2$ and $-4$ at $x_0$, but its assigned value there is $99$. What does the piecewise smooth theorem predict?
2. Let $f=0$ everywhere except $f(0)=1$, extended periodically. Find its coefficients and describe pointwise and mean-square convergence.

## Worked answers

1. The sum tends to $(2-4)/2=-1$. The assigned value $99$ has no effect on any coefficient because an isolated point has integral zero. Thus the series does not converge to that assigned value at $x_0$.
2. Every coefficient is zero, since every coefficient integral is zero. All partial sums are the zero function. They converge pointwise to $f$ away from the exceptional points but not at zero. They converge in mean square because $\|f\|_2=0$; in $L^2$, this $f$ is the zero vector.
