title: "Energy of an expansion"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Parseval's identity expresses a function's squared integral norm as the sum of the squared magnitudes of its Fourier coefficients.

## Energy means a squared norm

In this article, **energy** is a mathematical name for a nonnegative squared norm. It introduces no physical quantity. For a $T$-periodic function $f\in L^2$, use

$$
\|f\|_2^2=\frac1T\int_a^{a+T}|f(x)|^2\,dx,
\qquad
c_n=\frac1T\int_a^{a+T}f(x)e^{-2\pi inx/T}\,dx.
$$

The normalized exponential modes have norm one. Parseval's identity states

$$
\boxed{\|f\|_2^2=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}|c_n|^2.}
$$

The coefficient sum is a sum of nonnegative real numbers. There is no cancellation between modes. The modulus is necessary when coefficients are complex: $|c_n|^2=c_n\overline{c_n}$, not $c_n^2$.

## Why the identity holds

Let $S_Nf=\sum_{|n|\le N}c_ne^{2\pi inx/T}$. Its residual $f-S_Nf$ is orthogonal to every included mode. Expanding the squared norm and using this orthogonality gives the exact finite identity

$$
\|f\|_2^2=\|f-S_Nf\|_2^2+\|S_Nf\|_2^2
=\|f-S_Nf\|_2^2+\sum_{|n|\le N}|c_n|^2.
$$

Dropping the first nonnegative term gives **Bessel's inequality**. This inequality holds for any orthonormal family, even one that misses part of the function space. To obtain equality in the infinite limit, use completeness of the Fourier family: $\|f-S_Nf\|_2\to0$. The residual term then vanishes, leaving Parseval's identity. Thus completeness is essential to the last step.

The same reasoning also gives an exact truncation-error formula:

$$
\|f-S_Nf\|_2^2=\sum_{|n|>N}|c_n|^2.
$$

This formula measures accuracy in mean square. It does not give a pointwise error estimate near a jump. See [[16.06 Convergence]] for that distinction.

## What zero coefficients imply

If every Fourier coefficient is zero, Parseval gives $\|f\|_2^2=0$. Thus $f=0$ almost everywhere. This proves uniqueness of a Fourier representation in $L^2$: if two functions have the same coefficients, their difference has zero coefficients and therefore zero norm. It does not prove equality at every individual point. If both functions have continuous representatives, however, their difference is continuous; a nonzero value would force a nonzero integral on a small interval. In that continuous case, equality almost everywhere implies equality everywhere.

## Translate to real coefficients

For a real function written as $a_0/2+\sum_{n\ge1}(a_n\cos(n\omega_0x)+b_n\sin(n\omega_0x))$, opposite complex coefficients contribute

$$
|c_n|^2+|c_{-n}|^2
=\frac{a_n^2+b_n^2}{2}.
$$

The constant contributes $a_0^2/4$. Therefore

$$
\frac1T\int_a^{a+T}f(x)^2\,dx
=\frac{a_0^2}{4}+\frac12\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(a_n^2+b_n^2).
$$

If the integral is unnormalized, multiply the whole right side by $T$. In particular, for $T=2\pi$, the alternative expression $(1/\pi)\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f^2=a_0^2/2+\sum(a_n^2+b_n^2)$ uses a different factor on both sides.

## Worked example: evaluate a numerical series

Take $f(x)=x$ on $[-\pi,\pi)$ with periodic extension. Its coefficients are $a_0=a_n=0$ and $b_n=2(-1)^{n+1}/n$. Direct integration gives

$$
\|f\|_2^2=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}x^2\,dx
=\frac1\pi\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^\pi=\frac{\pi^2}{3}.
$$

The coefficient expression is

$$
\frac12\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac4{n^2}
=2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^2}.
$$

Equating the two sides gives $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/n^2=\pi^2/6$. The endpoint jump does not obstruct this argument: the function belongs to $L^2$, and Parseval concerns an integral norm. The squared error after $N$ sine modes is $2\sum_{n>N}1/n^2$. Since the integral comparison bounds the tail by $1/N$, this error is at most $2/N$.

## Practice

1. Calculate the squared normalized norm of $f(x)=2+3\cos x-4\sin2x$ on a $2\pi$ interval.
2. The periodic step $s(x)=\operatorname{sgn}(x)$ on $(-\pi,\pi)$ has $b_n=4/(\pi n)$ for odd $n$, with all other coefficients zero. Use Parseval to evaluate the sum of reciprocal odd squares.

## Worked answers

1. The constant is $a_0/2=2$, so its contribution is $4$. The two nonconstant contributions are $9/2$ and $16/2$. Their sum is $4+9/2+8=33/2$. Distinct modes have no cross terms.
2. Since $s^2=1$ almost everywhere, its normalized squared norm is one. Parseval gives $1=(1/2)(16/\pi^2)\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}1/(2m+1)^2$. Multiplication by $\pi^2/8$ gives $\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}1/(2m+1)^2=\pi^2/8$.
