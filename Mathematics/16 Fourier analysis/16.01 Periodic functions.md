> [!TLDR]
> A periodic function repeats after a fixed shift; a change of variable can express one complete period on a standard interval.

## What a period means

Let $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb C$ be a function. A number $T>0$ is a **period** of $f$ if

$$
f(x+T)=f(x)\qquad\text{for every }x\in\mathbb R.
$$

The function is then $T$-periodic. The condition applies to every input, not only to a set of sample points. Repeated substitution gives $f(x+mT)=f(x)$ for every integer $m$, including negative integers. Thus one complete interval of length $T$ determines the whole function.

If there is a least positive period, it is called the **fundamental period**. Every positive integer multiple of that number is also a period. A constant function has every positive number as a period, so it has no fundamental period. Do not infer a least period merely from the word “periodic.” In Fourier analysis, it is sufficient to choose a valid period, whether or not it is the least one.

The basic functions satisfy

$$
\sin(x+2\pi)=\sin x,\qquad e^{i(x+2\pi)}=e^{ix}.
$$

Their fundamental period is $2\pi$. A sum of periodic functions has a common period if all its terms have that period. This statement does not imply that every sum of periodic functions is periodic: the individual periods can be incompatible.

## One complete interval

Common choices are $[0,T)$ and $[-T/2,T/2)$. The half-open notation includes one copy of each position in the repeating pattern. Closed intervals are also useful for integration, since adding or removing a finite number of endpoints does not change an integral.

When an interval formula defines a periodic extension, its endpoints must be treated carefully. The formula $f(x)=x$ on $[-\pi,\pi)$ defines a valid $2\pi$-periodic function, but its extension has a jump at $\pi$. Assigning both $f(-\pi)=-\pi$ and $f(\pi)=\pi$ would contradict exact periodicity at those points.

If $f$ is integrable over one period, then

$$
\int_a^{a+T}f(x)\,dx=\int_0^T f(x)\,dx.
$$

For $0\le a<T$, split the first integral at $T$. In the part from $T$ to $T+a$, substitute $u=x-T$ and use periodicity. The two remaining pieces cover $[0,T]$. General $a$ reduces to this case by an integer shift.

## Rescale to a standard period

Set $\theta=2\pi x/T$ and define

$$
F(\theta)=f\!\left(\frac{T\theta}{2\pi}\right).
$$

Then $F(\theta+2\pi)=F(\theta)$. In the opposite direction, $f(x)=F(2\pi x/T)$. This substitution explains the modes $\cos(2\pi nx/T)$ and $\sin(2\pi nx/T)$ used in [[16.02 Trigonometric and complex bases]]. Integration also rescales: $dx=T\,d\theta/(2\pi)$. Omitting this factor changes every coefficient.

More generally, if $g(x)=f(ax+b)$ with $a\ne0$, then $T/|a|$ is a period of $g$. A change of $b$ shifts the pattern without changing its period. A negative $a$ also reverses the input direction. If $T$ is fundamental for $f$, the transformed period is fundamental for $g$ because any smaller period would produce a smaller positive period of $f$.

## Worked example

Consider $g(x)=\sin(3x-\pi/4)$. A complete cycle requires its argument to increase by $2\pi$. Therefore $3T=2\pi$, which gives $T=2\pi/3$. The shift places the first zero with positive slope at $x=\pi/12$ because $3x-\pi/4=0$ there. One complete period is consequently

$$
\left[\frac{\pi}{12},\frac{\pi}{12}+\frac{2\pi}{3}\right)
=\left[\frac{\pi}{12},\frac{3\pi}{4}\right).
$$

The interval $[0,2\pi/3)$ is equally valid. A period interval does not have to begin at a zero or at a maximum.

## Practice

1. Find the fundamental period of $h(x)=\sin^2x$ and explain why $2\pi$ is valid but not least.
2. Let $f(x)=\cos(\pi x/3)$. Find its fundamental period and write its standard $2\pi$-periodic form $F(\theta)$.

## Worked answers

1. The identity $\sin^2x=(1-\cos2x)/2$ gives period $\pi$. A period $P$ must map the zeros, which occur at integer multiples of $\pi$, to zeros. Since $h(0)=0$, a positive period satisfies $P=m\pi$ for a positive integer $m$. The least choice is $\pi$; $2\pi$ repeats the cycle twice.
2. Increasing $x$ by $6$ increases $\pi x/3$ by $2\pi$, so $T=6$. The least positive shift of a cosine argument is $2\pi$, so this period is fundamental. Substitute $x=3\theta/\pi$ to obtain $F(\theta)=\cos\theta$.
