> [!TLDR]
> Periodic boundary conditions select integer Fourier modes, and projections of the initial data set the coefficients of the solution.

## Why the labels are integers

Let functions be defined on a circle represented by $0\le x\le T$, with the endpoints identified. A complex exponential $X(x)=e^{ikx}$ satisfies $X(x+T)=X(x)$ only if

$$
e^{ikT}=1,\qquad kT=2\pi n,\qquad
k=k_n=\frac{2\pi n}{T},\quad n\in\mathbb Z.
$$

Thus the integer index comes from periodicity, not from an arbitrary restriction on a continuous parameter. Negative indices are needed for general complex functions and for conjugate pairs representing real functions.

The same modes are eigenfunctions of the differential operator $-d^2/dx^2$:

$$
-X_n''=k_n^2X_n,\qquad X_n(x)=e^{ik_nx}.
$$

For this second-order operator, impose both $X(T)=X(0)$ and $X'(T)=X'(0)$. The derivative condition makes integration-by-parts boundary terms cancel. If $-X''=\lambda X$ for a nonzero periodic eigenfunction, then

$$
\lambda\int_0^T|X|^2\,dx
=\int_0^T|X'|^2\,dx\ge0.
$$

This identity shows that $\lambda$ is real and nonnegative. For $\lambda=0$, $X$ is constant. For $\lambda>0$, solving the constant-coefficient equation and imposing both endpoint conditions gives precisely the modes above, with $\lambda=k_n^2$.

## An evolution boundary-value problem

Consider the mathematical problem

$$
u_t=u_{xx},\qquad t\ge0,\qquad
u(0,t)=u(T,t),\qquad u_x(0,t)=u_x(T,t),
$$

with initial data $u(x,0)=g(x)$. Here $t$ is a nonnegative parameter and subscripts denote partial derivatives. Assume first that $g$ is a trigonometric polynomial. Write

$$
u(x,t)=\sum_n A_n(t)e^{ik_nx}.
$$

Substitution gives $A_n'(t)=-k_n^2A_n(t)$ for each mode, because the exponentials are linearly independent. Solving this scalar equation yields $A_n(t)=A_n(0)e^{-k_n^2t}$. The initial condition determines

$$
A_n(0)=\widehat g_n=\frac1T\int_0^Tg(x)e^{-ik_nx}\,dx.
$$

Therefore

$$
u(x,t)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}\widehat g_n e^{-k_n^2t}e^{ik_nx}.
$$

For $g\in L^2$, this formula still defines a solution for $t>0$ and tends to $g$ in mean square as $t\downarrow0$. The exponential factors suppress large indices, which allows termwise differentiation for positive $t$. This does not require a discontinuous initial function to be attained pointwise at every endpoint. The type of initial convergence must be stated.

## Worked example

Choose $T=2\pi$ and $g(x)=2+3\cos2x-\sin x$. Its only nonzero mode families have indices zero, one, and two. Since $k_n=n$, the formula gives

$$
u(x,t)=2+3e^{-4t}\cos2x-e^{-t}\sin x.
$$

Check the equation directly:

$$
u_t=-12e^{-4t}\cos2x+e^{-t}\sin x
=u_{xx}.
$$

At $t=0$, the original coefficients return. Every term and its $x$ derivative repeat with period $2\pi$. As $t\to\infty$, the nonconstant terms tend to zero and $u$ tends to its initial mean, two. The zero eigenvalue explains why the mean stays fixed.

## Boundary conditions change the basis

On an interval with zero endpoint values, sine functions replace the full periodic basis. With zero endpoint derivatives, cosine functions are appropriate. One must derive the allowed modes from the actual boundary conditions before projecting data. Importing periodic modes into a different boundary problem can violate the endpoints even if the differential equation holds inside the interval.

## Practice

1. For the periodic problem above with $T=4$ and $g(x)=\sin(\pi x)$, find $u(x,t)$ and identify its integer mode label.
2. For a $2\pi$-periodic twice differentiable function, when can $-v''=g$ have a periodic solution? Solve it for $g(x)=\cos2x$.

## Worked answers

1. Here $k_n=\pi n/2$, so $\pi$ corresponds to $n=2$. Thus $u(x,t)=e^{-\pi^2t}\sin(\pi x)$. Its second $x$ derivative and first $t$ derivative both equal $-\pi^2u$. The chosen interval contains two complete cycles.
2. Integrating over a period gives $0=-v'(2\pi)+v'(0)=\int_0^{2\pi}g$, so the mean must vanish. For the given single cosine this holds, and $v(x)=\tfrac14\cos2x+C$ works because $-v''=\cos2x$. The constant is free: the differential operator annihilates the zero mode.
