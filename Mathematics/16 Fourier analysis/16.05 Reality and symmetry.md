---
title: "Reality and symmetry"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Real functions have conjugate complex coefficients, while even and odd symmetry removes sine or cosine terms.

## Reality pairs opposite indices

Use period $T=2L>0$, set $\omega_0=\pi/L$, and define

$$
c_n=\frac1{2L}\int_{-L}^{L}f(x)e^{-in\omega_0x}\,dx.
$$

If $f$ is real almost everywhere, conjugation can pass through the integral:

$$
\overline{c_n}
=\frac1{2L}\int_{-L}^{L}f(x)e^{in\omega_0x}\,dx
=c_{-n}.
$$

In particular, $c_0$ is real. A real function can still have nonreal coefficients; reality constrains pairs rather than each coefficient separately. Conversely, a finite expansion with $c_{-n}=\overline{c_n}$ and real $c_0$ is real because each opposite pair equals twice a real part. The same conclusion holds for an infinite expansion in its $L^2$ limit.

## Even and odd functions

A function is **even** if $f(-x)=f(x)$ and **odd** if $f(-x)=-f(x)$. On a symmetric interval, the integral of an odd integrable function is zero. This fact follows by substituting $u=-x$ in the negative half of the interval; its contribution cancels the positive half.

Cosine is even and sine is odd. Therefore an even function has

$$
b_n=0,\qquad
a_n=\frac2L\int_0^Lf(x)\cos(n\pi x/L)\,dx\quad(n\ge0).
$$

An odd function has

$$
a_n=0\quad(n\ge0),\qquad
b_n=\frac2L\int_0^Lf(x)\sin(n\pi x/L)\,dx.
$$

The factor $2/L$ comes from the general factor $1/L$ and the doubled half-interval integral. For a real even function, $c_n=c_{-n}$ and these coefficients are real. For a real odd function, $c_{-n}=-c_n$, so its nonzero coefficients are purely imaginary. The zero coefficient of an odd function is zero.

Every function on a symmetric domain has the decomposition

$$
f(x)=f_{\mathrm e}(x)+f_{\mathrm o}(x),\qquad
f_{\mathrm e}(x)=\frac{f(x)+f(-x)}2,\qquad
f_{\mathrm o}(x)=\frac{f(x)-f(-x)}2.
$$

The first part is even and the second is odd. Thus cosine coefficients detect the even part and sine coefficients detect the odd part. This is useful even when the original function has neither symmetry by itself.

## Worked example: a periodic quadratic

Let $f(x)=x^2$ on $[-\pi,\pi]$, extended periodically. It is even, so $b_n=0$. Its constant coefficient is

$$
a_0=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi x^2\,dx=\frac{2\pi^2}{3}.
$$

For $n\ge1$, integrate twice by parts. The first boundary term vanishes because it contains $\sin(n\pi)$:

$$
\int_0^\pi x^2\cos(nx)\,dx
=-\frac2n\int_0^\pi x\sin(nx)\,dx
=\frac{2\pi(-1)^n}{n^2}.
$$

Here $\int_0^\pi x\sin(nx)\,dx=-\pi(-1)^n/n$. Multiplying by $2/\pi$ gives $a_n=4(-1)^n/n^2$. The expansion is

$$
x^2=\frac{\pi^2}{3}+4\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{n^2}\cos(nx).
$$

The coefficient bound $4/n^2$ makes the series uniformly convergent. The periodic function is continuous, including its joined endpoints, so the usual piecewise smooth convergence theorem identifies this sum with $f$ everywhere. Its complex coefficients are $c_0=\pi^2/3$ and $c_n=2(-1)^n/n^2$ for $n\ne0$.

## The origin matters

Evenness and oddness refer to the selected origin. Translating an even function can introduce sine terms. If $g(x)=f(x-x_0)$, substitution in the coefficient integral gives $c_n(g)=e^{-in\omega_0x_0}c_n(f)$. The phase generally makes an originally real coefficient complex, while the conjugate relation still holds for real $g$.

## Practice

1. Split $f(x)=x+x^2$ into even and odd parts on $[-\pi,\pi]$. Which part determines each coefficient family?
2. Let $f(x)=\cos x$ and $g(x)=f(x-\pi/2)$. Find $g$, its nonzero complex coefficients, and its symmetry.

## Worked answers

1. Since $f(-x)=-x+x^2$, the half-sum is $x^2$ and the half-difference is $x$. Hence the cosine coefficients and constant come from $x^2$, while all sine coefficients come from $x$. No new integral is needed once the two parts have been calculated separately.
2. The subtraction formula gives $g(x)=\sin x$. Originally $c_1=c_{-1}=1/2$. Translation multiplies these by $e^{-i\pi/2}=-i$ and $e^{i\pi/2}=i$, respectively. Thus $c_1=-i/2$ and $c_{-1}=i/2$. The shifted function is odd and real, as these coefficients predict.
