---
title: "Operations on expansions"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Differentiation multiplies a Fourier mode by its index, while integration divides nonzero modes; valid operations require convergence or regularity conditions.

## Begin with finite sums

Let $T>0$, $\omega_0=2\pi/T$, and $p(x)=\sum_{|n|\le N}c_ne^{in\omega_0x}$. A finite sum can be differentiated term by term:

$$
p'(x)=\sum_{|n|\le N}in\omega_0c_ne^{in\omega_0x}.
$$

The zero mode disappears. A second derivative multiplies each coefficient by $-(n\omega_0)^2$. These operations explain why Fourier modes simplify differential equations. For an infinite series, the same formal calculation requires justification; convergence of the original sum alone is insufficient.

## A sufficient differentiation condition

Suppose the coefficients satisfy

$$
\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}|n c_n|<\infty.
$$

Then both $\sum c_ne^{in\omega_0x}$ and its formal derivative converge uniformly. The derivative series is bounded term by term by $\omega_0|n c_n|$, whose sum is finite. For nonzero indices, $|c_n|\le|n c_n|$, so the original series also converges absolutely and uniformly. The theorem on uniform convergence of derivatives then gives a continuously differentiable sum with the stated derivative.

This is a sufficient condition, not a necessary one. Smoothness conditions offer another route. For example, if $f$ is $C^3$ and periodic with its first two derivatives matching across the endpoints, three integrations by parts give $c_n=O(|n|^{-3})$. Consequently $\sum|n c_n|$ converges. The endpoint matching makes all boundary terms vanish; it cannot be omitted.

For a continuously differentiable periodic function, integration by parts also identifies the derivative's coefficients directly:

$$
c_n(f')=\frac1T\left[f(x)e^{-in\omega_0x}\right]_a^{a+T}
+in\omega_0c_n(f)=in\omega_0c_n(f).
$$

This coefficient identity does not by itself prove pointwise convergence of the derivative's Fourier series. It states which coefficients that derivative has.

## Integration is less restrictive

If $f\in L^2$ and $S_Nf\to f$ in mean square, then their integrals from a fixed base point converge uniformly on a period interval. Indeed, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives, for $a\le x\le a+T$,

$$
\left|\int_a^x(f-S_Nf)(s)\,ds\right|
\le\sqrt{x-a}\left(\int_a^{a+T}|f-S_Nf|^2\,ds\right)^{1/2}
\le T\|f-S_Nf\|_2.
$$

The last bound does not depend on $x$ and tends to zero. For a nonzero index, an antiderivative mode has coefficient $c_n/(in\omega_0)$. The zero mode instead gives $c_0(x-a)$.

A periodic integrable function has a periodic primitive exactly when its mean is zero. Necessity follows from $F(a+T)-F(a)=\int_a^{a+T}f=T c_0$. If $c_0=0$, the integral primitive repeats after each period, which proves sufficiency. An arbitrary integration constant determines the primitive's zero mode.

## Worked example

Define the $2\pi$-periodic function

$$
F(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(nx)}{n^3}.
$$

The derivative series is $-\sum_{n\ge1}\sin(nx)/n^2$, which converges uniformly because $|\sin(nx)/n^2|\le1/n^2$. Thus

$$
F'(x)=-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(nx)}{n^2}.
$$

At $x=0$, every term vanishes, so $F'(0)=0$. Integrating from zero is also valid and gives

$$
\int_0^xF(s)\,ds=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(nx)}{n^4}.
$$

All cosine terms have zero mean, so this primitive is periodic. Differentiating a second time produces a formal series $-\sum\cos(nx)/n$, for which the same absolute-convergence test fails. That failure is a reason to seek a new justification, not a proof that the second derivative never exists.

## Practice

1. Differentiate $f(x)=2+3\cos2x-\sin3x$ and find the squared normalized norm of its derivative.
2. Can $f(x)=1+\cos x$ have a $2\pi$-periodic primitive? Find a primitive and explain its change over a period.

## Worked answers

1. The derivative is $-6\sin2x-3\cos3x$. Orthogonality gives squared norm $36/2+9/2=45/2$. The constant contributes nothing because its derivative is zero.
2. Its mean is one, so no periodic primitive exists. Every primitive is $F(x)=x+\sin x+C$. Its change is $F(x+2\pi)-F(x)=2\pi$, equal to the integral of $f$ over one period. The nonzero mean causes the linear term.
