> [!TLDR]
> A solution must satisfy an equation on an interval and satisfy its stated data; local existence and uniqueness require separate hypotheses.

## Functions, intervals, and families

A classical solution of an $n$th-order ODE is a function with the required $n$ continuous derivatives on an interval $I$ that satisfies the equation at every point of $I$. A formula without an interval is incomplete when it contains a denominator or a logarithm. For example,

$$
y(t)=\frac{1}{1-t}
$$

solves $y'=y^2$ on $(-\infty,1)$ and on $(1,\infty)$ separately. It is not a solution on an interval that contains $1$, where it is undefined.

A **general solution** usually means a family with enough independent constants to represent the local solutions under discussion. A **particular solution** is one selected member. These phrases do not prove that the family includes every exceptional solution. Separation can discard a constant solution when it divides by an expression that can vanish; [[14.03 First-order methods]] shows how to retain these cases.

For a regular linear equation of order $n$, $n$ independent constants describe its homogeneous solution space. An inhomogeneous solution family is a fixed particular solution plus that space. This statement needs a nonzero leading coefficient and continuous normalized coefficients on the chosen interval.

## Initial conditions and boundary conditions

An initial-value problem specifies all derivative data at one point:

$$
y(t_0)=b_0,\quad y'(t_0)=b_1,\quad\ldots,\quad
y^{(n-1)}(t_0)=b_{n-1}.
$$

A boundary-value problem gives conditions at different points, such as $y(0)=0$ and $y(1)=0$. Two conditions for a second-order equation do not by themselves guarantee a unique answer. Their placement and their relation to the equation matter.

For example, $y''+y=0$ has family $A\cos t+B\sin t$. The condition $y(0)=0$ gives $A=0$. At $t=\pi$, every remaining member has $y(\pi)=0$, so the two zero boundary conditions leave infinitely many solutions. Replacing the second condition by $y(\pi)=1$ gives no solution. In contrast, prescribing $y(0)$ and $y'(0)$ determines $A$ and $B$ uniquely.

## What local existence and uniqueness mean

For $y'=f(t,y)$ and $y(t_0)=y_0$, suppose $f$ is continuous on a rectangle around $(t_0,y_0)$. Continuity guarantees at least one local solution. A standard sufficient condition for uniqueness is that $f$ is also locally Lipschitz in $y$: on a smaller rectangle there is a constant $L$ such that

$$
|f(t,u)-f(t,v)|\le L|u-v|.
$$

A continuous partial derivative $\partial f/\partial y$ on a surrounding rectangle is a convenient stronger sufficient condition. These are sufficient tests, not necessary conditions for every unique problem. The same idea holds for vector systems with a norm in place of absolute value.

The integral identity

$$
y(t)=y_0+\int_{t_0}^{t}f(s,y(s))\,ds
$$

explains the theorem's structure. Continuity allows integral approximations; a Lipschitz bound controls how much two candidates can separate. Local means on some interval around $t_0$. It does not mean for all real $t$. The problem $y'=y^2$, $y(0)=1$ satisfies the local hypotheses, but its solution becomes unbounded as $t$ approaches $1$ from below.

Without the uniqueness condition, branching can occur. For $y'=2\sqrt{|y|}$, $y(0)=0$, both $y=0$ and, for each $a\ge0$,

$$
y_a(t)=\begin{cases}0,&t\le a,\\
(t-a)^2,&t>a\end{cases}
$$

solve the problem. The derivative is zero from both sides at $a$, so the joined function is differentiable. The square-root right side is continuous but is not locally Lipschitz at zero.

## Worked example: solve a boundary problem

Solve $y''=-2$, with $y(0)=y(1)=0$. Integrating once gives $y'=-2t+C_1$. Integrating again gives $y=-t^2+C_1t+C_2$. At zero, $C_2=0$. At one, $0=-1+C_1$, so $C_1=1$. Hence

$$
y(t)=t(1-t),\qquad 0\le t\le1.
$$

Its second derivative is $-2$, and both endpoint values are zero. To check uniqueness directly, subtract two possible solutions. Their difference has second derivative zero, so it is $at+b$. Its two zero endpoint values force $a=b=0$.

## Practice

1. Solve $y'=2y$, $y(0)=3$, and state its interval of existence.
2. Solve $y'=y^2$, $y(0)=2$. Find the maximal interval containing zero.

## Worked answers

1. The family is $y=Ce^{2t}$. The initial value gives $C=3$. Substitution gives $y'=6e^{2t}=2y$. The formula is defined for every real $t$, so its maximal interval is $\mathbb R$.
2. For a nonzero solution, $-1/y=t+C$. At zero, $C=-1/2$. Thus $y=2/(1-2t)$. Its pole is at $t=1/2$, so the maximal connected interval containing zero is $(-\infty,1/2)$. The branch beyond the pole cannot extend this initial-value solution through the singularity.
