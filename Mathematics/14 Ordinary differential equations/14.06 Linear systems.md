> [!TLDR]
> A matrix exponential solves a constant linear system, while eigenvectors separate the solution into modes when a full eigenvector basis exists.

## Matrix form and its solution operator

Let $\mathbf{x}(t)\in\mathbb R^n$ be a column vector and let $A$ be a constant real $n\times n$ matrix. A homogeneous linear system is

$$
\mathbf{x}'=A\mathbf{x},\qquad \mathbf{x}(0)=\mathbf{x}_0.
$$

Each row gives one scalar differential equation. Define the matrix exponential by its convergent power series

$$
e^{tA}=I+tA+\frac{t^2A^2}{2!}+\cdots.
$$

Here $I$ is the identity matrix. Differentiation term by term yields $(e^{tA})'=Ae^{tA}$, while $e^{0A}=I$. Thus $\mathbf{x}(t)=e^{tA}\mathbf{x}_0$ satisfies both the system and its data. The series converges for all real $t$, so this solution is global. For data at $t_0$, replace $t$ in the exponential by $t-t_0$.

The matrix exponential is not the entrywise exponential of $A$. Matrix powers combine rows and columns by matrix multiplication. This distinction matters even for a matrix with many zero entries.

## Eigenvectors and independent modes

An eigenvector $\mathbf{v}\ne0$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$ satisfies $A\mathbf{v}=\lambda\mathbf{v}$. The function $e^{\lambda t}\mathbf{v}$ is then a mode, since

$$
\frac{d}{dt}(e^{\lambda t}\mathbf{v})
=\lambda e^{\lambda t}\mathbf{v}
=A(e^{\lambda t}\mathbf{v}).
$$

If $A$ has $n$ independent eigenvectors, collect them as columns of an invertible matrix $P$. Then $A=P\Lambda P^{-1}$, where $\Lambda$ is diagonal. Put $\mathbf{x}=P\mathbf{z}$ and multiply the system by $P^{-1}$:

$$
\mathbf{z}'=\Lambda\mathbf{z},\qquad
z_j'=\lambda_j z_j,\qquad z_j=c_j e^{\lambda_j t}.
$$

The coordinates $z_j$ evolve independently. Returning to the original coordinates gives $\mathbf{x}=\sum_j c_je^{\lambda_jt}\mathbf{v}_j$ and $e^{tA}=P e^{t\Lambda}P^{-1}$. For complex eigenvalues of a real matrix, conjugate modes combine into real sine and cosine vector functions, just as in [[14.04 Constant-coefficient equations]].

## Worked example: separate sum and difference

Consider

$$
\begin{pmatrix}x'\\y'\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}-2&1\\1&-2\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\begin{pmatrix}x(0)\\y(0)\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}2\\0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The characteristic determinant is $(-2-\lambda)^2-1$, with roots $-1$ and $-3$. Corresponding eigenvectors are $(1,1)^T$ and $(1,-1)^T$. Decompose the initial vector:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}2\\0\end{pmatrix}
=c_1\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}
+c_2\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The equations $c_1+c_2=2$ and $c_1-c_2=0$ give $c_1=c_2=1$. Therefore

$$
x=e^{-t}+e^{-3t},\qquad y=e^{-t}-e^{-3t}.
$$

Alternatively define $u=x+y$ and $v=x-y$. Adding and subtracting the original equations gives $u'=-u$ and $v'=-3v$, with $u(0)=v(0)=2$. Thus $u=2e^{-t}$, $v=2e^{-3t}$, and $x=(u+v)/2$, $y=(u-v)/2$. Both derivations show why the difference mode decays faster than the sum mode.

## Incomplete eigenvector bases and a right-side term

An eigenvalue list alone does not always produce a basis. For

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}=I+N,\qquad N^2=0,
$$

the matrix has only one independent eigenvector. Because $I$ and $N$ commute, the series gives $e^{tA}=e^t(I+tN)$. Therefore $y=c_2e^t$ and $x=e^t(c_1+c_2t)$. The polynomial factor supplies the missing independent behavior. The exponential method still works even though diagonalization fails.

For an inhomogeneous system $\mathbf{x}'=A\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}(t)$ with continuous $\mathbf{b}$, variation of constants gives

$$
\mathbf{x}(t)=e^{tA}\mathbf{x}_0+
\int_0^t e^{(t-s)A}\mathbf{b}(s)\,ds.
$$

This formula follows by writing $\mathbf{x}=e^{tA}\mathbf{c}(t)$, canceling $Ae^{tA}\mathbf{c}$, and integrating $\mathbf{c}'=e^{-tA}\mathbf{b}$. For a variable matrix $A(t)$, the expression $e^{\int_{t_0}^t A(s)\,ds}$ requires extra conditions. Pairwise commutation of $A(s)$ and $A(t)$ is a sufficient condition for this expression to give the solution operator.

## Practice

1. Solve $x'=2x$, $y'=-y$ with $(x(0),y(0))=(1,3)$.
2. Compute $e^{tN}$ and the solution for $N=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ with initial vector $(a,b)^T$.

## Worked answers

1. The matrix is diagonal, so the coordinates are already modes. The data give $x=e^{2t}$ and $y=3e^{-t}$. The first grows while the second decays; behavior along one eigendirection does not describe every solution.
2. Since $N^2=0$, every series term of order at least two vanishes. Thus $e^{tN}=I+tN=\begin{pmatrix}1&t\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. Multiplication by the initial vector gives $x=a+bt$, $y=b$. Directly, $x'=b=y$ and $y'=0$, as required.
