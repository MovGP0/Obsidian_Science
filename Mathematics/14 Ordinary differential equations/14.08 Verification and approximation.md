> [!TLDR]
> Verify an exact formula by substitution and data checks; assess a numerical approximation through its method, step size, and error rather than through visual agreement alone.

## Exact verification has three parts

An exact solution satisfies the equation as an identity throughout its stated interval. To verify a proposed solution, first check its domain and differentiability, then substitute its derivatives into the equation, and finally check every initial or boundary condition. A correct differential identity can still select the wrong member of a family.

For an equation $L[y]=g$, define the **residual** of a candidate $v$ as $R=L[v]-g$. A classical exact solution has $R(t)=0$ everywhere in its interval. A nonzero residual proves that the candidate is not exact. A small residual can be useful evidence for an approximation, but its relation to solution error depends on stability and on the data.

For example, every function $v=Ce^t$ has zero residual for $y'-y=0$. Only $C=1$ satisfies $y(0)=1$. Even zero residual is insufficient if the initial value is wrong. Numerical checks at finitely many points cannot prove an identity between arbitrary functions.

## Worked example: check an inhomogeneous solution

Consider $y'+2y=t$ with $y(0)=1$, and propose

$$
v(t)=\frac t2-\frac14+\frac54e^{-2t}.
$$

The function is differentiable for every real $t$, and $v'=1/2-(5/2)e^{-2t}$. Substitution gives

$$
v'+2v=\left(\frac12-\frac52e^{-2t}\right)
+\left(t-\frac12+\frac52e^{-2t}\right)=t.
$$

Thus the residual vanishes. The data check is $v(0)=-1/4+5/4=1$. Both requirements hold, so the formula is an exact solution. Since the normalized linear coefficients are continuous, uniqueness also shows it is the solution of this initial-value problem on $\mathbb R$. If the exponential coefficient were $1$ instead, the equation check would still pass, but the data check would fail.

## Euler's method and its assumptions

For $y'=f(t,y)$ with $y(t_0)=y_0$, choose a positive step $h$ and grid points $t_n=t_0+nh$. Euler's method defines numbers $Y_n$ by

$$
Y_0=y_0,\qquad Y_{n+1}=Y_n+h f(t_n,Y_n).
$$

The capital $Y_n$ denotes an approximation to $y(t_n)$, not the exact value. The update uses the tangent slope at the start of each step. If the exact solution has a bounded second derivative, Taylor's theorem gives

$$
y(t_{n+1})=y(t_n)+h f(t_n,y(t_n))+O(h^2).
$$

Thus the one-step defect, when a step starts at the exact value, is $O(h^2)$. Over a fixed finite interval there are order $1/h$ steps. Under a Lipschitz condition in $y$ and suitable bounded derivatives, accumulated error is $O(h)$. Some texts divide the one-step defect by $h$ when defining local truncation error; check that convention before comparing stated orders.

## Worked example: exact and numerical values

For $y'=y$, $y(0)=1$, the exact solution is $e^t$. Euler gives $Y_{n+1}=(1+h)Y_n$, hence $Y_n=(1+h)^n$. With $h=1/2$, two steps reach $t=1$:

$$
Y_1=1+\tfrac12=1.5,\qquad
Y_2=1.5+\tfrac12(1.5)=2.25.
$$

The exact endpoint is $e\approx2.7182818$, so the endpoint error $Y_2-e$ is about $-0.4682818$. With $h=1/4$, four steps give $Y_4=(5/4)^4=625/256=2.44140625$, reducing the error to about $-0.2768756$. More steps improve this result, but neither value is exact. The limit $(1+1/n)^n\to e$ explains convergence at $t=1$.

The numerical sequence alone does not define derivatives between grid points. If you join the points with straight segments, the result has slope changes at grid points and usually does not satisfy the ODE there. It is an interpolated approximation, not a new exact classical solution.

## Stability and useful error checks

For the exact decay equation $y'=-ay$, $a>0$, Euler has multiplier $1-ah$. The computed values decay to zero only if $|1-ah|<1$, or $0<ah<2$. A large step can produce growth even though the exact solution decays. This is a limitation of the method, not evidence against the exact equation.

When no exact solution is available, compare calculations with $h$ and $h/2$, check the data, and examine the expected order on a fixed interval. Such comparisons provide error evidence, not a proof without additional estimates. Roundoff error can also matter when the step becomes very small. Keep the method and step size with any reported approximation so another reader can reproduce it.

## Practice

1. Verify $v(t)=(1+t)e^{-t}$ for $y''+2y'+y=0$, $y(0)=1$, $y'(0)=0$.
2. Apply Euler to $y'=-2y$, $y(0)=1$, with $h=1/4$ for two steps. Compare with the exact value at the endpoint.

## Worked answers

1. Product differentiation gives $v'=-te^{-t}$ and $v''=(t-1)e^{-t}$. Thus $v''+2v'+v=[(t-1)-2t+(1+t)]e^{-t}=0$. Also $v(0)=1$ and $v'(0)=0$. The formula is smooth on $\mathbb R$ and satisfies all requirements.
2. The multiplier is $1-2h=1/2$, so $Y_1=1/2$ and $Y_2=1/4$ at $t=1/2$. The exact value is $e^{-1}\approx0.3678794$, so the error is about $-0.1178794$. The step obeys $0<2h<2$, so this sequence decays, although decay alone does not guarantee high accuracy.
