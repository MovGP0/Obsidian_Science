> [!TLDR]
> Near a smooth equilibrium, eigenvalues with nonzero real parts determine local attraction or instability; zero real parts require further analysis.

## Equilibria and three stability statements

Consider the autonomous system $\mathbf{x}'=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})$ in $\mathbb R^n$. An **equilibrium** is a point $\mathbf{x}_*$ where $\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_*)=0$. The constant function $\mathbf{x}(t)=\mathbf{x}_*$ is a solution. Assume $\mathbf{f}$ is continuously differentiable near the equilibrium, so nearby initial-value problems have unique local solutions.

An equilibrium is **stable** in the sense of Lyapunov if every desired distance $\varepsilon>0$ has an initial distance $\delta>0$ such that a solution starting within $\delta$ stays within $\varepsilon$ for all future times. It is **asymptotically stable** if it is stable and all sufficiently close initial states approach it as $t\to\infty$. It is **unstable** if the stability condition fails. These definitions refer to forward evolution. Reversing the independent variable reverses growth and decay.

A stable point need not attract. The linear system $x'=-y$, $y'=x$ has circles $x^2+y^2=\text{constant}$ as trajectories. Small circles remain small, but do not approach the origin.

## Derive the linearization

Write the displacement as $\boldsymbol{\eta}=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_*$. The first-order Taylor expansion is

$$
\boldsymbol{\eta}'=J\boldsymbol{\eta}+\mathbf{r}(\boldsymbol{\eta}),\qquad
J=D\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}_*),\qquad
\frac{\|\mathbf{r}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\|}{\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|}\longrightarrow0.
$$

The matrix $J$ is the Jacobian: its entry $(i,j)$ is $\partial f_i/\partial x_j$ at the equilibrium. Omitting the remainder gives the linearized system. Its modes are described in [[14.06 Linear systems]]. For an eigenvalue $\lambda=\alpha+i\beta$, the factor $e^{\alpha t}$ determines growth or decay, while $\beta$ produces rotation.

If all eigenvalues of $J$ have negative real part, the nonlinear equilibrium is locally asymptotically stable. If at least one eigenvalue has positive real part, it is unstable. A **hyperbolic** equilibrium has no eigenvalues with zero real part. If a zero real part occurs and no eigenvalue has positive real part, these eigenvalue tests alone do not decide stability. These are local conclusions. They do not claim that every initial state approaches the equilibrium.

In two dimensions, two negative real eigenvalues usually give a stable node; a complex conjugate pair with negative real part gives a stable spiral. Opposite signs give a saddle, which is unstable because some nearby directions grow even though another direction decays. Repeated eigenvalues and missing eigenvectors change trajectory shapes, but do not change the negative-real-part attraction test.

Staying near the origin requires control of every nearby initial state, so the attracting direction cannot make the saddle stable. ## Worked example: two equilibria of a nonlinear system

Analyze $x'=x-x^2$ and $y'=-2y$. The equations $x-x^2=0$ and $-2y=0$ give equilibria $(0,0)$ and $(1,0)$. The Jacobian is

$$
J(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}1-2x&0\\0&-2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

At $(0,0)$ the eigenvalues are $1$ and $-2$, so it is a saddle and is unstable. This can be seen directly: for small positive $x_0$, the scalar solution $x=1/(1+C e^{-t})$, where $C=(1-x_0)/x_0$, increases toward one and leaves a small neighborhood of zero.

At $(1,0)$ the eigenvalues are $-1$ and $-2$, so the equilibrium is locally asymptotically stable. With $u=x-1$, the system becomes $u'=-u-u^2$, $y'=-2y$. The linear terms predict decay near $(u,y)=(0,0)$; the quadratic term is smaller there. The conclusion is local: initial states with sufficiently negative $x$ need not remain near either equilibrium.

## Why a zero eigenvalue is inconclusive

The equations $x'=-x^3$ and $x'=x^3$ both have derivative zero at the equilibrium $x=0$, so both have the same linearization $x'=0$. Their behavior differs. In the first equation a positive value decreases and a negative value increases toward zero. In fact,

$$
x(t)=\frac{x_0}{\sqrt{1+2x_0^2t}}
$$

approaches zero for every $x_0$. In the second equation a nonzero value moves away from zero. Higher-order terms settle the question that the linearization cannot answer. A purely imaginary pair has a similar limitation for nonlinear systems; a linear center alone is not proof of nonlinear stability.

## Practice

1. Classify the origin for $x'=-x-2y$, $y'=2x-y$.
2. Find the equilibria of $x'=x(2-x)$ and classify each by the scalar derivative test.

## Worked answers

1. The characteristic polynomial is $(\lambda+1)^2+4$, with roots $-1\pm2i$. Their real parts are negative, so the origin is an asymptotically stable spiral. Also $(x^2+y^2)'=-2(x^2+y^2)$, which directly gives exponential decay of the squared radius.
2. The equilibria are zero and two. For $f(x)=2x-x^2$, $f'(x)=2-2x$. Thus $f'(0)=2>0$ makes zero unstable, while $f'(2)=-2<0$ makes two locally asymptotically stable. The test is valid here because neither derivative is zero.
