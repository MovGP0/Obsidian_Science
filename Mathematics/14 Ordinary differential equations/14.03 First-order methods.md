title: "First-order methods"

> [!TLDR]
> Separate a product of single-variable factors, or use an integrating factor to turn a first-order linear equation into an exact product derivative.

## Separation of variables

A first-order equation is separable if it can be written as $y'=a(t)b(y)$. Assume the functions are continuous on the region in use. Wherever $b(y)\ne0$, divide by $b(y)$ and integrate:

$$
\frac{y'}{b(y)}=a(t),\qquad
\int\frac{dy}{b(y)}=\int a(t)\,dt+C.
$$

The change of variable on the left follows from the chain rule. If $B'(y)=1/b(y)$, then $(B(y(t)))'=y'/b(y)$. The separated relation is an implicit solution. It need not be possible or useful to solve explicitly for $y$.

Before division, record every zero $r$ of $b$: each constant function $y=r$ solves the original equation. Check these solutions separately. Also select an interval on which all divisions and logarithms are valid. The symbol $\ln|u|$ permits either sign of a nonzero $u$; it does not permit $u=0$.

## Worked example: a nonlinear separable equation

Solve $y'=y(1-y)$ with $y(0)=1/4$. The constant functions $y=0$ and $y=1$ are equilibrium solutions, although neither matches these data. On the branch $0<y<1$, partial fractions give

$$
\frac{1}{y(1-y)}=\frac1y+\frac1{1-y}.
$$

Integrate. Since the derivative of $1-y$ is $-1$, the second term contributes a minus sign:

$$
\ln|y|-\ln|1-y|=t+C,
\qquad \frac{y}{1-y}=K e^t.
$$

The initial value gives $K=(1/4)/(3/4)=1/3$. Solving the algebraic equation yields

$$
y(t)=\frac{e^t}{3+e^t}=\frac{1}{1+3e^{-t}}.
$$

The denominator is positive for all real $t$. The formula stays between zero and one and therefore remains on the branch used in the calculation. Differentiation gives $y'=3e^{-t}/(1+3e^{-t})^2$, which equals $y(1-y)$. This final check guards against a sign error in the logarithms.

## The integrating factor for a linear equation

Write a linear first-order equation in normalized form

$$
y'+p(t)y=q(t),
$$

where $p$ and $q$ are continuous on an interval $I$. If the original coefficient of $y'$ is not one, divide by it only where it is nonzero. Choose a nonzero integrating factor

$$
\mu(t)=\exp\left(\int p(t)\,dt\right).
$$

Since $\mu'=p\mu$, multiplication turns the left side into a product derivative:

$$
\mu y'+\mu p y=(\mu y)'=\mu q.
$$

One integration then gives $y=\mu^{-1}(\int\mu q\,dt+C)$. Multiplying $\mu$ by a nonzero constant does not change the solution family. For initial data at $t_0$, a useful formula with no arbitrary integration limit is

$$
P(t)=\int_{t_0}^{t}p(s)\,ds,\qquad
y(t)=e^{-P(t)}\left[y_0+\int_{t_0}^{t}e^{P(s)}q(s)\,ds\right].
$$

The outer factor accounts for the homogeneous solution, while the integral accounts for the right side. Unlike separation, this method does not divide by $y$ or by a function of $y$, so it does not discard a zero solution through such a division.

## Worked example: a linear equation

Solve $y'+2y=t$, $y(0)=1$. Here $\mu=e^{2t}$, so $(e^{2t}y)'=t e^{2t}$. Integration by parts gives

$$
\int t e^{2t}\,dt=\frac{t e^{2t}}2-\frac{e^{2t}}4+C.
$$

Divide by $e^{2t}$ to obtain $y=t/2-1/4+C e^{-2t}$. The data give $1=-1/4+C$, so

$$
y(t)=\frac t2-\frac14+\frac54e^{-2t}.
$$

The derivative is $1/2-(5/2)e^{-2t}$. Adding $2y$ cancels both the constant terms and the exponential terms, leaving $t$. The formula is therefore an exact solution for all real $t$.

## Practice

1. Solve $y'=-2ty$ with $y(0)=3$. Identify the equilibrium solution that separation can discard.
2. Solve $y'+y=e^t$ with $y(0)=0$ using an integrating factor.

## Worked answers

1. The zero function is an equilibrium. On a nonzero branch, $dy/y=-2t\,dt$, so $\ln|y|=-t^2+C$ and $y=K e^{-t^2}$. The initial value gives $K=3$. Thus $y=3e^{-t^2}$ on $\mathbb R$. Allowing $K=0$ restores the discarded equilibrium in this final family.
2. With $\mu=e^t$, the product equation is $(e^ty)'=e^{2t}$. Hence $e^ty=e^{2t}/2+C$ and $y=e^t/2+C e^{-t}$. The zero initial value gives $C=-1/2$, so $y=(e^t-e^{-t})/2$. Direct differentiation verifies the equation.
