> [!TLDR]
> Classify a differential equation before you choose a solution method: its order, linearity, forcing term, and explicit dependence on the independent variable matter.

## What an ordinary differential equation specifies

An ordinary differential equation, or ODE, relates an unknown function of one real variable to its derivatives. Write the independent variable as $t$, the unknown function as $y(t)$, and its $k$th derivative as $y^{(k)}(t)$. The letter $t$ is only a mathematical coordinate here. A general implicit equation has the form

$$
F(t,y,y',\ldots,y^{(n)})=0.
$$

The **order** is the highest derivative that occurs with a nonzero coefficient on the interval under study. Thus $y''+y=0$ has order two, whereas $y'+y^3=0$ has order one. A power of $y$ does not increase the order. Before classification, simplify terms that cancel. An expression such as $y''-y''+y'=0$ has order one.

The normal form $y^{(n)}=f(t,y,\ldots,y^{(n-1)})$ isolates the highest derivative. It is useful for initial-value problems, but an implicit equation need not admit this form at every point. For example, $t y''+y=0$ permits division by $t$ only on intervals that exclude zero. Its behavior at zero requires a separate analysis.

## Linearity and the two meanings of homogeneous

A scalar linear equation of order $n$ has the form

$$
a_n(t)y^{(n)}+a_{n-1}(t)y^{(n-1)}+\cdots+a_0(t)y=g(t),
\qquad a_n(t)\ne0.
$$

The coefficients depend on $t$ alone. The unknown function and all its derivatives occur to the first power, without products between them. Thus $y''+t y'=\sin t$ is linear. The equations $y'=y^2$, $y y''=1$, and $y'=\sin y$ are nonlinear. A complicated coefficient such as $e^{t^2}$ does not itself destroy linearity.

Let $L[y]$ denote the linear left side. The equation is **homogeneous** if $g=0$ and **inhomogeneous** if $g$ is not identically zero. Linearity gives

$$
L[c_1u+c_2v]=c_1L[u]+c_2L[v].
$$

Therefore linear combinations of homogeneous solutions are solutions. If $p$ solves $L[p]=g$, then $p+h$ also solves it whenever $L[h]=0$. This explains the particular-plus-homogeneous form used in [[14.04 Constant-coefficient equations]].

Some first-order texts also call $y'=H(y/t)$ a homogeneous equation because a scaling substitution is useful. This is a different convention. The equation $y'=(y/t)^2$ is nonlinear, even though it is homogeneous in that scaling sense. State which meaning you use.

## Autonomous equations and systems

A system is **autonomous** when its normal-form right side has no explicit $t$ dependence:

$$
\mathbf{x}'=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}).
$$

Here $\mathbf{x}$ is a column vector of unknown functions. The scalar equation $y'=y(1-y)$ is autonomous; $y'=t y$ is not. Autonomy is independent of linearity: both $y'=2y$ and $y'=y^2$ are autonomous. For an autonomous equation, shifting a solution in $t$ gives another solution on the shifted interval. A fixed initial condition need not be preserved by that shift.

An equation with explicit $t$ dependence can be enlarged into an autonomous system by adding $s'=1$ and replacing $t$ by the new state $s$. This changes the state space; it does not make the original right side independent of its original independent variable.

## Worked example: classify and reduce the order

Consider $y''+3y'=t$. It has order two, constant coefficients, and a nonzero right side. It is linear and inhomogeneous. Put $x_1=y$ and $x_2=y'$. Then

$$
x_1'=x_2,\qquad x_2'=-3x_2+t,
\qquad
\mathbf{x}'=
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&-3\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{x}
+\begin{pmatrix}0\\t\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The system is first order because each state is differentiated once. It is nonautonomous because $t$ occurs explicitly. The reduction preserves the original two pieces of initial data: $x_1(t_0)=y(t_0)$ and $x_2(t_0)=y'(t_0)$. It does not remove the need for two data values.

## Practice

1. Classify $y''+t^2y=0$ by order, linearity, homogeneity, and autonomy after reduction to a first-order system.
2. Reduce $y''=y^2$ to first order. Is the new system linear? Is it autonomous?

## Worked answers

1. The highest derivative is $y''$, so the order is two. The coefficient $t^2$ depends only on $t$, and the right side is zero; the equation is linear and homogeneous. With $x_1=y$ and $x_2=y'$, the second equation is $x_2'=-t^2x_1$. The explicit $t$ makes the system nonautonomous.
2. The system is $x_1'=x_2$, $x_2'=x_1^2$. The square of a state makes it nonlinear. The absence of an explicit $t$ makes it autonomous. A nonlinear autonomous equation must not be classified as linear merely because each derivative has first power.
