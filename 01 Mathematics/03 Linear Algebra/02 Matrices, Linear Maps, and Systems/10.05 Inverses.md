title: "Inverses"

> [!TLDR]
> An invertible linear map has one input for every output; a square matrix is invertible exactly when it has full rank.

## What an inverse must do

For a map $T:V\to W$, an **inverse** is a map $S:W\to V$ such that $S\circ T=I_V$ and $T\circ S=I_W$, where each $I$ is the identity map on the indicated space. We write $S=T^{-1}$. The first equation restores every input and requires injectivity. The second reaches every output and requires surjectivity. Thus a map has a two-sided inverse exactly when it is bijective.

If $T$ is linear and bijective, its inverse is linear. To prove this, let $w_1=T(v_1)$ and $w_2=T(v_2)$. Then

$$
T(av_1+bv_2)=aw_1+bw_2.
$$

There is only one preimage of this output, so $T^{-1}(aw_1+bw_2)=aT^{-1}(w_1)+bT^{-1}(w_2)$. Uniqueness of the inverse also follows: if $S$ and $R$ are both inverses, then $S=S(TR)=(ST)R=R$.

## Finite-dimensional conditions

Let $\dim V=n$ and $\dim W=m$. Rank–nullity gives $\dim\ker T=n-\operatorname{rank}T$. Therefore

$$
T\text{ injective}\iff\operatorname{rank}T=n,
\qquad
T\text{ surjective}\iff\operatorname{rank}T=m.
$$

Both conditions can hold together only when $n=m$. When input and output have the same finite dimension, either injectivity or surjectivity implies the other. For a square $n\times n$ matrix $A$, the following statements are equivalent: it is invertible; its rank is $n$; its kernel is zero; every $Ax=b$ has exactly one solution; its reduced row echelon form is $I_n$; and $\det A\ne0$.

The equal-dimension condition matters. The inclusion $x\mapsto(x,0)$ from $\mathbb R$ to $\mathbb R^2$ is injective but not surjective. Projection $(x,y)\mapsto x$ is surjective but not injective. Finite dimension also matters: on polynomials of arbitrary finite degree, multiplication by the variable is injective but misses every nonzero constant.

## Worked example: compute an inverse by elimination

Take $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$. Apply row operations to $[A\mid I_2]$, keeping the same operation on both halves:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}2&1&\mid&1&0\\1&1&\mid&0&1\end{pmatrix}
\xrightarrow{R_1\leftrightarrow R_2}
\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\mid&0&1\\2&1&\mid&1&0\end{pmatrix}
\xrightarrow{R_2-2R_1}
\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\mid&0&1\\0&-1&\mid&1&-2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Multiply the second row by $-1$, then subtract it from the first:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\mid&1&-1\\0&1&\mid&-1&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Thus $A^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}$. The row operations collectively form a matrix $E$ with $EA=I$; reducing a square matrix completely to $I$ proves full rank, so $E$ is the two-sided inverse. Direct multiplication checks the result:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

To solve $Ax=(3,2)^T$, multiply by the inverse to get $x=(1,1)^T$. Substitution gives $(3,2)^T$ as required. In larger numerical problems, solve the system by a factorization rather than first forming the entire inverse; the inverse is a mathematical description, not a required computational step.

## Useful rules and limitations

For invertible square matrices $A$ and $B$ of the same size,

$$
(AB)^{-1}=B^{-1}A^{-1},\qquad
(A^T)^{-1}=(A^{-1})^T.
$$

The first rule reverses the order because $ABB^{-1}A^{-1}=I$. A sum has no corresponding simple rule: $(A+B)^{-1}$ is generally not $A^{-1}+B^{-1}$. Even if both summands are invertible, their sum can be zero. Also, $A^{-1}$ means a matrix inverse, not the reciprocal of each entry.

## Practice

1. Is $\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}$ invertible? Give a kernel-based reason.
2. Find the inverse of $D=\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&-2\end{pmatrix}$ and solve $Dx=(6,4)^T$.

## Worked answers

1. Multiplication by $(-2,1)^T$ gives $(0,0)^T$. A nonzero kernel vector shows that the map is not injective, so there is no inverse. The second column is twice the first, which also shows rank one.
2. The equations are $3x_1=6$ and $-2x_2=4$. Their nonzero coefficients can be divided separately, so $D^{-1}=\operatorname{diag}(1/3,-1/2)$ and $x=(2,-2)^T$. Multiplying $D$ by this proposed inverse gives the identity.
