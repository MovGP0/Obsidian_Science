title: "Linear maps"

> [!TLDR]
> A linear map preserves linear combinations; its kernel measures lost input directions and its image contains all possible outputs.

## Definition and first tests

Let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces over the same field $\mathbb F$, either $\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$. A map $T:V\to W$ is **linear** if

$$
T(au+bv)=aT(u)+bT(v)
$$

for all $u,v\in V$ and all $a,b\in\mathbb F$. This includes additivity and compatibility with scalar multiplication. Setting both scalars to zero shows that $T(0)=0$. This is a necessary test, not a sufficient test. For example, $f(x)=x^2$ has $f(0)=0$, but $f(1+1)=4$ differs from $f(1)+f(1)=2$.

The map $T(x,y)=(x+2y,3x-y)$ is linear because each output is a linear combination of input components. The map $(x,y)\mapsto(x+1,y)$ is not linear because it sends zero to $(1,0)$. A map of the form $x\mapsto Ax+b$ is called affine; it is linear only if $b=0$.

## Kernel, image, and rank

The **kernel** and **image** of $T$ are

$$
\ker T=\{v\in V:T(v)=0\},
\qquad
\operatorname{im}T=\{T(v):v\in V\}.
$$

Both are subspaces. For example, if $T(u)=T(v)=0$, then $T(au+bv)=0$. For the image, a linear combination $aT(u)+bT(v)$ equals $T(au+bv)$ and is therefore still an output.

The map is **injective** if different inputs always give different outputs. Linearity gives a useful equivalent condition:

$$
T(u)=T(v)
\iff T(u-v)=0,
\qquad
T\text{ injective}\iff\ker T=\{0\}.
$$

It is **surjective** if $\operatorname{im}T=W$. The **rank** is $\dim(\operatorname{im}T)$ and the **nullity** is $\dim(\ker T)$.

For finite-dimensional $V$, the rank–nullity theorem states

$$
\dim V=\operatorname{rank}T+\operatorname{nullity}T.
$$

To see why, choose a basis $k_1,\ldots,k_r$ of the kernel and extend it to a basis $k_1,\ldots,k_r,v_1,\ldots,v_s$ of $V$. The outputs $T(v_1),\ldots,T(v_s)$ span the image because the kernel terms contribute zero. If a combination of these outputs is zero, the same combination of the $v_i$ lies in the kernel. Independence of the full basis forces all its coefficients to vanish. Thus these outputs are a basis of the image, and $r+s=\dim V$ proves the formula.

## Worked example: a map with one lost direction

Define $T:\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R^2$ by

$$
T(x,y,z)=(x+y,y+z).
$$

To find the kernel, set both outputs to zero. The equations $x+y=0$ and $y+z=0$ give $x=-y$ and $z=-y$. Put $y=t$. Then

$$
\ker T=\{t(-1,1,-1):t\in\mathbb R\}.
$$

The nullity is one. The outputs of $(1,0,0)$ and $(0,0,1)$ are $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$, so the image contains a basis of $\mathbb R^2$. Its rank is two and the map is surjective. Rank–nullity gives $3=1+2$, as required.

For output $(2,3)$, choose $y=t$. The preimage is the line

$$
(x,y,z)=(2-t,t,3-t)=(2,0,3)+t(-1,1,-1).
$$

Every nonempty preimage of a linear map is one particular solution plus its kernel. This explains the families of solutions in [[10.04 Linear systems]].

## Composition

If $S:W\to U$ is linear, then $S\circ T:V\to U$ is linear, since applying the two linearity rules in turn preserves every linear combination. Composition acts from right to left. For the map above and $S(a,b)=a-b$,

$$
(S\circ T)(x,y,z)=(x+y)-(y+z)=x-z.
$$

Its rank is one and its kernel is the plane $x=z$. Composition can remove more information: its rank cannot exceed the rank of either factor. Also $\ker T\subseteq\ker(S\circ T)$; an input already sent to zero remains zero.

## Practice

1. Find the rank and kernel of $P(x,y)=(x,0)$ on $\mathbb R^2$.
2. Let $D$ send real polynomials of degree at most two to their derivatives. Give its kernel and image, and check rank–nullity.

## Worked answers

1. $P(x,y)=0$ requires $x=0$, with $y$ free. Thus the kernel is $\operatorname{span}((0,1))$. The image is $\operatorname{span}((1,0))$, so the rank and nullity are both one.
2. Write $p(t)=a+bt+ct^2$. Then $D(p)=b+2ct$. The derivative is zero exactly when $b=c=0$, so constants form a one-dimensional kernel. Every polynomial $u+vt$ occurs by taking $b=u$ and $c=v/2$. The image has basis $1,t$ and rank two. The input has basis $1,t,t^2$, so $3=1+2$.
