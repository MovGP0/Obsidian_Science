> [!TLDR]
> A matrix records a linear map in chosen bases, and multiplication records the successive application of two maps.

## Coordinates come before entries

Let $V$ and $W$ be finite-dimensional vector spaces over $\mathbb F$, with ordered bases $B=(b_1,\ldots,b_n)$ and $C=(c_1,\ldots,c_m)$. Every $v\in V$ has a unique expression $v=x_1b_1+\cdots+x_nb_n$. Its **coordinate column** in $B$ is

$$
[v]_B=\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\\vdots\\x_n\end{pmatrix}.
$$

For a linear map $T:V\to W$, define its matrix $A=[T]_{C\leftarrow B}$ by placing the coordinate columns $[T(b_j)]_C$ side by side. Thus $A$ has $m$ rows and $n$ columns. Its entry $a_{ij}$ is the coefficient of $c_i$ in $T(b_j)$.

Linearity then gives the central rule:

$$
[T(v)]_C
=x_1[T(b_1)]_C+\cdots+x_n[T(b_n)]_C
=A[v]_B.
$$

The column rule explains matrix multiplication by a vector: each input coordinate weights one column. The map and its matrix are different objects. The map acts on vectors; its matrix acts on coordinate columns and changes when the bases change.

## Worked example with different input and output bases

Take $T:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^2$, $T(x,y)=(x+2y,3x+y)$. Use

$$
B=((1,1),(1,-1)),\qquad
C=((1,0),(1,1)).
$$

First evaluate $T$ on the input basis: $T(1,1)=(3,4)$ and $T(1,-1)=(-1,2)$. A vector $(p,q)$ has $C$ coordinates $(p-q,q)$, because

$$
(p,q)=(p-q)(1,0)+q(1,1).
$$

Consequently the two matrix columns are $(-1,4)^T$ and $(-3,2)^T$:

$$
A=[T]_{C\leftarrow B}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&-3\\4&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

If $[v]_B=(2,-1)^T$, the actual vector is $2(1,1)-(1,-1)=(1,3)$. Matrix multiplication gives

$$
[T(v)]_C=
\begin{pmatrix}-1&-3\\4&2\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}1\\6\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Convert back: $1(1,0)+6(1,1)=(7,6)$. Direct evaluation also gives $T(1,3)=(7,6)$. This checks both bases and the order of the columns.

## Matrix multiplication is composition

Suppose $S:W\to U$ has matrix $D=[S]_{E\leftarrow C}$ in a basis $E$ of $U$. The output coordinates of $T$ are exactly the input coordinates required by $D$. Therefore

$$
[(S\circ T)(v)]_E=D(A[v]_B)=(DA)[v]_B.
$$

If $D$ is $p\times m$ and $A$ is $m\times n$, their product is $p\times n$, with entries

$$
(DA)_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^m d_{ik}a_{kj}.
$$

The shared index $k$ runs through intermediate coordinates. For example,

$$
\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\1&3\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}4&6\\1&3\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The upper-left entry is $1\cdot2+2\cdot1=4$. Reversing the factors gives $\begin{pmatrix}2&4\\1&5\end{pmatrix}$, so composition and multiplication are generally not commutative. They are associative because applying three maps does not depend on how their composition is grouped.

## Change of basis

Let $P$ have columns equal to a new input basis in old coordinates, and let $Q$ do the same for the output. Then $[v]_{\rm old}=P[v]_{\rm new}$ and $[T(v)]_{\rm old}=Q[T(v)]_{\rm new}$. Substitution into the old matrix equation gives

$$
A_{\rm new}=Q^{-1}A_{\rm old}P.
$$

For an endomorphism $T:V\to V$ with the same basis change on input and output, this becomes $P^{-1}AP$, called **similarity**. Arbitrary row operations are not a similarity transformation. They change equations or output coordinates without necessarily making the same change on the input. Invertible coordinate matrices are treated in [[10.05 Inverses]].

## Practice

1. Find the standard matrix of $T(x,y,z)=(x-z,2y)$.
2. Let $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $B=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$. Compute the matrix of “apply $A$, then $B$” and its action on $(1,2)^T$.

## Worked answers

1. The outputs of the standard basis vectors are $(1,0)$, $(0,2)$, and $(-1,0)$. Put them in columns to obtain $\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-1\\0&2&0\end{pmatrix}$, a $2\times3$ matrix.
2. The order is $BA=\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$. It gives $(6,6)^T$. Checking the stages, $A(1,2)^T=(3,2)^T$ and $B(3,2)^T=(6,6)^T$. The product $AB$ would describe the opposite order.
