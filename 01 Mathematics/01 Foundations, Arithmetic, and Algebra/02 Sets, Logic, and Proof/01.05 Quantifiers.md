> [!TLDR]
> Quantifiers state how many objects satisfy a condition; their order determines which choices can depend on earlier choices.

## Universal and existential statements

A predicate $P(x)$ is a condition about an object $x$. A domain tells us which objects are permitted. The statement

$$
\forall x\in A,\ P(x)
$$

means that $P(x)$ holds for every $x$ in the set $A$. The statement $\exists x\in A,\ P(x)$ means that at least one member of $A$ satisfies the condition. A suitable member is called a witness. Existence alone does not assert uniqueness. The notation $\exists!x\in A,\ P(x)$ asserts that exactly one member works.

For example, $\forall x\in\mathbb R,\ x^2\ge0$ is true. The statement $\exists x\in\mathbb R,\ x^2=4$ is true and has two witnesses, $2$ and $-2$. Over $[0,\infty)$, the equation has the unique witness $2$. Changing the domain changes the claim.

To prove a universal statement, start with an arbitrary member of the domain and establish the condition without an extra restriction on that member. A few examples do not prove it. To disprove it, one counterexample suffices. To prove existence, exhibit a witness and check it, or give an argument that guarantees one exists.

## Order and dependence

Quantifiers are read from left to right, with the scope of each extending through the following condition. Compare

$$
\forall x\in\mathbb R\ \exists y\in\mathbb R,\ y>x
$$

with

$$
\exists y\in\mathbb R\ \forall x\in\mathbb R,\ y>x.
$$

The first is true: after any $x$ is given, choose $y=x+1$. The witness can depend on $x$. The second is false: it requires one fixed real $y$ to exceed every real number. Taking $x=y$ contradicts $y>x$. A statement can therefore change truth when adjacent unlike quantifiers are exchanged. Two universal quantifiers over fixed independent domains can be exchanged, as can two existential quantifiers.

A useful reading habit is to describe the choice process. “For every input, there is an output” lets the output depend on the input. “There is an output for every input” can be ambiguous in ordinary English; symbols or a phrase such as “one fixed output” remove the ambiguity.

## Negation

Negating a universal statement requires an exception. Negating an existential statement requires failure everywhere:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\neg(\forall x\in A,\ P(x))&\Leftrightarrow\exists x\in A,\ \neg P(x),\\
\neg(\exists x\in A,\ P(x))&\Leftrightarrow\forall x\in A,\ \neg P(x).
\end{aligned}
$$

When several quantifiers occur, change each one and negate the final condition. Keep their order. For example,

$$
\neg(\forall x\in\mathbb R\ \exists y\in\mathbb R,\ y>x)
\Leftrightarrow
\exists x\in\mathbb R\ \forall y\in\mathbb R,\ y\le x.
$$

The inequality changes from $>$ to $\le$, not to $<$. Equality must remain in the negation. If the condition is an implication, use the rule that its failure requires a true hypothesis and a false conclusion; see [[01.04 Logical statements]].

## Worked example: a quantified bound

Consider the claim

$$
\exists M\in\mathbb R\ \forall n\in\mathbb N,\ n<M.
$$

It asserts a single strict upper bound for all natural numbers. Its negation is

$$
\forall M\in\mathbb R\ \exists n\in\mathbb N,\ n\ge M.
$$

The Archimedean property of $\mathbb R$ states that the natural numbers have no real upper bound, so the negation is true. For a concrete proposed bound $M=12.7$, the witness $n=13$ defeats the original claim. A single numerical example defeats only that proposed bound; the Archimedean property is what covers every real $M$. By contrast, if the domain were $\{0,1,\ldots,12\}$, then $M=13$ would prove the bounded claim.

The empty domain has special but consistent behavior. A universal claim over $\varnothing$ is true, because no element violates it. An existential claim over $\varnothing$ is false, because no witness exists. These facts follow directly from the meanings of “every” and “there exists.”

## Practice

1. Decide whether each statement is true: $\forall x\in\mathbb R\ \exists y\in\mathbb R,\ x+y=0$, and $\exists y\in\mathbb R\ \forall x\in\mathbb R,\ x+y=0$.
2. Negate $\forall n\in\mathbb Z,\ (n>0\Rightarrow n^2>1)$. Use the negation to find a counterexample.

> [!example]- Worked answers
> 1. The first statement is true because, once $x$ is given, $y=-x$ gives $x+y=0$. The second is false. Input $x=0$ would require the fixed $y$ to be $0$, but input $x=1$ would then give $1+y=1\ne0$.
>
> 2. Its negation is $\exists n\in\mathbb Z,\ (n>0\land n^2\le1)$. Choose $n=1$. It is a positive integer, but $1^2=1$ fails the strict conclusion. Thus the original universal statement is false.
