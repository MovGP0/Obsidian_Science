---
title: "3.3 The electric field"
---

> [!TLDR]
> The electric field gives the force per unit positive charge at a point.

The electric and magnetic fields describe how forces act on charges. Electrostatics uses the [[2.8 Gradient|gradient]], [[2.9 Divergence|divergence]], [[2.10 Curl|curl]], and [[2.11 Laplacian|Laplacian]] to relate the field to the charge and potential.

Michael Faraday used the term *field of force* for the region around electric charges. James Clerk Maxwell described the electric field in terms of the space around electrified bodies. An operational definition uses the force $\mechanical{\vec F}_{\mathrm E}$ that a small positive test charge $\electric{q}_0$ would experience at a point.[^10]

$$
\electric{\vec E}=\frac{\mechanical{\vec F}_{\mathrm E}}{\electric{q}_0}. \tag{3.26}
$$

The field describes the force per unit charge at the point, including when the test charge is absent.

The electric field has the direction of the force on positive charge. Its SI unit is the newton per coulomb, $\unit{N\,C^{-1}}$, equivalent to the volt per meter, $\unit{V\,m^{-1}}$, because $\unit{V}=\unit{N\,m\,C^{-1}}$.

In electrostatics, the field can be represented by the covector $-\mathrm d\electric{V}$, where $\electric{V}$ is electric potential. Its components transform as a [[5.9 Vectors and one-forms|one-form]]. The metric identifies this covector with the usual field vector. See [[4 Covariant and contravariant vector components]] for component transformations.

Once the field is known, a charge $\electric{q}$ placed at that point experiences the force

$$
\mechanical{\vec F}_{\mathrm E}=\electric{q}\electric{\vec E}. \tag{3.27}
$$

A negative charge experiences a force opposite to the field direction.

Stationary electric charges produce electrostatic fields. A changing magnetic field can also produce an electric field. To visualize a field, use arrows or field lines. The arrow length shows the magnitude at its location. Closer field lines show a stronger field, and the field also exists between the drawn lines.

Figs. 3.16 and 3.17 illustrate the fields of positive and negative point charges. Arrows and field lines point in the direction of the force on a positive test charge. Electrostatic lines leave positive charges and enter negative charges, with endpoints at infinity possible for isolated charges. At a point where the field is nonzero, its direction is unique, so two field lines cannot cross there. When several charges contribute, add their fields as vectors.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-3-16.png|**Fig. 3.16** Electric field arrows for a positive point charge (a) and a negative point charge (b).]]

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-3-17.png|**Fig. 3.17** Electric field lines for a positive point charge (a) and a negative point charge (b).]]

To find the force on a charge in a fixed distribution, first calculate the total field produced by the other charges at its location. Then multiply that field by the charge. The charge itself also produces a field. This calculation treats the other charges as fixed, which is appropriate when the charge under study has negligible effect on their positions. The approach is analogous to using Earth's gravitational field to find the force on a small object.

### Field of a point charge

For a point charge $\electric{q}$, the field at a distance $\mechanical{r}$ is

$$
\electric{\vec E}=\electric{k}_e\frac{\electric{q}}{\mechanical{r}^2}\hat r, \tag{3.28}
$$

where $\electric{k}_e=\quantity{8.99\times10^9}{N\,m^2\,C^{-2}}$ is the Coulomb constant and $\hat r$ points away from the source charge.

At $\quantity{1}{m}$ from a proton with charge $\electric{q}\approx\quantity{1.60\times10^{-19}}{C}$,

$$
\electric{\vec E}
\approx(\quantity{8.99\times10^9}{N\,m^2\,C^{-2}})
\frac{\quantity{1.60\times10^{-19}}{C}}{(\quantity{1}{m})^2}\hat r
\approx(\quantity{1.44\times10^{-9}}{N\,C^{-1}})\hat r.
$$

An electron has the opposite charge, so at the same distance its field is $-(\quantity{1.44\times10^{-9}}{N\,C^{-1}})\hat r$. The negative sign makes the field point inward.

### Superposition of point-charge fields

Fig. 3.18 shows three point charges near an electron at the origin. The field from $\electric{q}_1$ points away from that positive charge. The fields from $\electric{q}_2$ and $\electric{q}_3$ point toward those negative charges. Fig. 3.19 shows the directions of all three contributions at the electron.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-3-18.png|**Fig. 3.18** Charges of +5 nC, −6 nC, and −8 nC at specified positions near an electron.]]

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-3-19.png|**Fig. 3.19** The three electric fields and the point at which to calculate their sum.]]

The arrows are drawn so that each set shows a direction along the line through the electron. At the electron, the field from $\electric{q}_1$ points down and to the right, the field from $\electric{q}_2$ points down and to the left, and the field from $\electric{q}_3$ points up and to the right. Add all three vector contributions. The field continues beyond the arrows shown in the figure.

For each source, apply Equation (3.28) with a unit vector $\hat r_i$ pointing from that source to the observation point.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\electric{\vec E}_1&=\electric{k}_e\frac{\electric{q}_1}{\mechanical{r}_1^2}\hat r_1,\\
\electric{\vec E}_2&=\electric{k}_e\frac{\electric{q}_2}{\mechanical{r}_2^2}\hat r_2,\\
\electric{\vec E}_3&=\electric{k}_e\frac{\electric{q}_3}{\mechanical{r}_3^2}\hat r_3.
\end{aligned} \tag{3.29}
$$

The three unit vectors generally point in different directions. Add the Cartesian components of their field contributions to find the total field. The [[3.5 Chapter 3 problems|Chapter 3 problems]] include an example.

### Gauss's law

The differential form of Gauss's law for the electric field is

$$
\nabla\cdot\electric{\vec E}=\frac{\electric{\rho}}{\electric{\varepsilon}_0}, \tag{3.30}
$$

where $\electric{\rho}$ is the volume charge density and $\electric{\varepsilon}_0\approx\quantity{8.85\times10^{-12}}{F\,m^{-1}}$ is the vacuum permittivity. Positive charge is a source of electric field lines, and negative charge is a sink.

For an isolated point charge, the field has only a radial component. Away from the source, the spherical divergence from [[2.9 Divergence]] gives

$$
\nabla\cdot\electric{\vec E}
=\frac1{\mechanical{r}^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mechanical{r}}
   \left(\mechanical{r}^2\electric{E}_r\right)
=\frac1{\mechanical{r}^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mechanical{r}}
   \left(\electric{k}_e\electric{q}\right)=0,\qquad\mechanical{r}>0.
$$

At the source, $\mechanical{r}=0$, the field is singular and the point charge contributes to the divergence there.

### Curl and electric potential

For a point charge, $\electric{E}_\theta=\electric{E}_\phi=0$. The spherical formula for [[2.10 Curl|curl]] reduces to

$$
\nabla\times\electric{\vec E}
=\frac1{\mechanical{r}\sin\theta}\frac{\partial\electric{E}_r}{\partial\phi}\hat\theta
-\frac1{\mechanical{r}}\frac{\partial\electric{E}_r}{\partial\theta}\hat\phi
=\vec 0.
$$

Both derivatives vanish because $\electric{E}_r=\electric{k}_e\electric{q}/\mechanical{r}^2$ depends only on the radius. Curl-free fields are locally gradients of scalar potentials. In electrostatics the convention is

$$
\electric{\vec E}=-\nabla\electric{V}, \tag{3.31}
$$

where $\electric{V}$ is the electric potential in volts, equivalent to joules per coulomb. Following the field decreases the potential. Going against the field increases it. Moving perpendicular to the field leaves the potential unchanged to first order, so equipotential surfaces are perpendicular to the field at points where it is nonzero.

### Poisson's and Laplace's equations

Taking the divergence of Equation (3.31) gives

$$
\nabla\cdot\electric{\vec E}=-\nabla^2\electric{V}. \tag{3.32}
$$

Combining it with Gauss's law gives *Poisson's equation*.

$$
\nabla^2\electric{V}=-\frac{\electric{\rho}}{\electric{\varepsilon}_0}. \tag{3.33}
$$

Positive charge gives a negative Laplacian of the potential, and negative charge gives a positive Laplacian. These signs are consistent with peaks and valleys, respectively. In a region with zero charge density, *Laplace's equation* applies.

$$
\nabla^2\electric{V}=0. \tag{3.34}
$$

In a connected charge-free region, a nonconstant potential has no interior local maximum or minimum.

[^10]: Use a test charge small enough to have negligible effect on the field sources and physically small enough to sample the field near one point.
