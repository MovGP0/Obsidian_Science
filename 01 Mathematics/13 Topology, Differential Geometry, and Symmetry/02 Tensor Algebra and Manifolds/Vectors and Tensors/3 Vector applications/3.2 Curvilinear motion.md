---
title: "3.2 Curvilinear motion"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Tangential acceleration changes speed, and normal acceleration changes direction along a curved path.

Acceleration measures the rate of change of velocity, including changes in speed and direction. In a car, the accelerator can increase speed and the brake can decrease it. Turning the steering wheel can change the direction of motion at constant speed.[^8]

The *tangential* component of acceleration is parallel or antiparallel to the velocity. The perpendicular component is the *normal*, or locally *radial*, acceleration. A nonzero normal component turns the velocity and produces curvilinear motion.

In Fig. 3.7, the velocity of a car at position $B$ is tangent to the path. To find the acceleration at $A$, $B$, or $C$, determine how the velocity changes with time there.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-3-7.png|**Fig. 3.7** The velocity of a car is tangent to its curved path.]]

Fig. 3.8 compares the velocity just before and just after each position. At $A$, the car slows down before the turn. The velocity vector becomes shorter and keeps its direction. At $B$, the car turns at constant speed. The vector changes direction and keeps its length. At $C$, the car speeds up after the turn and the vector becomes longer.

The average acceleration over a time interval $\Delta t$ is $\mechanical{\vec a}_{\mathrm{avg}}=\Delta\mechanical{\vec v}/\Delta t$. To obtain the change in velocity, subtract the earlier vector from the later one, $\Delta\mechanical{\vec v}=\mechanical{\vec v}_{\mathrm{final}}-\mechanical{\vec v}_{\mathrm{initial}}$. Fig. 3.9 shows these pairs of vectors.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-3-8.png|**Fig. 3.8** Velocity just before and after positions A, B, and C.]]

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-3-9.png|**Fig. 3.9** Initial, final, and reversed initial velocity vectors at the three positions.]]

To subtract the initial velocity, add its negative to the final velocity by the [[1.4 Vector addition and multiplication by a scalar|head-to-tail method]]. In Fig. 3.10, the vectors at $A$ and $C$ are offset slightly so that both remain visible.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-3-10.png|**Fig. 3.10** The difference between the final and initial velocity at positions A, B, and C.]]

At $A$, the change in velocity points opposite the velocity, so the tangential acceleration is negative. At $B$, the speed is constant and the instantaneous acceleration points toward the center of curvature, perpendicular to the velocity.[^9] Fig. 3.11 shows the directions of these changes on the path. At $C$, the change in velocity points along the velocity, so the tangential acceleration is positive.

The magnitude of the normal acceleration at $B$ depends on the square of the speed and on the radius of curvature. An acceleration toward the center of curvature is called *centripetal acceleration*. The net inward force that produces it is called the *centripetal force*. For a car turning on a flat road, tire friction can supply this force. For a rock swung on a rope, the rope tension supplies it. For a bucket swung in a circle, the arm transmits force through the handle and the bucket exerts force on the water.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-3-11.png|**Fig. 3.11** The velocity changes along the path give the directions of acceleration at A, B, and C.]]

The outward radial direction and the inward centripetal direction use opposite signs. If $\mechanical{a}_r$ and $\mechanical{a}_c$ are the signed components of the same acceleration in those two directions, then $\mechanical{a}_r=-\mechanical{a}_c$.

### Centripetal and centrifugal force

In an inertial frame, the car, the rock, and the bucket accelerate inward. In a car turning right, the passenger tends to continue in a straight line and moves toward the left door relative to the car. The door can then push the passenger to the right. In the rotating frame of the car, an apparent force points outward. This is the *centrifugal force*. The rotating frame is non-inertial.

Fig. 3.12 shows the same distinction for a hammer thrower. From the stadium, the cable pulls the hammer toward the center and changes its direction. In the frame rotating with the hammer, the hammer stays at a fixed distance before release, so its inward cable tension balances the apparent outward centrifugal force.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-3-12.png|**Fig. 3.12** Top view of a hammer thrower and the circular path of the hammer.]]

### Uniform circular motion

Once you know the centripetal acceleration, Newton's second law $\mechanical{\vec F}=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{\vec a}$ gives the centripetal force. Figs. 3.13 and 3.14 show how the acceleration depends on the speed and radius of curvature.

In uniform circular motion, the speed and radius of curvature stay constant. In Fig. 3.13, the positions subtend an angle $\Delta\theta$ at the center. The arc length between them is $\mechanical{r}\Delta\theta$, with $\Delta\theta$ measured in radians. The velocities at the endpoints have equal magnitudes, $|\mechanical{\vec v}_{\mathrm{initial}}|=|\mechanical{\vec v}_{\mathrm{final}}|=|\mechanical{\vec v}|$. The speed is the arc length divided by the travel time.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-3-13.png|**Fig. 3.13** Two tangent velocities on a circular path.]]

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-3-14.png|**Fig. 3.14** The velocity difference is the chord between the tips of two equal-length velocity vectors.]]

The speed and angular displacement are related by

$$
|\mechanical{\vec v}|=\frac{\mechanical{r}\Delta\theta}{\Delta t}, \tag{3.21}
$$

and

$$
\Delta\theta=\frac{|\mechanical{\vec v}|\Delta t}{\mechanical{r}}. \tag{3.22}
$$

To find the change in velocity, add $\mechanical{\vec v}_{\mathrm{final}}$ to $-\mechanical{\vec v}_{\mathrm{initial}}$ as in Fig. 3.14. The triangle has two sides of length $|\mechanical{\vec v}|$ and an interior angle $\Delta\theta$ between them. The opposite side has length $|\Delta\mechanical{\vec v}|$. It is a chord of an arc of length $|\mechanical{\vec v}|\Delta\theta$.

The figure places the change in velocity at the midpoint of the two positions. As $\Delta\theta$ approaches zero, the chord length approaches the arc length to first order.

$$
|\Delta\mechanical{\vec v}|
\approx|\mechanical{\vec v}|\Delta\theta
=\frac{|\mechanical{\vec v}|^2\Delta t}{\mechanical{r}}. \tag{3.23}
$$

Divide by $\Delta t$ and take the limit.

$$
|\mechanical{\vec a}_c|
=\lim_{\Delta t\to0}\frac{|\Delta\mechanical{\vec v}|}{\Delta t}
=\frac{|\mechanical{\vec v}|^2}{\mechanical{r}}. \tag{3.24}
$$

The same result gives the instantaneous normal acceleration on a smooth path, using the local speed and radius of curvature. Doubling the speed at fixed curvature quadruples the required acceleration and force.

In Fig. 3.12, suppose the hammer has a mass of $\quantity{4}{kg}$, the cable is $\quantity{1.2}{m}$ long, and the speed just before release is $\quantity{20}{m\,s^{-1}}$.

$$
|\mechanical{\vec a}_c|
=\frac{(\quantity{20}{m\,s^{-1}})^2}{\quantity{1.2}{m}}
\approx\quantity{333.3}{m\,s^{-2}}.
$$

The required centripetal force is

$$
|\mechanical{\vec F}_c|
=\mechanical{m}|\mechanical{\vec a}_c|
\approx(\quantity{4}{kg})(\quantity{333.3}{m\,s^{-2}})
\approx\quantity{1333.3}{N}.
$$

This is nearly $\quantity{300}{lbf}$, with the mass of the cable ignored. To find the total acceleration, add the inward acceleration to the tangential acceleration, which gives the rate of change of speed. Fig. 3.15 shows the vector sum.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-3-15.png|**Fig. 3.15** Total acceleration as the vector sum of centripetal and tangential acceleration.]]

The tangential component is $\mathrm d|\mechanical{\vec v}|/\mathrm dt$. Since it is perpendicular to the centripetal component,

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\mechanical{\vec a}_{\mathrm{total}}|
&=\sqrt{|\mechanical{\vec a}_c|^2+|\mechanical{\vec a}_{\mathrm{tang}}|^2}\\
&=\sqrt{\left(\frac{|\mechanical{\vec v}|^2}{\mechanical{r}}\right)^2
+\left(\frac{\mathrm d|\mechanical{\vec v}|}{\mathrm dt}\right)^2}.
\end{aligned} \tag{3.25}
$$

The [[3.5 Chapter 3 problems|Chapter 3 problems]] include an example with both components.

[^8]: During a real turn, friction can also change the car's speed. The perpendicular acceleration turns the car.
[^9]: When the positive radial direction points outward, centripetal acceleration has a negative radial component.

| $\mechanical{m}$ | $\unit{kg}$ | Mass | The mass of the moving object. |
