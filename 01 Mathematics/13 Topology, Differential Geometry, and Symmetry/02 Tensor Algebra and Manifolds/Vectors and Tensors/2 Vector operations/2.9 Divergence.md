---
title: "2.9 Divergence"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Divergence measures the local net outward flow of a vector field.

For a vector field $\vec A$, $\nabla\cdot\vec A$ denotes its *divergence*. It measures the local tendency of the field to flow away from a point or toward it.[^7]

Electrostatic field vectors point away from positive charges and toward negative charges. Fluid velocity shows the same pattern around a source, such as an underwater spring, or a sink, such as a drain. James Clerk Maxwell called the inward tendency *convergence*. The modern divergence convention takes net outward flow as positive and net inward flow as negative.

In a flow, imagine sprinkling sawdust on the surface. If the sawdust spreads out, the local divergence is positive. If it becomes more concentrated, the local divergence is negative.

In Fig. 2.12, the divergence is positive at points 1 and 2, where the arrows grow in the direction of flow. It is negative at point 3, where the arrows get shorter in that direction. In Fig. 2.13(a), the divergence is positive at points 4 and 5. In Fig. 2.13(b), it is positive at point 6. At points 7 and 8, the arrows spread out but get shorter along the flow. The calculation must account for both effects.

In Cartesian coordinates, expand the operator and the vector field.

$$
\nabla\cdot\vec A
=\left(\hat i\frac{\partial}{\partial x}
+\hat j\frac{\partial}{\partial y}
+\hat k\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)
\cdot(A_x\hat i+A_y\hat j+A_z\hat k). \tag{2.33}
$$

The Cartesian basis vectors are constant, have unit magnitude, and are mutually perpendicular. Only the terms with matching unit vectors survive.

$$
\nabla\cdot\vec A
=\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial x}
+\frac{\partial A_y}{\partial y}
+\frac{\partial A_z}{\partial z}. \tag{2.34}
$$

The divergence is a scalar. To model Fig. 2.12, use $\vec A=\sin(\pi x)\hat i$. Its $y$- and $z$-components are zero, so

$$
\nabla\cdot\vec A=\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial x}=\pi\cos(\pi x). \tag{2.35}
$$

The result is positive on $0<x<1/2$, zero at $x=1/2$, and negative on $1/2<x<3/2$.

### Radial fields

Fig. 2.13(a) represents a slice through a spherically symmetric field $\vec A=r^2\hat r$. For $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}>0$,

$$
\hat r=\frac{x\hat i+y\hat j+z\hat k}{r},
\qquad
\vec A=r(x\hat i+y\hat j+z\hat k).
$$

The product rule gives $\partial A_x/\partial x=r+x^2/r$. Apply the same rule to the other components and add.

$$
\nabla\cdot\vec A=3r+\frac{x^2+y^2+z^2}{r}=4r.
$$

The divergence in Fig. 2.13(a) therefore increases linearly with distance from the origin.

In Fig. 2.13(b), the magnitude decreases as the inverse square of the distance. For $r>0$,

$$
\vec A=\frac{\hat r}{r^2}
=\frac{x\hat i+y\hat j+z\hat k}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}}.
$$

Its $x$-component gives

$$
\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial x}
=\frac1{r^3}-\frac{3x^2}{r^5}.
$$

Adding the three component derivatives gives

$$
\nabla\cdot\vec A=\frac3{r^3}-\frac{3(x^2+y^2+z^2)}{r^5}=0,\qquad r>0.
$$

The decrease in magnitude exactly compensates for the spreading of the radial field lines away from the origin.[^8] To determine the sign of the divergence, account for both the spacing and the magnitude of the vectors. Compare the outward and inward flows across a small volume around the point. Greater outward flow gives positive divergence, greater inward flow gives negative divergence, and equal flows give zero divergence.

### Cylindrical and spherical coordinates

Use the coordinate system that matches the symmetry of the field. In cylindrical coordinates,

$$
\nabla\cdot\vec A
=\frac1r\frac{\partial(rA_r)}{\partial r}
+\frac1r\frac{\partial A_\phi}{\partial\phi}
+\frac{\partial A_z}{\partial z}. \tag{2.36}
$$

In spherical coordinates,

$$
\nabla\cdot\vec A
=\frac1{r^2}\frac{\partial(r^2A_r)}{\partial r}
+\frac1{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial(A_\theta\sin\theta)}{\partial\theta}
+\frac1{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial A_\phi}{\partial\phi}. \tag{2.37}
$$

Equation (2.37) simplifies the last two examples because each field has only a radial component.

[^7]: For fields that do not describe motion, interpret flow as an analogy with an incompressible fluid.
[^8]: The inverse-square field is singular at the origin. Its divergence there is described with the Dirac delta function.
