> [!TLDR]
> The Laplacian takes the divergence of the gradient and measures the sum of the Cartesian second partial derivatives.

The *Laplacian*, named for Pierre-Simon Laplace, is the divergence of the gradient of a scalar field. It is usually written $\nabla^2\phi$ and is also written $\Delta\phi$.

In Cartesian coordinates, the [[2.8 Gradient|gradient]] is

$$
\nabla\phi=\hat i\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}
+\hat j\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}
+\hat k\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}. \tag{2.43}
$$

The [[2.9 Divergence|divergence]] of a vector field is

$$
\nabla\cdot\vec A=\frac{\partial A_x}{\partial x}
+\frac{\partial A_y}{\partial y}
+\frac{\partial A_z}{\partial z}. \tag{2.44}
$$

Substitute the components of $\nabla\phi$ for those of $\vec A$.

$$
\nabla\cdot(\nabla\phi)=\nabla^2\phi
=\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x^2}
+\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial y^2}
+\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial z^2}. \tag{2.45}
$$

### Comparison with neighboring values

The Cartesian Laplacian $\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2+\partial_z^2$ adds the changes in slope in the three coordinate directions. Changes in slope help identify peaks and valleys of a function and features such as blobs or edges in a digital image. Acceleration is another example of a second derivative.

The Laplacian describes, up to a scale factor, how the value around a point differs on average from the value at that point. In Fig. 2.17, the center $(0,0,0)$ has value $\phi_0$. The six neighbors lie in front and behind along $x$, to the right and left along $y$, and above and below along $z$.

On the $x$-axis, place the front and back points a distance $\Delta x$ from the origin. In Fig. 2.18, approximate the slope at $B$ by $(\phi_0-\phi_{\mathrm{Back}})/\Delta x$ and the slope at $A$ by $(\phi_{\mathrm{Front}}-\phi_0)/\Delta x$.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-2-17.png|**Fig. 2.17** Six neighboring points around the origin, where the function has value φ₀.]]

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-2-18.png|**Fig. 2.18** Estimates of the first and second derivatives along the x-axis.]]

Take the difference between the two slope estimates and divide by the distance $\Delta x$ between $A$ and $B$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x^2}
&\approx
\frac{(\phi_{\mathrm{Front}}-\phi_0)/\Delta x-(\phi_0-\phi_{\mathrm{Back}})/\Delta x}{\Delta x}\\
&=\frac{\phi_{\mathrm{Front}}+\phi_{\mathrm{Back}}-2\phi_0}{(\Delta x)^2}.
\end{aligned} \tag{2.46}
$$

The same construction at the points to the right and left gives

$$
\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial y^2}
\approx\frac{\phi_{\mathrm{Right}}+\phi_{\mathrm{Left}}-2\phi_0}{(\Delta y)^2}. \tag{2.47}
$$

The points above and below give

$$
\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial z^2}
\approx\frac{\phi_{\mathrm{Top}}+\phi_{\mathrm{Bottom}}-2\phi_0}{(\Delta z)^2}. \tag{2.48}
$$

Choose equal spacings $h=\Delta x=\Delta y=\Delta z$. Add the three second differences.

$$
\nabla^2\phi
\approx\frac{\phi_{\mathrm{Front}}+\phi_{\mathrm{Back}}+\phi_{\mathrm{Right}}+
\phi_{\mathrm{Left}}+\phi_{\mathrm{Top}}+\phi_{\mathrm{Bottom}}-6\phi_0}{h^2}. \tag{2.49}
$$

Let $\phi_{\mathrm{avg}}$ be the average of the six neighboring values. Then

$$
\nabla^2\phi\approx-\frac6{h^2}(\phi_0-\phi_{\mathrm{avg}}). \tag{2.50}
$$

The estimate is negative when the center is above the average and positive when the center is below it. At a smooth local maximum the Laplacian is nonpositive, and at a smooth local minimum it is nonnegative. The Laplacian is useful for detecting concavity and features such as peaks.

Near a smooth peak, gradient vectors point inward toward the peak. An inward flow has negative divergence, so the divergence of the gradient is negative where that convergence occurs, consistent with the sign near a nondegenerate maximum.

Fig. 2.19 uses a $1/r$ profile to illustrate a steep peak and inward-pointing gradient vectors. The ideal $1/r$ function is singular at $r=0$. The finite height of the illustration represents a plot limit. For a spherically symmetric $1/r$ field in three dimensions, the full Laplacian is zero for $r>0$, even though the gradient in the plotted plane points inward.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-2-19.png|**Fig. 2.19** Side and top views of a 1/r profile and its inward-pointing planar gradient.]]

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-2-20.png|**Fig. 2.20** Side and top views of a negative 1/r profile and its outward-pointing planar gradient.]]

Near a smooth valley bottom, gradient vectors point outward. The divergence of the gradient is positive where the outward flow exceeds the inward flow. Away from an extremum, the sign depends on the shape of the function around the point. Equal outward and inward flows give zero Laplacian.

In a circularly symmetric valley with constant slope, the gradient vectors spread apart while keeping their magnitude. This gives a positive Laplacian. If the walls become less steep with distance from the bottom, the decrease in gradient magnitude can offset the spreading.

To examine this compensation in three dimensions, take $\phi=k/r$ for $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}$ and constant $k$. The first derivative in the $x$-direction is

$$
\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}
=-\frac{k(2x)}{2(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}}
=-\frac{kx}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}}.
$$

Differentiation with respect to $x$ again gives

$$
\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x^2}
=-\frac{k}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}}+\frac{3kx^2}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{5/2}}.
$$

Similarly,

$$
\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial y^2}=-\frac{k}{r^3}+\frac{3ky^2}{r^5},
\qquad
\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial z^2}=-\frac{k}{r^3}+\frac{3kz^2}{r^5}.
$$

Adding them yields

$$
\nabla^2\phi=-\frac{3k}{r^3}+\frac{3k(x^2+y^2+z^2)}{r^5}=0,\qquad r>0.
$$

The function is singular at the origin. A distributional treatment of that point uses the Dirac delta function.

### Cylindrical and spherical coordinates

The cylindrical Laplacian of a scalar field $\psi$ is

$$
\nabla^2\psi
=\frac1r\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial r}\right)
+\frac1{r^2}\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial\phi^2}
+\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial z^2}. \tag{2.51}
$$

The spherical Laplacian is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla^2\psi
&=\frac1{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^2\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial r}\right)
+\frac1{r^2\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}
  \left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\theta}\right)\\
&\quad+\frac1{r^2\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial\phi^2}.
\end{aligned} \tag{2.52}
$$
