> [!TLDR]
> A partial derivative measures how a function changes with one independent variable while the others stay fixed.

Partial derivatives are used to calculate the [[2.8 Gradient|gradient]], [[2.9 Divergence|divergence]], [[2.10 Curl|curl]], and [[2.11 Laplacian|Laplacian]].

Ordinary derivatives appear in the slope of a curve, $m=\mathrm{d}y/\mathrm{d}x$, and in the velocity component $\mechanical{v}_x=\mathrm{d}\mechanical{x}/\mathrm{d}t$. Partial derivatives extend the same ideas to functions of several variables. Ordinary derivative operators use $\mathrm{d}$, as in $\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}x$. Partial derivative operators use $\partial$, as in $\partial/\partial x$.

In a function $y=f(x)$, the value of $y$ depends on the value of $x$. Thus $y$ is the dependent variable and $x$ is the independent variable. An ordinary derivative measures the change of the dependent variable with respect to the independent variable.

For two points $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$ on the graph of $y=f(x)$, the secant slope is the rise divided by the run.

$$
\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}=\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}.
$$

This ratio is the average slope over the interval. If the mean value theorem applies, it equals the tangent slope at some point between the endpoints. As the run $\Delta x$ tends to zero, the secant slope tends to the slope of the tangent line at that point. This is the derivative $\mathrm{d}y/\mathrm{d}x$.

For a function $z=f(x,y)$, the graph is a surface above the $xy$-plane, as in Fig. 2.7. Its height can change at different rates in different directions. At the point shown in Fig. 2.8, the slope is steep when $y$ increases with $x$ fixed, and nearly zero when $x$ increases with $y$ fixed.

A partial derivative measures the rate of change as one independent variable changes and the others stay fixed. For $z=f(x,y)$, $\partial z/\partial x$ is the slope along the $x$-direction and $\partial z/\partial y$ is the slope along the $y$-direction. The notation $\left.\partial z/\partial x\right|_y$ or $\left.\partial z/\partial y\right|_x$ identifies the variable held fixed.

If only $x$ changes, $\mathrm{d}z=(\partial z/\partial x)\,\mathrm{d}x$. If only $y$ changes, $\mathrm{d}z=(\partial z/\partial y)\,\mathrm{d}y$.

If both variables change, the total differential is

$$
\mathrm{d}z=\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\,\mathrm{d}x+
             \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\,\mathrm{d}y. \tag{2.20}
$$

To calculate a partial derivative, treat the other variables as constants and differentiate as usual.

Consider $z=f(x,y)=6x^2y+3x+5xy+10$. A surface plot makes its slopes easier to compare. Along $y=-3$, the slope in the $x$-direction decreases as $x$ increases from $-3$ to $3$. It is positive at first, zero near $x=0$, and increasingly negative after that. Along $x=-3$, the slope in the $y$-direction is constant and positive.

To calculate the partial derivative with respect to $x$, hold $y$ fixed.

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=12xy+3+5y. \tag{2.21}
$$

Hold $x$ fixed to calculate the other partial derivative.

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=6x^2+5x. \tag{2.22}
$$

### The power rule from the definition

The derivative is the limit of the change in the function divided by the change in its argument.

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\mathrm{d}x}
=\lim_{\Delta x\to0}\frac{z(x+\Delta x)-z(x)}{\Delta x}. \tag{2.23}
$$

For $z=x^2$, this is

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}(x^2)}{\mathrm{d}x}
=\lim_{\Delta x\to0}\frac{(x+\Delta x)^2-x^2}{\Delta x}. \tag{2.24}
$$

In the numerator, the terms $x^2$ cancel and leave $2x\Delta x+(\Delta x)^2$. Division by $\Delta x$ gives $2x+\Delta x$, which tends to $2x$. For a positive integer power $n$, the expansion of $(x+\Delta x)^n$ gives $n$ terms of the form $x^{n-1}\Delta x$. After division by $\Delta x$, higher powers of $\Delta x$ tend to zero. Thus $\mathrm{d}(x^3)/\mathrm{d}x=3x^2$, and in general $\mathrm{d}(x^n)/\mathrm{d}x=nx^{n-1}$.

### Slopes of the surface

At $(x,y)=(-3,2)$, Equation (2.21) gives $12(-3)(2)+3+5(2)=-59$. Equation (2.22) gives $6(-3)^2+5(-3)=39$. The slopes differ because they refer to different directions.

At $y=-3$, the $x$-slope is $-36x-12$. It decreases from $96$ at $x=-3$ to $0$ at $x=-1/3$ and to $-120$ at $x=3$. At $x=-3$, the $y$-slope is $39$ everywhere along that edge. Both results agree with Fig. 2.9.

### Higher-order partial derivatives

Differentiate a first derivative to obtain a second derivative. For example,

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\right)
=\frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x^2},
\qquad
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\right)
=\frac{\partial^2 z}{\partial y^2}.
$$

The second partial derivative gives the rate of change of the first slope. In this example, $\partial^2z/\partial x^2=12y$. Squaring the first derivative gives $(\partial z/\partial x)^2=(12xy+3+5y)^2$. The superscript immediately after $\mathrm{d}$ or $\partial$ specifies the derivative order.

A mixed partial derivative differentiates first in one direction, then in another. For example, $\partial_x(\partial_y z)=\partial^2z/(\partial x\,\partial y)$ gives the change in the $y$-slope as $x$ changes. For functions with continuous second partial derivatives, the two orders of differentiation give the same result.[^5] In this example, both orders give $12x+5$.

### The chain rule

Suppose $z=f(x,y)$ and both $x$ and $y$ depend on $u$ and $v$. The partial derivatives with respect to $u$ and $v$ are

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial u}
=\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial u}
+\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial u}, \tag{2.25}
$$

and

$$
\frac{\partial z}{\partial v}
=\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial v}
+\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial v}. \tag{2.26}
$$

A change in $u$ can change $z$ through its effect on $x$ and through its effect on $y$. Each term computes one contribution. The same argument applies to $v$.

[^5]: The source uses the term *well-behaved* for a function with continuous derivatives. Here, continuity of the second partial derivatives is sufficient.
