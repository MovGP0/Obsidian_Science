---
title: "4.1 Coordinate-system transformations"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Changing coordinates changes the components that represent a vector while preserving the vector itself.

A change of coordinates assigns different components to the same vector. For example, a measurement in inches gives different numerical dimensions for a room than a measurement in meters. Tilting the axes changes which directions you call height and width. Translating the origin changes the position coordinates of points in the room. In each case the physical room is invariant.

In Fig. 4.1, the origin stays fixed while both Cartesian axes rotate counterclockwise by $\theta$. The dashed axes $x'$ and $y'$ denote the rotated coordinates.

Fig. 4.2 shows $\vec A$ in the two systems. The length and direction of $\vec A$ are fixed. In the example, $A'_x>A_x$ and $A'_y<A_y$. If you continue rotating the axes until $\vec A$ lies along positive $x'$, then $A'_y=0$ and $A'_x=|\vec A|$.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-4-1.png|**Fig. 4.1** A two-dimensional Cartesian coordinate system rotated counterclockwise by θ.]]

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-4-2.png|**Fig. 4.2** Components of the same vector along the original axes (a) and rotated axes (b).]]

You can find the new components by geometry or by taking dot products with the new unit vectors. The [[4.10 Chapter 4 problems|Chapter 4 problems]] cover the dot-product method.

The component $A'_x$ depends on both $A_x$ and $A_y$. The new $x'$-axis has a component along the old $y$-axis, so the part of $\vec A$ along the old $y$-axis contributes to $A'_x$ as well as to $A'_y$.

In Fig. 4.3(a), the original $x$-component contributes a segment $\ell_1$ along the rotated $x'$-axis. In Fig. 4.3(b), the original $y$-component contributes a segment $\ell_2$ along the same axis. The new component is their sum.

$$
A'_x=\ell_1+\ell_2. \tag{4.1}
$$

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-4-3.png|**Fig. 4.3** Contributions of the original x-component (a) and y-component (b) to the rotated x-component.]]

Let $\alpha_{11}$ be the angle between $x'$ and $x$, and let $\alpha_{12}$ be the angle between $x'$ and $y$. The right triangles in Fig. 4.3 show that the signed projections onto $x'$ are

$$
\ell_1=A_x\cos\alpha_{11}, \tag{4.2}
$$

and

$$
\ell_2=A_y\cos\alpha_{12}. \tag{4.3}
$$

Adding the projections gives a weighted linear combination of the original components.

$$
A'_x=A_x\cos\alpha_{11}+A_y\cos\alpha_{12}. \tag{4.4}
$$

Similarly, if $\alpha_{21}$ is the angle between $y'$ and $x$, and $\alpha_{22}$ is the angle between $y'$ and $y$, then

$$
A'_y=A_x\cos\alpha_{21}+A_y\cos\alpha_{22}. \tag{4.5}
$$

Write the two equations as a matrix product.[^1]

$$
\begin{pmatrix}A'_x\\A'_y\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\alpha_{11}&\cos\alpha_{12}\\
\cos\alpha_{21}&\cos\alpha_{22}
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}A_x\\A_y\end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.6}
$$

The entries of the transformation matrix are the *direction cosines*. For a counterclockwise rotation of the axes through $\theta$, the diagonal angles are $\theta$, and the off-diagonal angles are $90^\circ-\theta$ and $90^\circ+\theta$.

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\theta&\cos(90^\circ-\theta)\\
\cos(90^\circ+\theta)&\cos\theta
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\
-\sin\theta&\cos\theta
\end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.7}
$$

Consider the vector

$$
\vec A=5\hat i+3\hat j. \tag{4.8}
$$

Rotate the axes counterclockwise by $150^\circ$. From Fig. 4.4(b), both components are negative in the rotated system, and the magnitude of the $y'$-component is larger than that of the $x'$-component.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-4-4.png|**Fig. 4.4** Components before (a) and after (b) a 150° counterclockwise rotation of the Cartesian axes.]]

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-4-5.png|**Fig. 4.5** Angles between the original and rotated Cartesian axes.]]

For $\theta=150^\circ$, Fig. 4.5 gives $\alpha_{11}=150^\circ$, $\alpha_{12}=60^\circ$, $\alpha_{21}=240^\circ$, and $\alpha_{22}=150^\circ$. Equation (4.6) becomes

$$
\begin{pmatrix}A'_x\\A'_y\end{pmatrix}=
\begin{pmatrix}\cos150^\circ&\cos60^\circ\\ \cos240^\circ&\cos150^\circ\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}A_x\\A_y\end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.9}
$$

Hence

$$
A'_x=5\cos150^\circ+3\cos60^\circ\approx-2.8, \tag{4.10}
$$

and

$$
A'_y=5\cos240^\circ+3\cos150^\circ\approx-5.1. \tag{4.11}
$$

The calculation agrees with the signs and relative magnitudes in Fig. 4.4(b). Equation (4.6) gives the components of the original vector in the rotated basis. It also gives the new components of the original unit vectors if you substitute $(1,0)$ or $(0,1)$.

Let $M$ denote the matrix in Equation (4.9). The original basis vector $\hat i$ has the following components in the rotated system.

$$
M\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}\cos150^\circ\\\cos240^\circ\end{pmatrix}
\approx\begin{pmatrix}-0.866\\-0.5\end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.12}
$$

For the original basis vector $\hat j$, the calculation gives

$$
M\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}\cos60^\circ\\\cos150^\circ\end{pmatrix}
\approx\begin{pmatrix}0.5\\-0.866\end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.13}
$$

Fig. 4.6 shows the original unit vectors expressed in the rotated basis. The unit vectors $\hat i'$ and $\hat j'$ along the rotated axes have components $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ in that basis.

A rotation is one example of a coordinate transformation. Any linear component transformation represents the new components as weighted sums of the original ones and can be written as a matrix product. When the matrix expresses the same vector in a different basis, the source calls this a *passive* or *inverse* transformation. See [[4.3 Basis-vector vs. component transformations]].

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-4-6.png|**Fig. 4.6** Components of the original Cartesian unit vectors in the rotated coordinate system.]]

In general, the passive transformation has the form

$$
[\vec A]_{\mathrm{new}}=M_{\mathrm{passive}}[\vec A]_{\mathrm{original}}. \tag{4.14}
$$

The next article, [[4.2 Basis-vector transformations]], gives the new unit vectors in terms of the original unit vectors.

[^1]: The source points to a review of matrix notation and algebra on the book’s website.
