---
title: "4.7 Index notation"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Indices label components in any dimension and let a repeated index denote a sum.

Use indices $1,2,3,\ldots$ for coordinate directions. This notation extends beyond three dimensions and makes repeated sums easier to write.

In three dimensions, write the coordinates as $(x_1,x_2,x_3)$ or $(x^1,x^2,x^3)$ according to the convention. Write the covariant components as $(A_1,A_2,A_3)$ and the contravariant components as $(A^1,A^2,A^3)$. In $N$ dimensions the indices run from $1$ to $N$.

For a rotation of two Cartesian axes, [[4.1 Coordinate-system transformations|Equation (4.6)]] becomes

$$
\begin{pmatrix}A^{\prime 1}\\A^{\prime 2}\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\alpha_{11}&\cos\alpha_{12}\\
\cos\alpha_{21}&\cos\alpha_{22}
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}A^1\\A^2\end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.43}
$$

In three dimensions,

$$
\begin{pmatrix}A^{\prime 1}\\A^{\prime 2}\\A^{\prime 3}\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\alpha_{11}&\cos\alpha_{12}&\cos\alpha_{13}\\
\cos\alpha_{21}&\cos\alpha_{22}&\cos\alpha_{23}\\
\cos\alpha_{31}&\cos\alpha_{32}&\cos\alpha_{33}
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}A^1\\A^2\\A^3\end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.44}
$$

Write the matrix entries as $a_{ij}=\cos\alpha_{ij}$. In this matrix notation, the first subscript serves as the row label.

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^{\prime 1}&=a_{11}A^1+a_{12}A^2+a_{13}A^3,\\
A^{\prime 2}&=a_{21}A^1+a_{22}A^2+a_{23}A^3,\\
A^{\prime 3}&=a_{31}A^1+a_{32}A^2+a_{33}A^3.
\end{aligned} \tag{4.45}
$$

Use a sum for each row.

$$
A^{\prime 1}=\sum_{j=1}^3 a_{1j}A^j,\qquad
A^{\prime 2}=\sum_{j=1}^3 a_{2j}A^j,\qquad
A^{\prime 3}=\sum_{j=1}^3 a_{3j}A^j. \tag{4.46}
$$

A single equation now describes the three rows.

$$
A^{\prime i}=\sum_{j=1}^3 a_{ij}A^j,\qquad i=1,2,3. \tag{4.47}
$$

Under the *Einstein summation convention*, an index that appears once above and once below in a term is summed over its full range.

$$
A^{\prime i}=a_{ij}A^j. \tag{4.48}
$$

The repeated index $j$ is a *dummy index*. You can replace it with any unused index without changing the sum.[^8] In tensor notation, the same matrix entries are written $a^i{}_j$, which places the free index $i$ in the same position on both sides. Albert Einstein introduced the summation convention in 1916 and jokingly called it his “great discovery in mathematics.”[^9]

Each component in the new system is a weighted sum of the original components. The transformation matrix entries are the weights. See [[4.8 Quantities that transform contravariantly]] for their form in a general coordinate change.

[^8]: The index $i$ appears once in each term and is free. It is not summed.
[^9]: A. Pais, *Subtle Is the Lord: The Science and the Life of Albert Einstein*, Oxford University Press, 1983.
