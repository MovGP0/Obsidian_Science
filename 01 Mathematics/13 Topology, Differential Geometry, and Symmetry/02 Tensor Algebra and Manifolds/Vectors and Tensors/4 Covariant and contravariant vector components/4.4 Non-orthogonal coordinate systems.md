---
title: "4.4 Non-orthogonal coordinate systems"
---

> [!TLDR]
> In non-orthogonal coordinates, perpendicular and parallel projections give different component descriptions.

In a Cartesian coordinate system, projecting perpendicular to the $x$-axis is the same as projecting parallel to the $y$-axis. In the shadow analogy from [[1.3 Vector components|Fig. 1.6(a)]], the light travels along the negative $y$-direction and casts a shadow on the $x$-axis. These two descriptions are equivalent because the axes are perpendicular.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-4-10.png|**Fig. 4.10** Projections onto one axis along light rays parallel to the other axis.]]

Now let the axes be nonperpendicular.[^4] Project $\vec A$ onto one axis either along the other axis, as in Fig. 4.10, or perpendicular to the receiving axis, as in Fig. 4.11. The two shadows generally have different lengths.

Fig. 4.12 compares the vector sums obtained from the two sets of projections. Projections along the other axis reconstruct $\vec A$ when added as vectors along the original axes. Perpendicular projections require a different basis for reconstruction.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-4-11.png|**Fig. 4.11** Projections onto each axis along light rays perpendicular to that axis.]]

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-4-12.png|**Fig. 4.12** Addition of the parallel-projection components (a) and perpendicular-projection components (b) along the original axes.]]

The two kinds of components obey different transformation laws. The coefficients from projections parallel to the other axes are *contravariant components*. They transform by the inverse of the basis change. The dot products with the individual basis vectors are *covariant components*. They transform along with the basis vectors. For unit basis vectors, the dot products are the signed lengths of perpendicular projections. In an orthonormal basis, the two sets of components coincide.

[[4.5 Dual basis vectors]] explains how to reconstruct a vector from its covariant components.

[^4]: Non-orthogonal coordinate axes occur in relativity, fluid dynamics, and other applications.
