---
title: "4.8 Quantities that transform contravariantly"
---

> [!TLDR]
> An infinitesimal displacement transforms with the Jacobian from the original coordinates to the new coordinates.

An infinitesimal displacement is a standard example of a quantity with contravariant components. A coordinate transformation can be nonlinear. For example, the spherical-to-Cartesian relation $x=r\sin\theta\cos\phi$ is nonlinear in $(r,\theta,\phi)$. The differentials at a point nevertheless obey a linear relation with coefficients given by partial derivatives.

$$
\mathrm dx=a_{11}\mathrm dr+a_{12}\mathrm d\theta+a_{13}\mathrm d\phi, \tag{4.49}
$$

with analogous equations for $\mathrm dy$ and $\mathrm dz$.

For general coordinates $(x,y,z)=(x^1,x^2,x^3)$ and $(x',y',z')=(x^{\prime 1},x^{\prime 2},x^{\prime 3})$, apply the chain rule.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm dx'&=\frac{\partial x'}{\partial x}\mathrm dx+
             \frac{\partial x'}{\partial y}\mathrm dy+
             \frac{\partial x'}{\partial z}\mathrm dz,\\
\mathrm dy'&=\frac{\partial y'}{\partial x}\mathrm dx+
             \frac{\partial y'}{\partial y}\mathrm dy+
             \frac{\partial y'}{\partial z}\mathrm dz,\\
\mathrm dz'&=\frac{\partial z'}{\partial x}\mathrm dx+
             \frac{\partial z'}{\partial y}\mathrm dy+
             \frac{\partial z'}{\partial z}\mathrm dz.
\end{aligned} \tag{4.50}
$$

The row for coordinate $x^{\prime i}$ uses the entries $\partial x^{\prime i}/\partial x^j$.

With the indexed coordinate labels,[^10] Equation (4.50) has the matrix form

$$
\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm dx^{\prime 1}\\\mathrm dx^{\prime 2}\\\mathrm dx^{\prime 3}\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial x^{\prime 1}}{\partial x^1}&\dfrac{\partial x^{\prime 1}}{\partial x^2}&\dfrac{\partial x^{\prime 1}}{\partial x^3}\\
\dfrac{\partial x^{\prime 2}}{\partial x^1}&\dfrac{\partial x^{\prime 2}}{\partial x^2}&\dfrac{\partial x^{\prime 2}}{\partial x^3}\\
\dfrac{\partial x^{\prime 3}}{\partial x^1}&\dfrac{\partial x^{\prime 3}}{\partial x^2}&\dfrac{\partial x^{\prime 3}}{\partial x^3}
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm dx^1\\\mathrm dx^2\\\mathrm dx^3\end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.51}
$$

Each row is a sum over $j$. The index $i$ selects the row.

$$
\mathrm dx^{\prime i}=\sum_{j=1}^3\frac{\partial x^{\prime i}}{\partial x^j}\mathrm dx^j. \tag{4.52}
$$

Omit the summation sign under the Einstein convention.

$$
\mathrm dx^{\prime i}=\frac{\partial x^{\prime i}}{\partial x^j}\mathrm dx^j. \tag{4.53}
$$

Compare this with the vector component rule $A^{\prime i}=a^i{}_jA^j$ from [[4.7 Index notation]].

The coefficients in Equation (4.53) are the entries of the Jacobian from the original coordinates to the new ones. Column $j$ also gives the components of the original coordinate basis vector $\vec e_j$ in the new basis.[^11] Combining an original basis vector with an upper-index component preserves the geometric vector. The differentials $\mathrm dx^i$ are the standard example of components that transform this way.

A vector with contravariant components $A^i$ follows the same rule.

$$
A^{\prime i}=\frac{\partial x^{\prime i}}{\partial x^j}A^j. \tag{4.54}
$$

For example, take $(x^1,x^2)=(r,\theta)$ and $(x^{\prime 1},x^{\prime 2})=(x,y)$. Differentiate $x=r\cos\theta$ and $y=r\sin\theta$.

$$
\frac{\partial x}{\partial r}=\cos\theta,\qquad
\frac{\partial y}{\partial r}=\sin\theta. \tag{4.55}
$$

$$
\frac{\partial x}{\partial\theta}=-r\sin\theta,\qquad
\frac{\partial y}{\partial\theta}=r\cos\theta. \tag{4.56}
$$

The first column gives the radial coordinate basis vector expressed in Cartesian unit vectors.

$$
\vec e_1=\frac{\partial x}{\partial r}\hat i+\frac{\partial y}{\partial r}\hat j
=\cos\theta\,\hat i+\sin\theta\,\hat j. \tag{4.57}
$$

The second column gives the angular coordinate basis vector.

$$
\vec e_2=\frac{\partial x}{\partial\theta}\hat i+\frac{\partial y}{\partial\theta}\hat j
=-r\sin\theta\,\hat i+r\cos\theta\,\hat j. \tag{4.58}
$$

The radial vector points outward along $\hat r$, while the angular vector points along $\hat\theta$ for $r>0$.[^12] This confirms that the Jacobian columns give the original coordinate basis vectors in the new coordinates.

[^10]: The differential components use upper indices because they transform contravariantly.
[^11]: See [[2.6 Vectors as derivatives]] for coordinate basis vectors represented by differential operators.
[^12]: See [[1.5 Non-Cartesian unit vectors]] for the directions in polar coordinates.
