---
title: "4.6 Finding covariant and contravariant components"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Calculate contravariant components with the reciprocal basis and covariant components with the original basis.

Fig. 4.15 shows $\vec A$ and the non-orthogonal basis $\vec e_1,\vec e_2$. Parallel projections in Fig. 4.16 give a first estimate of the contravariant coefficients. The component along $\vec e_1$ has about two thirds of its length. The component along $\vec e_2$ has about one and a half times its length. Solve the vector equation in the next step to calculate the coefficients.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-4-15.png|**Fig. 4.15** The example vector and its two non-orthogonal basis vectors.]]

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-4-16.png|**Fig. 4.16** Parallel projections onto the original basis directions.]]

The expansion in the original basis is

$$
\vec A=A^1\vec e_1+A^2\vec e_2. \tag{4.26}
$$

Equate the Cartesian components on both sides.

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_x&=A^1 e_{1,x}+A^2 e_{2,x},\\
A_y&=A^1 e_{1,y}+A^2 e_{2,y}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Use elimination, substitution, or a matrix method. Substituting the Cartesian components gives

$$
\begin{pmatrix}7\\2\end{pmatrix}
=A^1\begin{pmatrix}1\\3\end{pmatrix}+A^2\begin{pmatrix}4\\0\end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.27}
$$

In matrix form,

$$
\begin{pmatrix}7\\2\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}1&4\\3&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A^1\\A^2\end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.28}
$$

Cramer's rule gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^1&=\frac{\begin{vmatrix}7&4\\2&0\end{vmatrix}}{\begin{vmatrix}1&4\\3&0\end{vmatrix}}
=\frac{-8}{-12}\approx0.667,\\
A^2&=\frac{\begin{vmatrix}1&7\\3&2\end{vmatrix}}{\begin{vmatrix}1&4\\3&0\end{vmatrix}}
=\frac{-19}{-12}\approx1.583.
\end{aligned} \tag{4.29}
$$

These values agree with the estimates from Fig. 4.16.

To use the same method for the covariant components, first find the reciprocal basis. Its first vector is perpendicular to $\vec e_2$, and its second is perpendicular to $\vec e_1$. The lengths of the original vectors are

$$
|\vec e_1|=\sqrt{1^2+3^2}\approx3.16,\qquad
|\vec e_2|=\sqrt{4^2+0^2}=4.00. \tag{4.30}
$$

Fig. 4.17 shows that the angles between corresponding original and reciprocal vectors are $\theta_1=\theta_2=\arctan(1/3)\approx18.43^\circ$.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-4-17.png|**Fig. 4.17** Perpendicular projections and the directions of the reciprocal basis vectors.]]

The reciprocal basis has lengths

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\vec e^{\,1}|&=\frac{1}{|\vec e_1|\cos\theta_1}
\approx\frac{1}{3.16\cos18.43^\circ}\approx0.333,\\
|\vec e^{\,2}|&=\frac{1}{|\vec e_2|\cos\theta_2}
\approx\frac{1}{4.00\cos18.43^\circ}\approx0.264.
\end{aligned} \tag{4.31}
$$

To find $A_1$ and $A_2$ geometrically, first take perpendicular projections onto the original axes. Then extend the projection lines to the corresponding reciprocal directions as in Fig. 4.17. The magnitude of the example vector is

$$
|\vec A|=\sqrt{7^2+2^2}\approx7.28. \tag{4.32}
$$

Its direction is $\arctan(2/7)\approx15.94^\circ$ from the positive $x$-axis. It makes an angle of approximately $55.62^\circ$ with $\vec e_1$ and $15.94^\circ$ with $\vec e_2$. Thus the projection onto the direction of $\vec e_1$ has length

$$
\ell_1=|\vec A|\cos55.62^\circ\approx4.11. \tag{4.33}
$$

Convert the first projection to a length along the corresponding reciprocal direction.

$$
A_1|\vec e^{\,1}|=\frac{\ell_1}{\cos18.43^\circ}\approx4.33. \tag{4.34}
$$

Divide by $|\vec e^{\,1}|\approx0.333$ to obtain $A_1\approx13.0$. For the second component,

$$
\ell_2=|\vec A|\cos15.94^\circ\approx7.00, \tag{4.35}
$$

and

$$
A_2|\vec e^{\,2}|=\frac{\ell_2}{\cos18.43^\circ}\approx7.38. \tag{4.36}
$$

Divide by $|\vec e^{\,2}|\approx0.264$ to obtain $A_2\approx28.0$. The geometric segment gives the component multiplied by the basis-vector magnitude. Divide by that magnitude to obtain the component itself.

For an algebraic calculation, start with

$$
\vec A=A_1\vec e^{\,1}+A_2\vec e^{\,2}. \tag{4.37}
$$

The vector $\vec e^{\,1}$ points at $90^\circ$ and has exact components $(0,1/3)$. The vector $\vec e^{\,2}$ points at approximately $360^\circ-18.43^\circ$ and has exact components $(1/4,-1/12)$. Thus

$$
\begin{pmatrix}7\\2\end{pmatrix}
=A_1\begin{pmatrix}0\\1/3\end{pmatrix}
+A_2\begin{pmatrix}1/4\\-1/12\end{pmatrix}, \tag{4.38}
$$

or

$$
\begin{pmatrix}7\\2\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}0&1/4\\1/3&-1/12\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}A_1\\A_2\end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.39}
$$

Using the exact matrix entries, Cramer's rule gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_1&=\frac{\begin{vmatrix}7&1/4\\2&-1/12\end{vmatrix}}
{\begin{vmatrix}0&1/4\\1/3&-1/12\end{vmatrix}}
=\frac{-13/12}{-1/12}=13,\\
A_2&=\frac{\begin{vmatrix}0&7\\1/3&2\end{vmatrix}}
{\begin{vmatrix}0&1/4\\1/3&-1/12\end{vmatrix}}
=\frac{-7/3}{-1/12}=28.
\end{aligned} \tag{4.40}
$$

When both bases are known, dot products give the components directly.

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_1&=\vec A\cdot\vec e_1=A_xe_{1,x}+A_ye_{1,y},\\
A_2&=\vec A\cdot\vec e_2=A_xe_{2,x}+A_ye_{2,y},
\end{aligned} \tag{4.41}
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^1&=\vec A\cdot\vec e^{\,1}=A_xe^1_x+A_ye^1_y,\\
A^2&=\vec A\cdot\vec e^{\,2}=A_xe^2_x+A_ye^2_y.
\end{aligned} \tag{4.42}
$$

In the example,

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_1&=(7,2)\cdot(1,3)=13,\\
A_2&=(7,2)\cdot(4,0)=28,\\
A^1&=(7,2)\cdot(0,1/3)=2/3,\\
A^2&=(7,2)\cdot(1/4,-1/12)=19/12.
\end{aligned}
$$

Both sets of components describe the same vector when paired with their corresponding basis.

The terms *covariant* and *contravariant* describe the two sets of components used in this calculation.[^7] In a space with a metric, you can calculate either set for a vector. The same component transformation rules also apply to rank-one tensors. [[4.7 Index notation]] introduces a compact way to write the rules.

[^7]: See [[5.6 Index raising and lowering]] for conversion between the two sets of components with the metric.
