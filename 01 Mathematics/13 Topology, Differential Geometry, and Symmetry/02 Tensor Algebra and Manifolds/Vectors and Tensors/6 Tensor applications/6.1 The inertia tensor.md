---
title: "6.1 The inertia tensor"
---

> [!TLDR]
> The inertia tensor relates angular velocity to angular momentum in three dimensions.

Mass measures the resistance of an object to linear acceleration. Moment of inertia plays a similar role in rotation, where torque changes angular velocity.

The quantities for linear motion are position $\mechanical{x}$, velocity $\mechanical{\vec v}$, and acceleration $\mechanical{\vec a}$. The rotational quantities are angle $\mechanical{\theta}$, angular velocity $\mechanical{\vec\omega}$, and angular acceleration $\mechanical{\vec\alpha}$. Force $\mechanical{\vec F}$, mass $\mechanical{m}$, and momentum $\mechanical{\vec p}$ correspond to torque $\mechanical{\vec\tau}$, moment of inertia $\mechanical{I}$, and angular momentum $\mechanical{\vec L}$.

For fixed-axis rotation, the relation $\mechanical{\tau}_z=\mechanical{I}\mechanical{\alpha}_z$ parallels $\mechanical{\vec F}=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{\vec a}$.[^1] Likewise, $\mechanical{L}_z=\mechanical{I}\mechanical{\omega}_z$ parallels $\mechanical{\vec p}=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{\vec v}$.

A particle moving in the $xy$-plane about the $z$-axis has $\mechanical{v}=\mechanical{\omega}\mechanical{r}$ in magnitude and $\mechanical{L}_z=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{v}\mechanical{r}=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{r}^2\mechanical{\omega}$ when the rotation is positive. The moment of inertia is $\mechanical{I}=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{r}^2$. This scalar relation describes one component of angular momentum. In three dimensions, use the [[2.2 Cross product|cross product]] for the rotational velocity.

$$
\mechanical{\vec v}=\mechanical{\vec\omega}\times\mechanical{\vec r}. \tag{6.1}
$$

Angular momentum about the origin is

$$
\mechanical{\vec L}
=\mechanical{\vec r}\times\mechanical{\vec p}
=\mechanical{\vec r}\times(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{\vec v})
=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{\vec r}\times\mechanical{\vec v}. \tag{6.2}
$$

The moment of inertia $\mechanical{m}\mechanical{r}^2$ of a particle in planar rotation depends on its mass and its distance from the rotation axis. For an extended body the moment of inertia depends on how its mass is distributed.

Fig. 6.1 shows a particle rotating about the $z$-axis in a plane above the origin. The angular velocity points upward. At the left position, the velocity points out of the page and the [[2.2 Cross product|right-hand rule]] gives angular momentum up and to the right. After half a revolution, the velocity points into the page and the angular momentum points up and to the left. A single scalar multiplier of $\mechanical{\vec\omega}$ could not reproduce these angular momentum directions.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-6-1.png|**Fig. 6.1** Angular momentum about the origin at two positions of a particle orbiting the z-axis.]]

In Fig. 6.1, the angular momentum direction changes while the angular velocity stays along the rotation axis. The inertia tensor $\mechanical{\mathbf I}$ maps the angular velocity to the angular momentum.

$$
\mechanical{\vec L}=\mechanical{\mathbf I}\,\mechanical{\vec\omega}. \tag{6.3}
$$

If you add a second particle of equal mass at the same height and opposite position around the axis, their transverse angular momenta cancel. The total angular momentum then points along the rotation axis. This illustrates the link between the symmetry of the mass distribution and the inertia tensor.

Substitute Equation (6.1) into Equation (6.2) and use the [[2.4 Triple vector product|triple vector product]].

$$
\mechanical{\vec L}
=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{\vec r}\times(\mechanical{\vec\omega}\times\mechanical{\vec r})
=\mechanical{m}\left[
\mechanical{\vec\omega}(\mechanical{\vec r}\cdot\mechanical{\vec r})
-\mechanical{\vec r}(\mechanical{\vec r}\cdot\mechanical{\vec\omega})\right].
$$

For a rigid body with mass elements $\mechanical{m}_i$, sum the contributions.

$$
\mechanical{\vec L}
=\sum_i \mechanical{m}_i\left[
\mechanical{\vec\omega}(\mechanical{\vec r}_i\cdot\mechanical{\vec r}_i)
-\mechanical{\vec r}_i(\mechanical{\vec r}_i\cdot\mechanical{\vec\omega})\right]. \tag{6.4}
$$

For continuous mass, replace the sum by an integral over mass. In Cartesian components, $\mechanical{\vec r}_i=\mechanical{x}_i\hat i+\mechanical{y}_i\hat j+\mechanical{z}_i\hat k$ and $\mechanical{\vec\omega}=\mechanical{\omega}_x\hat i+\mechanical{\omega}_y\hat j+\mechanical{\omega}_z\hat k$. The angular velocity is the same for every element of a rigid body.

Evaluate the two dot products in Equation (6.4).

$$
\mechanical{\vec L}=\sum_i\mechanical{m}_i
\left[\mechanical{\vec\omega}(\mechanical{x}_i^2+\mechanical{y}_i^2+\mechanical{z}_i^2)
-\mechanical{\vec r}_i(\mechanical{x}_i\mechanical{\omega}_x+
\mechanical{y}_i\mechanical{\omega}_y+\mechanical{z}_i\mechanical{\omega}_z)\right].
$$

In the $x$-component, the terms $\mechanical{\omega}_x\mechanical{x}_i^2$ cancel. Collect the remaining terms in each direction.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{L}_x&=\sum_i\mechanical{m}_i\left[
\mechanical{\omega}_x(\mechanical{y}_i^2+\mechanical{z}_i^2)
-\mechanical{x}_i\mechanical{y}_i\mechanical{\omega}_y
-\mechanical{x}_i\mechanical{z}_i\mechanical{\omega}_z\right],\\
\mechanical{L}_y&=\sum_i\mechanical{m}_i\left[
\mechanical{\omega}_y(\mechanical{x}_i^2+\mechanical{z}_i^2)
-\mechanical{y}_i\mechanical{x}_i\mechanical{\omega}_x
-\mechanical{y}_i\mechanical{z}_i\mechanical{\omega}_z\right],\\
\mechanical{L}_z&=\sum_i\mechanical{m}_i\left[
\mechanical{\omega}_z(\mechanical{x}_i^2+\mechanical{y}_i^2)
-\mechanical{z}_i\mechanical{x}_i\mechanical{\omega}_x
-\mechanical{z}_i\mechanical{y}_i\mechanical{\omega}_y\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

Write the three equations as one matrix equation.

$$
\begin{pmatrix}\mechanical{L}_x\\\mechanical{L}_y\\\mechanical{L}_z\end{pmatrix}
=\sum_i\mechanical{m}_i
\begin{pmatrix}
\mechanical{y}_i^2+\mechanical{z}_i^2 & -\mechanical{x}_i\mechanical{y}_i & -\mechanical{x}_i\mechanical{z}_i\\
-\mechanical{y}_i\mechanical{x}_i & \mechanical{x}_i^2+\mechanical{z}_i^2 & -\mechanical{y}_i\mechanical{z}_i\\
-\mechanical{z}_i\mechanical{x}_i & -\mechanical{z}_i\mechanical{y}_i & \mechanical{x}_i^2+\mechanical{y}_i^2
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}\mechanical{\omega}_x\\\mechanical{\omega}_y\\\mechanical{\omega}_z\end{pmatrix}. \tag{6.5}
$$

The middle matrix, including the mass sum, is the inertia tensor. Each entry has units $\unit{kg\,m^2}$. Equivalently, with $a,b$ as Cartesian component indices,

$$
\mechanical{I}_{ab}=\sum_i\mechanical{m}_i
\left(\delta_{ab}|\mechanical{\vec r}_i|^2-\mechanical{r}_{i,a}\mechanical{r}_{i,b}\right).
$$

The diagonal entries are *moments of inertia*. For example, $\mechanical{I}_{xx}$ sums each mass times its squared distance from the $x$-axis. The off-diagonal entries are often called *products of inertia*. The entry $\mechanical{I}_{ab}$ gives the angular momentum along $a$ per unit angular velocity about $b$. For example, $\mechanical{I}_{yz}$ contains terms $-\mechanical{m}_i\mechanical{y}_i\mechanical{z}_i$ and describes the $y$-component produced by rotation about $z$. Symmetry in the mass distribution can make such terms cancel.

### Five point masses on a pyramid

Fig. 6.2 shows one mass at each corner of a square base and one at the apex. All have mass $\mechanical{m}$. The base side and the height are both $2\mechanical{a}$, where $\mechanical{a}$ here denotes a length. Substitute the five positions into Equation (6.5).

For the five equal masses, the first diagonal component is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{I}_{xx}
&=\mechanical{m}_1(\mechanical{y}_1^2+\mechanical{z}_1^2)
+\mechanical{m}_2(\mechanical{y}_2^2+\mechanical{z}_2^2)
+\mechanical{m}_3(\mechanical{y}_3^2+\mechanical{z}_3^2)\\
&\quad+\mechanical{m}_4(\mechanical{y}_4^2+\mechanical{z}_4^2)
+\mechanical{m}_5(\mechanical{y}_5^2+\mechanical{z}_5^2)\\
&=\mechanical{m}_1(\mechanical{a}^2+0^2)+\mechanical{m}_2(\mechanical{a}^2+0^2)
+\mechanical{m}_3[(-\mechanical{a})^2+0^2]\\
&\quad+\mechanical{m}_4[(-\mechanical{a})^2+0^2]+\mechanical{m}_5[0^2+(2\mechanical{a})^2]\\
&=8\mechanical{m}\mechanical{a}^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-6-2.png|**Fig. 6.2** Five point masses at the vertices of a square pyramid.]]

The other diagonal elements, $\mechanical{I}_{yy}$ and $\mechanical{I}_{zz}$, have the same value. For the off-diagonal terms, start with $\mechanical{I}_{xy}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{I}_{xy}
&=-\mechanical{m}_1\mechanical{x}_1\mechanical{y}_1
-\mechanical{m}_2\mechanical{x}_2\mechanical{y}_2
-\mechanical{m}_3\mechanical{x}_3\mechanical{y}_3
-\mechanical{m}_4\mechanical{x}_4\mechanical{y}_4
-\mechanical{m}_5\mechanical{x}_5\mechanical{y}_5\\
&=-\mechanical{m}_1(\mechanical{a})(\mechanical{a})
-\mechanical{m}_2(-\mechanical{a})(\mechanical{a})
-\mechanical{m}_3(-\mechanical{a})(-\mechanical{a})
-\mechanical{m}_4(\mechanical{a})(-\mechanical{a})
-\mechanical{m}_5(0)(0)\\
&=-\mechanical{m}(2\mechanical{a}^2-2\mechanical{a}^2)=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

All other off-diagonal elements are also zero. The inertia tensor for Fig. 6.2 is

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf I}
=\begin{pmatrix}
8\mechanical{m}\mechanical{a}^2&0&0\\
0&8\mechanical{m}\mechanical{a}^2&0\\
0&0&8\mechanical{m}\mechanical{a}^2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The zero off-diagonal elements show that the chosen axes are **principal axes** for this object and origin. Their moments are the **principal moments**. When the object rotates about a principal axis, the angular momentum and angular velocity vectors are parallel. All three principal moments are equal, so the inertia tensor describes a **spherical top**. Any three mutually orthogonal axes through the origin are then principal axes.

Double the height of $\mechanical{m}_5$ from $2\mechanical{a}$ to $4\mechanical{a}$. Its increased distance from the $x$- and $y$-axes gives

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf I}
=\begin{pmatrix}
20\mechanical{m}\mechanical{a}^2&0&0\\
0&20\mechanical{m}\mechanical{a}^2&0\\
0&0&8\mechanical{m}\mechanical{a}^2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The fifth mass stays on the $z$-axis, so its contribution to $\mechanical{I}_{zz}$ remains zero. A top with exactly two equal principal moments is a **symmetric top**. A top with three different principal moments is an **asymmetric top**. A body with one zero principal moment and two equal nonzero moments is a **rotor**.

Alternatively, keep the original positions and double $\mechanical{m}_5$ from $\mechanical{m}$ to $2\mechanical{m}$. The resulting tensor is

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf I}
=\begin{pmatrix}
12\mechanical{m}\mechanical{a}^2&0&0\\
0&12\mechanical{m}\mechanical{a}^2&0\\
0&0&8\mechanical{m}\mechanical{a}^2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The fifth mass still contributes zero to $\mechanical{I}_{zz}$.

Rotate the coordinate axes about the $x$-axis while keeping the origin and the masses fixed. Fig. 6.3(a) shows the tilted axes when viewed down the $x$-axis toward the origin. Fig. 6.3(b) shows the same arrangement with the primed axes drawn horizontally and vertically. To compute the inertia tensor in the rotated basis, first obtain the coordinates of each mass from the rotation matrix in [[4.1 Coordinate-system transformations|Section 4.1]].

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-6-3.png|**Fig. 6.3** Coordinate axes rotated approximately 30° counterclockwise about the x-axis. Panel (b) shows the primed axes horizontal and vertical.]]

$$
\begin{pmatrix}\mechanical{x}'\\\mechanical{y}'\\\mechanical{z}'\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}
1&0&0\\
0&\cos\mechanical{\theta}&\sin\mechanical{\theta}\\
0&-\sin\mechanical{\theta}&\cos\mechanical{\theta}
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}\mechanical{x}\\\mechanical{y}\\\mechanical{z}\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{6.6}
$$

For the original five equal masses with the apex at height $2\mechanical{a}$, substitution of the rotated coordinates gives

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf I}'
=\begin{pmatrix}
8\mechanical{m}\mechanical{a}^2&0&0\\
0&8\mechanical{m}\mechanical{a}^2&0\\
0&0&8\mechanical{m}\mechanical{a}^2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Because the original object is a spherical top, any orthonormal basis gives the same inertia matrix. The individual masses can still contribute off-diagonal terms. Take $\mechanical{\theta}=30^\circ$ and first keep only $\mechanical{m}_5$. Its contribution is

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf I}'_5
=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{a}^2
\begin{pmatrix}
4&0&0\\
0&3&-\sqrt{3}\\
0&-\sqrt{3}&1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The entry $\mechanical{I}'_{yz}\approx-1.73\mechanical{m}\mechanical{a}^2$ determines the $y'$-component of angular momentum due to rotation about $z'$. If instead the four base masses are present and the apex mass is omitted, the result is

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf I}'_{\text{base}}
=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{a}^2
\begin{pmatrix}
4&0&0\\
0&5&\sqrt{3}\\
0&\sqrt{3}&7
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-6-4.png|**Fig. 6.4** Angular momentum contributions from masses 1, 4, and 5 during rotation about the z′-axis.]]

The four base masses contribute as much angular momentum along the positive $y'$-direction as the apex mass contributes along the negative $y'$-direction. Fig. 6.4 illustrates this cancellation. Add the two inertia tensors component by component to recover the diagonal tensor for the five masses.

Double the apex height again and rotate the axes by 30° about $x$. The result is

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf I}'
\approx\mechanical{m}\mechanical{a}^2
\begin{pmatrix}
20&0&0\\
0&17&-5.2\\
0&-5.2&11
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The off-diagonal contributions no longer cancel. To obtain the inertia tensor for a general orientation, combine rotations about multiple axes. With the sign convention used below, first rotate the axes about $x$ by $\mechanical{\theta}_1$, then apply the $y$ rotation through $\mechanical{\theta}_2$.

$$
\begin{pmatrix}\mechanical{x}'\\\mechanical{y}'\\\mechanical{z}'\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\mechanical{\theta}_2&0&\sin\mechanical{\theta}_2\\
0&1&0\\
-\sin\mechanical{\theta}_2&0&\cos\mechanical{\theta}_2
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0&0\\
0&\cos\mechanical{\theta}_1&\sin\mechanical{\theta}_1\\
0&-\sin\mechanical{\theta}_1&\cos\mechanical{\theta}_1
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}\mechanical{x}\\\mechanical{y}\\\mechanical{z}\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{6.7}
$$

For two 30° rotations, the combined matrix gives

$$
\begin{pmatrix}\mechanical{x}'\\\mechanical{y}'\\\mechanical{z}'\end{pmatrix}
\approx
\begin{pmatrix}
0.866&-0.25&0.433\\
0&0.866&0.5\\
-0.5&-0.433&0.75
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}\mechanical{x}\\\mechanical{y}\\\mechanical{z}\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{6.8}
$$

For the apex at height $4\mechanical{a}$, use this matrix to transform the diagonal tensor with entries $(20,20,8)\mechanical{m}\mechanical{a}^2$. With the rotation matrix in Equation (6.8), the resulting inertia tensor is

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf I}'
\approx\mechanical{m}\mechanical{a}^2
\begin{pmatrix}
17.8&-2.6&-3.9\\
-2.6&17&-4.5\\
-3.9&-4.5&13.3
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{6.9}
$$

Check the calculation by comparing the trace and determinant with those of the original matrix. A coordinate rotation preserves both.[^2]

You can also transform the inertia matrix directly. In the original basis, $\mechanical{\vec L}=\mechanical{\mathbf I}\mechanical{\vec\omega}$. If $R$ maps the original vector components to the rotated components, insert the identity $R^{-1}R$ before the angular velocity.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{\vec L}'&=R\mechanical{\vec L}
=R(\mechanical{\mathbf I}\mechanical{\vec\omega})\\
&=R\mechanical{\mathbf I}(R^{-1}R)\mechanical{\vec\omega}\\
&=(R\mechanical{\mathbf I}R^{-1})R\mechanical{\vec\omega}\\
&=(R\mechanical{\mathbf I}R^{-1})\mechanical{\vec\omega}'.
\end{aligned}
$$

The **similarity transformation** $\mechanical{\mathbf I}'=R\mechanical{\mathbf I}R^{-1}$ therefore gives the inertia matrix in the rotated basis. The order of the matrix factors matters.

Using the rotation from Equation (6.7) gives the same result as substituting the rotated coordinates into Equation (6.5).

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{\mathbf I}'
&=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{a}^2\,R
\begin{pmatrix}20&0&0\\0&20&0\\0&0&8\end{pmatrix}
R^{-1}\\
&\approx\mechanical{m}\mechanical{a}^2
\begin{pmatrix}17.8&-2.6&-3.9\\-2.6&17&-4.5\\-3.9&-4.5&13.3\end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Diagonalize the inertia tensor to find the principal moments and principal axes. The eigenvalues are the principal moments, and an orthonormal set of eigenvectors gives the principal axes. When you can identify the principal axes from the symmetry of the object, you can use a similarity transformation to express the tensor in that basis. See [[6.4 Chapter 6 problems]].

[^1]: In momentum form, Newton’s second law is $\mechanical{\vec F}=d\mechanical{\vec p}/dt$. The rotational analogue is $\mechanical{\vec\tau}=d\mechanical{\vec L}/dt$ about a fixed origin in an inertial frame.
[^2]: The matrix review on the book’s website explains how to compute the trace and determinant.
