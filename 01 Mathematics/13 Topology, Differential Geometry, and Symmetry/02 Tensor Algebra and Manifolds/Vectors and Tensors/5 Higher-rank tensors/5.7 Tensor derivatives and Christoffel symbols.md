---
title: "5.7 Tensor derivatives and Christoffel symbols"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Christoffel symbols express how the coordinate basis vectors change from point to point.

In Cartesian coordinates, the basis vectors are constant, so differentiate only the components of a vector. In curvilinear coordinates, basis vectors can change in both magnitude and direction. Use the product rule to differentiate the basis vectors as well as the components.

For example, express $\vec A$ in the coordinate basis of $(x^1,x^2,x^3)$.

$$
\vec A=A^1\vec e_1+A^2\vec e_2+A^3\vec e_3.
$$

Differentiate along $x^1$.

$$
\frac{\partial\vec A}{\partial x^1}
=\frac{\partial(A^i\vec e_i)}{\partial x^1}
=\frac{\partial A^i}{\partial x^1}\vec e_i
+A^i\frac{\partial\vec e_i}{\partial x^1}.
$$

The second term accounts for the change in the basis. The other coordinate derivatives have the same form. [[5.8 Covariant differentiation]] uses this basis-change term to obtain a tensor derivative.

The derivative of a basis vector can be expanded in the basis at the point. The coefficients of this expansion are the *Christoffel symbols of the second kind*.[^5] In Euclidean space expressed in curvilinear coordinates,

$$
\frac{\partial\vec e_i}{\partial x^j}=\Gamma^k{}_{ij}\vec e_k. \tag{5.22}
$$

The lower index $i$ identifies the basis vector and $j$ gives the coordinate direction of differentiation. The upper index $k$ gives the direction of a component of the derivative. Fig. 5.1 illustrates this distinction.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-5-1.png|**Fig. 5.1** The three indices identify the output direction, the basis vector, and the derivative direction.]]

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-5-2.png|**Fig. 5.2** The derivative of the radial basis vector with respect to θ has an angular component.]]

In polar coordinates, $\Gamma^r{}_{r\theta}=0$ and $\Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta}=1/r$. By Equation (5.22),

$$
\frac{\partial\vec e_r}{\partial\theta}
=0\vec e_r+\frac1r\vec e_\theta.
$$

The metric determines these coefficients. The following derivation finds them from derivatives of the metric components.

### Derivation from the metric

Take the dot product of Equation (5.22) with $\vec e^{\,l}$ and use $\vec e_k\cdot\vec e^{\,l}=\delta^l{}_k$.

$$
\Gamma^l{}_{ij}
=\Gamma^k{}_{ij}\delta^l{}_k
=\vec e^{\,l}\cdot\partial_j\vec e_i.
$$

In a smooth coordinate basis, $\partial_j\vec e_i=\partial_i\vec e_j$ by equality of mixed derivatives. Average the two expressions and substitute $\vec e^{\,l}=g^{kl}\vec e_k$.

$$
\Gamma^l{}_{ij}=\frac{g^{kl}}2
\left(\vec e_k\cdot\partial_j\vec e_i+\vec e_k\cdot\partial_i\vec e_j\right).
$$

Add the two zero expressions

$$
\frac{g^{kl}}2(\partial_j\vec e_k-\partial_k\vec e_j)\cdot\vec e_i,
\qquad
\frac{g^{kl}}2(\partial_i\vec e_k-\partial_k\vec e_i)\cdot\vec e_j.
$$

These additions allow the six terms to be grouped as derivatives of dot products.

Using the product rule to combine the terms in pairs gives

$$
\Gamma^l{}_{ij}
=\frac{g^{kl}}2\left[
\partial_j(\vec e_k\cdot\vec e_i)
+\partial_i(\vec e_j\cdot\vec e_k)
-\partial_k(\vec e_i\cdot\vec e_j)
\right].
$$

With $g_{ij}=\vec e_i\cdot\vec e_j$, the result is

$$
\Gamma^l{}_{ij}
=\frac12 g^{kl}
\left(\frac{\partial g_{ik}}{\partial x^j}
+\frac{\partial g_{jk}}{\partial x^i}
-\frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial x^k}\right). \tag{5.23}
$$

These coefficients let a derivative account for changes in both the components and the basis. The complete covariant derivative transforms as a tensor.

### Cylindrical example

In [[1.5 Non-Cartesian unit vectors|cylindrical coordinates]] $(r,\phi,z)$,

$$
\mathrm ds^2=\mathrm dr^2+r^2\,\mathrm d\phi^2+\mathrm dz^2.
$$

Therefore

$$
(g_{ij})=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&r^2&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Let $x^1=r$, $x^2=\phi$, and $x^3=z$. The inverse metric is diagonal, so only the $k=1$ term in Equation (5.23) contributes when the upper index is $1$. For example,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Gamma^1{}_{11}
&=\frac12\sum_k g^{k1}
  (2\partial_1 g_{1k}-\partial_k g_{11})\\
&=\frac12g^{11}\partial_1g_{11}=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

For the two lower azimuthal indices, the derivative of $g_{22}$ can contribute.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Gamma^1{}_{22}
&=\frac12\sum_k g^{k1}
  (2\partial_2g_{2k}-\partial_k g_{22})\\
&=\frac12 g^{11}(2\partial_2 g_{21}-\partial_1 g_{22}).
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $g^{11}=1$, $g_{21}=0$, and $g_{22}=r^2$,

$$
\Gamma^1{}_{22}=\frac12(0+0-2r)=-r.
$$

As $\phi$ increases, the covariant basis vector $\vec e_\phi$ changes in the negative radial direction, with a coefficient of magnitude $r$. The other nonzero Christoffel symbols for this chart are $\Gamma^2{}_{12}=\Gamma^2{}_{21}=1/r$.[^6] The [[5.10 Chapter 5 problems|Chapter 5 problems]] ask you to verify these entries.

[^5]: These are the Christoffel symbols of the second kind. The symbols of the first kind have the upper index lowered with the metric.
[^6]: The Levi-Civita connection is symmetric in its two lower indices in a coordinate basis.
