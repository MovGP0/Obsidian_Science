title: "5.5 Metric tensor"
---

> [!TLDR]
> The metric tensor determines lengths and scalar products from coordinate components.

Coordinates assign numbers to points in a space. The axes can be straight or curved and can meet at angles other than right angles. The same approach applies to three-dimensional space or four-dimensional spacetime.

The *metric tensor* $g$ defines lengths and inner products independently of the coordinates used. Write its components as $g_{ij}$ and the components of the inverse metric as $g^{ij}$.

Consider the infinitesimal displacement $\mathrm d\vec r$ between two nearby points. In the coordinate and reciprocal bases,

$$
\mathrm d\vec r=\vec e_i\,\mathrm dx^i=\vec e^{\,i}\,\mathrm dx_i.
$$

In Euclidean space, the square of the line element is the displacement dotted with itself. If you use contravariant components for both factors,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm ds^2
&=\mathrm d\vec r\cdot\mathrm d\vec r\\
&=(\vec e_i\cdot\vec e_j)\,\mathrm dx^i\,\mathrm dx^j\\
&=g_{ij}\,\mathrm dx^i\,\mathrm dx^j.
\end{aligned}
$$

With covariant components for both factors,

$$
\mathrm ds^2=(\vec e^{\,i}\cdot\vec e^{\,j})\,\mathrm dx_i\,\mathrm dx_j
=g^{ij}\,\mathrm dx_i\,\mathrm dx_j.
$$

Using a different basis for each factor gives

$$
\mathrm ds^2=(\vec e_i\cdot\vec e^{\,j})\,\mathrm dx^i\,\mathrm dx_j
=\delta^j{}_i\,\mathrm dx^i\,\mathrm dx_j
=\mathrm dx^i\,\mathrm dx_i.
$$

The basis pairing gives the Kronecker delta. The metric is already included in the definition of the lowered components. All three expressions give the same line element because the transformation factors of the metric and components cancel.

### Lengths and angles

The length of a vector follows from its dot product with itself. Write both factors in the coordinate basis, both in the reciprocal basis, or one in each basis.

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\vec A|
&=\sqrt{(\vec e_i\cdot\vec e_j)A^iA^j}
=\sqrt{g_{ij}A^iA^j},\\
|\vec A|
&=\sqrt{(\vec e^{\,i}\cdot\vec e^{\,j})A_iA_j}
=\sqrt{g^{ij}A_iA_j},\\
|\vec A|
&=\sqrt{(\vec e_i\cdot\vec e^{\,j})A^iA_j}
=\sqrt{A^iA_i}.
\end{aligned}
$$

All three formulas give the same length. The same choices apply to the dot product of two vectors and hence to the angle between them.

For nonzero vectors in a Euclidean metric, the angle $\vartheta$ between them satisfies

$$
\begin{aligned}
\cos\vartheta
&=\frac{\vec A\cdot\vec B}{|\vec A|\,|\vec B|}\\
&=\frac{g_{ij}A^iB^j}{\sqrt{g_{ij}A^iA^j}\sqrt{g_{kl}B^kB^l}}\\
&=\frac{A_iB^i}{\sqrt{A_jA^j}\sqrt{B_kB^k}}\\
&=\frac{g^{ij}A_iB_j}{\sqrt{g^{ij}A_iA_j}\sqrt{g^{kl}B_kB_l}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus the metric provides the dot product needed to define lengths and angles.

### Transformation of metric components

The displacement components transform contravariantly.

$$
\mathrm dx^{\prime i}=\frac{\partial x^{\prime i}}{\partial x^j}\mathrm dx^j.
$$

Substitute this rule into the expression for the squared line element in the new coordinates.

For a primed Cartesian orthonormal basis, $\mathrm ds^2=\sum_{a=1}^3(\mathrm dx^{\prime a})^2$. Expanding it in the unprimed coordinates gives all nine products of differentials.

$$
\mathrm ds^2=\sum_{i=1}^3\sum_{j=1}^3
\left[
\frac{\partial x^{\prime 1}}{\partial x^i}\frac{\partial x^{\prime 1}}{\partial x^j}
+\frac{\partial x^{\prime 2}}{\partial x^i}\frac{\partial x^{\prime 2}}{\partial x^j}
+\frac{\partial x^{\prime 3}}{\partial x^i}\frac{\partial x^{\prime 3}}{\partial x^j}
\right]\mathrm dx^i\,\mathrm dx^j. \tag{5.12}
$$

Write the coefficient of $\mathrm dx^i\,\mathrm dx^j$ in Equation (5.12) as

$$
g_{ij}=\sum_{a=1}^{3}\frac{\partial x^{\prime a}}{\partial x^i}\frac{\partial x^{\prime a}}{\partial x^j}.
$$

The six choices $(i,j)=(1,1),(2,2),(3,3),(1,2),(1,3),(2,3)$ give its independent entries. The other entries follow from $g_{ji}=g_{ij}$. The full expansion is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm ds^2
&=g_{11}(\mathrm dx^1)^2+g_{22}(\mathrm dx^2)^2+g_{33}(\mathrm dx^3)^2\\
&\quad+2g_{12}\,\mathrm dx^1\,\mathrm dx^2
+2g_{13}\,\mathrm dx^1\,\mathrm dx^3
+2g_{23}\,\mathrm dx^2\,\mathrm dx^3.
\end{aligned}
$$

In Einstein notation this is

$$
\mathrm ds^2=g_{ij}\,\mathrm dx^i\,\mathrm dx^j. \tag{5.13}
$$

The coefficients transform as a covariant tensor of rank two. They combine with the coordinate differentials to give an invariant scalar. Each partial derivative in the coefficient formula is a Cartesian component of a vector tangent to one of the original coordinate axes.

For $i=1,2,3$, the coordinate basis vector expressed in the Cartesian system is

$$
\vec e_i=\left(
\frac{\partial x^{\prime 1}}{\partial x^i},
\frac{\partial x^{\prime 2}}{\partial x^i},
\frac{\partial x^{\prime 3}}{\partial x^i}\right). \tag{5.14}
$$

The coefficient formula is its dot product with the other basis vector.

$$
g_{ij}=\vec e_i\cdot\vec e_j
=\frac{\partial x^{\prime 1}}{\partial x^i}\frac{\partial x^{\prime 1}}{\partial x^j}
+\frac{\partial x^{\prime 2}}{\partial x^i}\frac{\partial x^{\prime 2}}{\partial x^j}
+\frac{\partial x^{\prime 3}}{\partial x^i}\frac{\partial x^{\prime 3}}{\partial x^j}. \tag{5.15}
$$

Thus the entries describe the lengths of the coordinate basis vectors and the angles between them.

### Spherical metric

Take $(x^1,x^2,x^3)=(r,\theta,\phi)$ in spherical polar coordinates and $(x^{\prime 1},x^{\prime 2},x^{\prime 3})=(x,y,z)$ in Cartesian coordinates.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x&=r\sin\theta\cos\phi=x^1\sin x^2\cos x^3,\\
y&=r\sin\theta\sin\phi=x^1\sin x^2\sin x^3,\\
z&=r\cos\theta=x^1\cos x^2.
\end{aligned} \tag{5.16}
$$

The nine partial derivatives form the columns of the Jacobian.

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
\partial_r x & \partial_\theta x & \partial_\phi x\\
\partial_r y & \partial_\theta y & \partial_\phi y\\
\partial_r z & \partial_\theta z & \partial_\phi z
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\sin\theta\cos\phi & r\cos\theta\cos\phi & -r\sin\theta\sin\phi\\
\sin\theta\sin\phi & r\cos\theta\sin\phi & r\sin\theta\cos\phi\\
\cos\theta & -r\sin\theta & 0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The squared lengths of the columns give the diagonal components in Equation (5.15).[^3]

Equation (5.15) gives $g_{11}=1$, $g_{22}=r^2$, and $g_{33}=r^2\sin^2\theta$. The products of distinct columns vanish, so $g_{12}=g_{13}=g_{23}=0$. Thus

$$
(g_{ij})=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0&0\\
0&r^2&0\\
0&0&r^2\sin^2\theta
\end{pmatrix}. \tag{5.17}
$$

The zero off-diagonal entries show that the coordinate directions meet at right angles wherever the coordinates are regular. Substituting the entries into Equation (5.13) gives

$$
\mathrm ds^2=\mathrm dr^2+r^2\,\mathrm d\theta^2
+r^2\sin^2\theta\,\mathrm d\phi^2. \tag{5.18}
$$

The coefficients convert coordinate increments into distances. The radial increment $\mathrm dr$ already gives the distance along the radial direction.

### Scale factors in orthogonal coordinates

In spherical coordinates, a change $\mathrm d\theta$ corresponds to a distance of magnitude $r|\mathrm d\theta|$. A change $\mathrm d\phi$ corresponds to $r\sin\theta|\mathrm d\phi|$, since the distance from the polar axis is $r\sin\theta$.

In any orthogonal coordinate system, the scale factors $h_i=\sqrt{g_{ii}}$ convert coordinate increments into lengths along their coordinate directions. For spherical coordinates, $h_1=1$, $h_2=r$, and $h_3=r\sin\theta$ in the regular chart.

Let $\hat e_i$ denote the normalized basis vectors and let $A_i^\mathrm{orth}$ be the components of $\vec A$ along them. Write $\partial_i=\partial/\partial x^i$ and $H=h_1h_2h_3$. The gradient of a scalar field $f$ is

$$
\nabla f
=\frac{\partial_1f}{h_1}\hat e_1
 +\frac{\partial_2f}{h_2}\hat e_2
 +\frac{\partial_3f}{h_3}\hat e_3.
$$

The divergence is

$$
\nabla\cdot\vec A
=\frac1H\left[
\partial_1(h_2h_3A_1^\mathrm{orth})
+\partial_2(h_1h_3A_2^\mathrm{orth})
+\partial_3(h_1h_2A_3^\mathrm{orth})
\right].
$$

For a right-handed basis, the curl is

$$
\nabla\times\vec A
=\frac1H
\begin{vmatrix}
h_1\hat e_1 & h_2\hat e_2 & h_3\hat e_3\\
\partial_1 & \partial_2 & \partial_3\\
h_1A_1^\mathrm{orth} & h_2A_2^\mathrm{orth} & h_3A_3^\mathrm{orth}
\end{vmatrix}.
$$

Expand it to obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla\times\vec A
&=\frac{h_1}{H}\left[\partial_2(h_3A_3^\mathrm{orth})-\partial_3(h_2A_2^\mathrm{orth})\right]\hat e_1\\
&\quad+\frac{h_2}{H}\left[\partial_3(h_1A_1^\mathrm{orth})-\partial_1(h_3A_3^\mathrm{orth})\right]\hat e_2\\
&\quad+\frac{h_3}{H}\left[\partial_1(h_2A_2^\mathrm{orth})-\partial_2(h_1A_1^\mathrm{orth})\right]\hat e_3.
\end{aligned}
$$

The Laplacian of $f$ is

$$
\nabla^2f
=\frac1H\left[
\partial_1\left(\frac{h_2h_3}{h_1}\partial_1f\right)
+\partial_2\left(\frac{h_1h_3}{h_2}\partial_2f\right)
+\partial_3\left(\frac{h_1h_2}{h_3}\partial_3f\right)
\right].
$$
