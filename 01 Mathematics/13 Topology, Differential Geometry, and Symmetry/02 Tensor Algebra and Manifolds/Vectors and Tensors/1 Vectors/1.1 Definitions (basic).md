title: "1.1 Definitions (basic)"
---

> [!TLDR]
> A vector describes magnitude and direction and can be represented by a directed line segment or a list of components.

> [!definition]
> A vector is the mathematical representation of a physical entity with magnitude and direction.

Velocity gives the speed and direction of motion. Force gives the strength and direction of a push or pull. Speed and temperature are scalars.

The word *vector* comes from the Latin *vehere*, meaning “to carry.” Eighteenth-century astronomers used the word when investigating how a planet is “carried” around the Sun.[^1] In equations, distinguish vectors from scalars. For example, write force with an arrow ($\mechanical{\vec F}$), in bold ($\mechanical{\mathbf F}$), or with an underline ($\mechanical{\underline F}$ or $\mechanical{\underset{\sim}{F}}$).

Fig. 1.1(a) shows a vector as a directed line segment. An ordered list of $N$ numbers can also represent a vector in an $N$-dimensional space.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-1-1.png|**Fig. 1.1** A vector (a) and a vector field (b).]]

A vector can represent wind velocity, rocket acceleration, or the force on a football. The length of the arrow represents the magnitude. Once you select a scale, draw comparable vectors to the same scale. The angle between the arrow and one or more reference directions, usually the coordinate axes, specifies the direction.

A *free vector* can move to any position while keeping its magnitude and direction. A *bound vector*, also called an *anchored vector*, applies at a given point.[^2] A *sliding vector* can move along its line of action while keeping its magnitude and direction. Sliding vectors are useful in problems involving torque and angular motion.

To represent wind velocity at different locations, draw a bound vector with its tail at each point of interest. Each vector gives the speed and direction of the wind at that location. This collection of vectors is a *vector field*, as in Fig. 1.1(b).

In a three-dimensional Cartesian coordinate system, the coordinates $x$, $y$, and $z$ at the start and end of the arrow specify a bound vector, as in Fig. 1.2(a). See [[1.5 Non-Cartesian unit vectors]] for non-Cartesian coordinate systems.

For a vector anchored at the origin, only the coordinates of the endpoint are necessary, as in Fig. 1.2(b).[^3] For example, a vector from the origin that extends five units along the $x$-axis is $(5,0,0)$. These numbers are the *components* of the vector. The number of components equals the dimension of the space. A vector in two dimensions has two components.

In four-dimensional spacetime, a vector has four components. A horizontal list of components is a *row vector*. A vertical list is a *column vector*.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-1-2.png|**Fig. 1.2** A vector in three-dimensional Cartesian coordinates with its tail at a general point (a) and at the origin (b).]]

The temperature can vary from point to point in a room. At each point, a single number gives the temperature. W. R. Hamilton called such quantities *scalars* and described them as “all values contained on the one scale of progression of numbers from negative to positive infinity.”[^4]

> [!definition]
> A scalar is the mathematical representation of a physical entity that has magnitude only.

Mass, charge, energy, and speed are also scalars. The variation of temperature in space gives rise to a vector called the [[2.8 Gradient|gradient]] of the temperature field.

A scalar has one component, and a vector has three components in three-dimensional space. Quantities whose representation requires more components can include higher-rank tensors.[^5] The last three chapters discuss tensors in more detail.

> [!definition]
> A tensor is the mathematical representation of a physical entity that can have magnitude and multiple directions.

The [[6.1 The inertia tensor|inertia tensor]] relates the angular velocity of a rotating object to its angular momentum. These vectors can point in different directions.

A rank-$R$ tensor in three-dimensional space has $3^R$ components. For example, a second-rank tensor has $3^2=9$ components. In $N$ dimensions, a scalar has one component, a vector has $N$, and a rank-$R$ tensor has $N^R$.

In three dimensions, a scalar, a vector, and a second-rank tensor can have the following representations.

| Scalar | Vector | Second-rank tensor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $(x)$ | $(x_1,x_2,x_3)$ or $\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\x_3\end{pmatrix}$ | $\begin{pmatrix}x_{11}&x_{12}&x_{13}\\x_{21}&x_{22}&x_{23}\\x_{31}&x_{32}&x_{33}\end{pmatrix}$ |

In this notation, a scalar has zero indices, a vector has one, and a rank-two tensor has two. A rank-three tensor can be represented by a three-dimensional array. See [[5 Higher-rank tensors]].
