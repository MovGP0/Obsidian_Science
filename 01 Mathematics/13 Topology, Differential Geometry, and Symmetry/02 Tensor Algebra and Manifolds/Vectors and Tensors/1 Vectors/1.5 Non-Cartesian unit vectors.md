---
title: "1.5 Non-Cartesian unit vectors"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Coordinate unit vectors specify local directions of increase. In polar coordinates, these directions vary with position.

A coordinate system can simplify a problem when its coordinate directions follow the way physical quantities vary. See [[4.1 Coordinate-system transformations]] for changes between systems.

To specify a location, give one coordinate for each dimension. For example, three Cartesian coordinates locate a point in space. Latitude and longitude locate a point on Earth's surface if height is ignored. One coordinate locates a point on an idealized, one-dimensional island. A distance of $\quantity{3.75}{km}$ identifies a location on the island once you specify the end from which you measure it.

At a point, a coordinate unit vector has length one and points in the local direction of increasing coordinate with the other coordinates fixed. In Cartesian coordinates, $\hat i$, $\hat j$, and $\hat k$ point in the directions of increasing $x$, $y$, and $z$.

In two dimensions, $x$ gives the signed horizontal position and $y$ gives the signed vertical position. Polar coordinates specify the same point by its distance $r$ from the origin and its angle $\theta$ counterclockwise from the positive $x$-axis.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-1-10.png|**Fig. 1.10** A point in Cartesian and polar coordinates (a) and the corresponding unit vectors (b).]]

Convert Cartesian coordinates to polar coordinates with

$$
\begin{aligned}
r &= \sqrt{x^2+y^2},\\
\theta &= \arctan\left(\frac yx\right).
\end{aligned} \tag{1.9}
$$

Apply the quadrant correction from [[1.3 Vector components]] to the angle. The reverse conversion is

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= r\cos\theta,\\
y &= r\sin\theta.
\end{aligned} \tag{1.10}
$$

For the point in Fig. 1.10, $(\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y})=(\quantity{4}{cm},\quantity{9}{cm})$ and $(\mechanical{r},\theta)\approx(\quantity{9.85}{cm},66.0^\circ)$ describe the same location.

At the point shown in Fig. 1.10(b), the radial unit vector $\hat r$ points up and to the right, in the direction of increasing $r$ at fixed $\theta$. The angular unit vector $\hat\theta$ points up and to the left, in the direction of increasing $\theta$ at fixed $r$.

The polar unit vectors are perpendicular and their directions depend on the angle.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat r &= \cos\theta\,\hat i+\sin\theta\,\hat j,\\
\hat\theta &= -\sin\theta\,\hat i+\cos\theta\,\hat j.
\end{aligned} \tag{1.11}
$$

On the positive $x$-axis, $\theta=0$, $\hat r=\hat i$, and $\hat\theta=\hat j$. On the positive $y$-axis, $\theta=90^\circ$, $\hat r=\hat j$, and $\hat\theta=-\hat i$.

The polar unit vectors are attached to their local coordinate directions. In general, moving them to a different angle changes their directions, so they do not constitute a fixed set of [[1.1 Definitions (basic)|free vectors]]. When differentiating a vector expressed in polar coordinates, account for changes in its unit vectors. The Cartesian unit vectors are constant.

Three-dimensional coordinate systems need three coordinates, each with a corresponding unit vector. Two common non-Cartesian systems are cylindrical and spherical coordinates, shown in Figs. 1.11 and 1.12.

### Cylindrical coordinates

The cylindrical coordinates of a point $P$ are $r$, $\phi$, and $z$. The radius $r$, also written $\rho$, is the perpendicular distance from the $z$-axis. The azimuth $\phi$ is the angle from the positive $x$-axis to the projection of the position onto the $xy$-plane. The coordinate $z$ is the same height as in Cartesian coordinates.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-1-11.png|**Fig. 1.11** Cylindrical coordinates and unit vectors at the intersection of surfaces of constant r, φ, and z.]]

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-1-12.png|**Fig. 1.12** Spherical coordinates and unit vectors at the intersection of surfaces of constant r, θ, and φ.]]

Convert from Cartesian to cylindrical coordinates with

$$
\begin{aligned}
r &= \sqrt{x^2+y^2},\\
\phi &= \arctan\left(\frac yx\right),\\
z &= z.
\end{aligned} \tag{1.12}
$$

Choose the quadrant for $\phi$ from the signs of $x$ and $y$.

Convert from cylindrical to Cartesian coordinates with

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= r\cos\phi,\\
y &= r\sin\phi,\\
z &= z.
\end{aligned} \tag{1.13}
$$

In Fig. 1.11, the three cylindrical unit vectors are mutually perpendicular. The unit vector $\hat r$ is normal to the cylinder of constant $r$ and points outward. The unit vector $\hat\phi$ is normal to the plane of constant $\phi$ and points toward increasing $\phi$. The unit vector $\hat z$ is normal to the plane of constant $z$ and points toward increasing $z$. The ordered set $(\hat r,\hat\phi,\hat z)$ is right-handed, so $\hat r\times\hat\phi=\hat z$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat r &= \cos\phi\,\hat i+\sin\phi\,\hat j,\\
\hat\phi &= -\sin\phi\,\hat i+\cos\phi\,\hat j,\\
\hat z &= \hat k.
\end{aligned} \tag{1.14}
$$

### Spherical coordinates

In Fig. 1.12, the spherical coordinates of $P$ are $r$, $\theta$, and $\phi$. The radius $r$ is the distance from the origin, $\theta$ is the zenith angle from the positive $z$-axis, and $\phi$ is the azimuth from the positive $x$-axis to the projection onto the $xy$-plane.

$$
\begin{aligned}
r &= \sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2},\\
\theta &= \arccos\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}}\right),\\
\phi &= \arctan\left(\frac yx\right).
\end{aligned} \tag{1.15}
$$

Choose the quadrant for $\phi$ from the signs of $x$ and $y$. To convert from spherical to Cartesian coordinates, use

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= r\sin\theta\cos\phi,\\
y &= r\sin\theta\sin\phi,\\
z &= r\cos\theta.
\end{aligned} \tag{1.16}
$$

At $P$, the spherical unit vectors are mutually perpendicular. The unit vector $\hat r$ is normal to the sphere of constant $r$. The unit vector $\hat\theta$ is normal to the cone of constant $\theta$. The unit vector $\hat\phi$ is normal to the plane of constant $\phi$. The ordered set $(\hat r,\hat\theta,\hat\phi)$ is right-handed.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat r &= \sin\theta\cos\phi\,\hat i+\sin\theta\sin\phi\,\hat j+\cos\theta\,\hat k,\\
\hat\theta &= \cos\theta\cos\phi\,\hat i+\cos\theta\sin\phi\,\hat j-\sin\theta\,\hat k,\\
\hat\phi &= -\sin\phi\,\hat i+\cos\phi\,\hat j.
\end{aligned} \tag{1.17}
$$

For motion along a line of constant longitude on a spherical planet, use the $\hat\theta$ direction. For the magnetic field around a straight current-carrying wire, use the cylindrical $\hat\phi$ direction. See the [[1.7 Chapter 1 problems|Chapter 1 problems]] for examples.
