---
title: "1.4 Vector addition and multiplication by a scalar"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Add vectors by adding their components, and scale a vector by multiplying each component by the same scalar.

The expansion from [[1.3 Vector components|Equation (1.1)]] uses both vector addition and scalar multiplication.

$$
\vec A=A_x\hat i+A_y\hat j+A_z\hat k.
$$

Each scalar coefficient multiplies a unit vector. Multiplication by a positive scalar preserves the direction and scales the magnitude. For example, $5\vec A$ points in the same direction as $\vec A$ and has five times its magnitude, as in Fig. 1.7(a). The vector $\tfrac12\vec A$ points in the same direction and has half the magnitude. Since $\hat i$ has magnitude one, $A_x\hat i$ has magnitude $|A_x|$ and lies along the $x$-axis.

Multiplication by a negative scalar reverses the direction and scales the magnitude by the absolute value of the scalar. Thus $-2\vec B$ has twice the magnitude of $\vec B$ and points in the opposite direction, as in Fig. 1.7(b).

The terms $A_y\hat j$ and $A_z\hat k$ in Equation (1.1) represent steps in different directions. Use vector addition to combine them.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-1-7.png|**Fig. 1.7** Multiplication of a vector by a positive scalar (a) and a negative scalar (b).]]

To add vectors graphically, move the second vector so that its tail meets the head of the first vector. Keep its length and direction fixed. Draw the sum from the tail of the first vector to the head of the second. In Fig. 1.5(b), the tail of $A_z\hat k$ meets the head of $A_y\hat j$.

The head-to-tail method works for any number of vectors, including vectors that are at an angle to one another. In Fig. 1.8, the tail of $\vec B$ moves to the head of $\vec A$. Their sum, the *resultant* $\vec C=\vec A+\vec B$, runs from the tail of $\vec A$ to the head of the relocated $\vec B$. Adding the vectors in the reverse order gives the same resultant.

You can measure the length and direction of a resultant with a ruler and protractor. To calculate it from components, add the signed components along each axis. For $\vec C=\vec A+\vec B$, the $x$-component is $C_x=A_x+B_x$, and the $y$-component is $C_y=A_y+B_y$.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-1-8.png|**Fig. 1.8** Graphical addition of vectors by the head-to-tail method.]]

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_x &= A_x+B_x,\\
C_y &= A_y+B_y.
\end{aligned} \tag{1.6}
$$

Fig. 1.9 shows why the components add this way. Once $C_x$ and $C_y$ are known, the magnitude and the reference direction follow from

$$
|\vec C|=\sqrt{C_x^2+C_y^2} \tag{1.7}
$$

and

$$
\theta=\arctan\left(\frac{C_y}{C_x}\right). \tag{1.8}
$$

Apply the quadrant correction from [[1.3 Vector components]] to find the direction. For example, Fig. 1.9 uses $\vec A=6\hat i+\hat j$ and $\vec B=-2\hat i+8\hat j$. Use Equation (1.6) to find their sum.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-1-9.png|**Fig. 1.9** Addition of the y-components (a) and x-components (b).]]

$$
\begin{aligned}
C_x &= A_x+B_x=6+(-2)=4,\\
C_y &= A_y+B_y=1+8=9.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus $\vec C=4\hat i+9\hat j$. Equation (1.7) gives its magnitude.

$$
|\vec C|=\sqrt{C_x^2+C_y^2}=\sqrt{4^2+9^2}=\sqrt{16+81}\approx9.85.
$$

Equation (1.8) gives the angle from the positive $x$-axis.

$$
\theta=\arctan\left(\frac{C_y}{C_x}\right)=\arctan\left(\frac94\right)\approx66.0^\circ.
$$
