---
title: "1.2 Cartesian unit vectors"
---

> [!TLDR]
> The Cartesian unit vectors have magnitude one and point along the positive Cartesian axes.

A *unit vector*, also called a *versor*, specifies a direction. Unit vectors are useful when a problem involves several vectors.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-1-3.png|**Fig. 1.3** Unit vectors in three-dimensional Cartesian coordinates.]]

In three-dimensional Cartesian coordinates, $\hat x$, $\hat y$, and $\hat z$ point along the positive $x$-, $y$-, and $z$-axes, respectively. The same vectors are also written $\hat i$, $\hat j$, and $\hat k$. Each has magnitude one.

You can draw a Cartesian unit vector at any location. Fig. 1.4 shows $\hat i$, $\hat j$, and $\hat k$ at a point away from the origin. Each direction stays parallel to its axis throughout the coordinate system.

Unit vectors identify directions in [[2 Vector operations|vector operations]], such as finding the part of a vector along a given direction. The expression $5\hat i$ means five units along the positive $x$-direction, $-3\hat j$ means three units along the negative $y$-direction, and $\hat k$ means one unit along the positive $z$-direction. Other coordinate systems also have [[1.5 Non-Cartesian unit vectors|unit vectors]].

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-1-4.png|**Fig. 1.4** Cartesian unit vectors at an arbitrary point.]]
