---
title: "1.6 Basis vectors"
---

> [!TLDR]
> A basis gives a set of independent directions whose linear combinations represent every vector in the space.

In three dimensions, any vector can be expressed as a sum of multiples of $\hat i$, $\hat j$, and $\hat k$. Each coefficient gives the signed step along one axis. These unit vectors form a *basis*.

The three vectors $2\hat i$, $2\hat j$, and $2\hat k$ also form a basis, as in Fig. 1.13(a). In this basis, the components of a given vector are half the corresponding components in the unit basis.

The vectors $\vec e_1$, $\vec e_2$, and $\vec e_3$ in Fig. 1.13(b) give an example of a non-orthogonal basis whose vectors have non-unit lengths. Three coplanar vectors can only produce vectors in their plane. Three noncoplanar vectors *span* the three-dimensional vector space because suitable multiples of them can sum to any vector in that space.

![[vectors-and-tensors-fig-1-13.png|**Fig. 1.13** Alternative basis vectors with twice the Cartesian unit lengths (a) and with non-orthogonal directions (b).]]

The vectors $\vec e_1$, $\vec e_2$, and $\vec e_3$ are *linearly independent* if

$$
A\vec e_1+B\vec e_2+C\vec e_3=\vec 0 \tag{1.18}
$$

implies $A=B=C=0$. For three vectors in three dimensions, this condition means that the vectors are noncoplanar and form a basis.

Normalize each basis vector by dividing it by its magnitude. This gives a unit vector with the same direction.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat e_1 &= \frac{\vec e_1}{|\vec e_1|},\\
\hat e_2 &= \frac{\vec e_2}{|\vec e_2|},\\
\hat e_3 &= \frac{\vec e_3}{|\vec e_3|}.
\end{aligned} \tag{1.19}
$$

An *orthonormal basis* consists of mutually perpendicular vectors with magnitude one. The Cartesian basis $(\hat i,\hat j,\hat k)$ is a common example.

### Coordinate basis vectors

Use partial derivatives to express the coordinate basis of one system in the basis of another system.[^8] For example, [[1.5 Non-Cartesian unit vectors|Equation (1.16)]] gives the Cartesian coordinates as functions of $(r,\theta,\phi)$. Differentiate with respect to one spherical coordinate at a time.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vec e_r
&= \frac{\partial x}{\partial r}\hat i+
   \frac{\partial y}{\partial r}\hat j+
   \frac{\partial z}{\partial r}\hat k\\
&= \sin\theta\cos\phi\,\hat i+
   \sin\theta\sin\phi\,\hat j+
   \cos\theta\,\hat k.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vec e_\theta
&= \frac{\partial x}{\partial\theta}\hat i+
   \frac{\partial y}{\partial\theta}\hat j+
   \frac{\partial z}{\partial\theta}\hat k\\
&= r\cos\theta\cos\phi\,\hat i+
   r\cos\theta\sin\phi\,\hat j-
   r\sin\theta\,\hat k,\\[4pt]
\vec e_\phi
&= \frac{\partial x}{\partial\phi}\hat i+
   \frac{\partial y}{\partial\phi}\hat j+
   \frac{\partial z}{\partial\phi}\hat k\\
&= -r\sin\theta\sin\phi\,\hat i+
   r\sin\theta\cos\phi\,\hat j.
\end{aligned}
$$

The radial basis vector has magnitude one and is dimensionless. The two angular basis vectors typically have non-unit magnitudes and, when $r$ is a physical distance, have the dimension of length. Each nonzero vector gives a unit vector after division by its magnitude.

In general, let $(x_1,x_2,x_3)$ be the original coordinates and $(x'_1,x'_2,x'_3)$ the new coordinates. Express the original basis vectors in the new basis as follows.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vec e_1 &= \frac{\partial x'_1}{\partial x_1}\vec e'_1+
            \frac{\partial x'_2}{\partial x_1}\vec e'_2+
            \frac{\partial x'_3}{\partial x_1}\vec e'_3,\\
\vec e_2 &= \frac{\partial x'_1}{\partial x_2}\vec e'_1+
            \frac{\partial x'_2}{\partial x_2}\vec e'_2+
            \frac{\partial x'_3}{\partial x_2}\vec e'_3,\\
\vec e_3 &= \frac{\partial x'_1}{\partial x_3}\vec e'_1+
            \frac{\partial x'_2}{\partial x_3}\vec e'_2+
            \frac{\partial x'_3}{\partial x_3}\vec e'_3.
\end{aligned} \tag{1.20}
$$

The partial derivatives in each row are the components of that original basis vector in the new basis. This is why some authors define coordinate basis vectors in terms of partial derivatives. See [[4.2 Basis-vector transformations]] and the [[1.7 Chapter 1 problems|Chapter 1 problems]].

[^8]: See [[2.5 Partial derivatives]] for an introduction to partial derivatives.
