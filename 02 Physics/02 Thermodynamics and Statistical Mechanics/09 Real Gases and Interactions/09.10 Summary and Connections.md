> [!TLDR]
> Excluded volume, attraction, and molecular correlations explain the main departures of real gases from ideal behavior.

The main results of this chapter are:

1. **Van der Waals equation.** Excluded volume reduces the volume available to molecular centers. Attraction lowers the pressure. Together they give a liquid–vapor transition, a critical point, and a law of corresponding states within the model.
2. **Virial expansion.** The equation of state is expanded in powers of density. Mayer-function integrals connect the coefficients to the interaction potential.
3. **Lennard–Jones potential.** The well depth and molecular length set two physical scales. The reduced Boyle temperature is about 3.42. The soft repulsive core requires a temperature-dependent high-temperature expansion.
4. **Cluster expansion.** Connected graphs enter cluster integrals. Irreducible graphs remain after conversion to density virial coefficients.
5. **Joule–Thomson effect.** Throttling conserves enthalpy under the stated flow assumptions. Its temperature change depends on the sign of the Joule–Thomson coefficient. In the dilute van der Waals limit, the upper inversion temperature is twice the Boyle temperature.

A useful reconstruction is to choose an observable before choosing a formula. For dilute pressure, use the second virial coefficient and check omitted density powers. For a liquid–vapor plateau, compare chemical potentials through the Maxwell construction. For cooling through a valve, use a flow energy balance and a heat capacity as well as the equation of state. These calculations use the same interactions but ask different thermodynamic questions.

Test the distinctions at the Boyle temperature. The leading pressure correction is zero, yet higher correlations can remain and the throttling coefficient need not vanish. Test them again at the critical point: the mean-field isotherm has a horizontal inflection, but this does not make the dilute expansion or the model critical exponents quantitatively reliable for a real fluid. These boundary cases connect microscopic correlations to the limits of a macroscopic equation.
