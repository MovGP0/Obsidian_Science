> [!TLDR]
> Identical quantum particles have the same intrinsic properties and cannot be distinguished by particle labels.

In classical mechanics, two identical particles can in principle be tracked separately. For example, two electrons or two atoms of the same isotope can be assigned labels, as if they were numbered billiard balls. Their equal mass, charge, and spin do not prevent this classical distinction.

Quantum particles are described by wavefunctions. When two wave packets overlap, there is no observable that identifies a permanent label as “particle 1” or “particle 2.” This is a property of identical particles, rather than a practical limit of measurement.

**Definition 6.1 — Identical particles.** Two particles are identical if they have the same intrinsic quantum numbers: mass ${\mechanical{m}}$, charge ${\electric{q}}$, spin ${\mechanical{s}}$, and all other conserved intrinsic quantum numbers. No physical observable can distinguish an exchange of their labels.

Consider two well-separated wave packets, one on the left and one on the right. Their positions can be used as approximate labels while their overlap is negligible. Interchange the formal particle labels: the statement “one particle in each packet” is unchanged. When the packets overlap, the two alternatives in a scattering amplitude must be combined with the appropriate exchange sign.

Different spin projections can distinguish occupied single-particle states, but they do not turn two electrons into different particle species. Antisymmetry still acts on the combined spatial and spin arguments. This distinction is needed when applying Pauli exclusion later.
