> [!TLDR]
> Differentiate equal chemical potentials to obtain the coexistence-curve slope.

Assume a single chemical component, macroscopic equilibrium, and no additional work terms such as an applied magnetic field. Surface and interface contributions are neglected. The chemical potential is the molar Gibbs free energy. Its differential therefore contains molar entropy and molar volume; this avoids mixing a total entropy change with a per-mole volume change.

Consider phases $\alpha$ and $\beta$ in equilibrium along a coexistence curve in the temperature–pressure plane:

$$
{\energy{\mu}}_\alpha({\thermal{T}},{\mechanical{P}})={\energy{\mu}}_\beta({\thermal{T}},{\mechanical{P}}).
$$

The equality must remain valid as temperature and pressure change along that curve:

$$
\mathrm d{\energy{\mu}}_\alpha=\mathrm d{\energy{\mu}}_\beta.
$$

Use the molar relation $\mathrm d{\energy{\mu}}=-{\thermal{s}}\,\mathrm d{\thermal{T}}+{\mechanical{v}}\,\mathrm d{\mechanical{P}}$:

$$
-{\thermal{s}}_\alpha\,\mathrm d{\thermal{T}}+{\mechanical{v}}_\alpha\,\mathrm d{\mechanical{P}}=-{\thermal{s}}_\beta\,\mathrm d{\thermal{T}}+{\mechanical{v}}_\beta\,\mathrm d{\mechanical{P}}.
$$

Collect the pressure and temperature terms:

$$
 ({\mechanical{v}}_\alpha-{\mechanical{v}}_\beta)\,\mathrm d{\mechanical{P}}=({\thermal{s}}_\alpha-{\thermal{s}}_\beta)\,\mathrm d{\thermal{T}}.
$$

Thus the **Clausius–Clapeyron equation** is:

$$
\frac{\mathrm d{\mechanical{P}}}{\mathrm d{\thermal{T}}}=\frac{{\thermal{s}}_\alpha-{\thermal{s}}_\beta}{{\mechanical{v}}_\alpha-{\mechanical{v}}_\beta}=\frac{\Delta {\thermal{s}}}{\Delta {\mechanical{v}}}=\frac{{\energy{L}}}{{\thermal{T}}\Delta {\mechanical{v}}}.
$$

Define both changes in the same direction, from phase $\beta$ to phase $\alpha$. The molar latent heat ${\energy{L}}={\thermal{T}}\Delta {\thermal{s}}$ is positive when the final phase has higher entropy, as in vaporization.

The differentiation is along the coexistence curve, so pressure is not held fixed in this step. Equal chemical potentials at one point would not suffice: their equality must persist at nearby coexistence points. Dividing the resulting differential relation is valid where the volume difference is nonzero. At an ordinary critical endpoint both phase differences vanish, so the formula becomes an indeterminate ratio and requires the limiting equation of state.

As checks, reversing the phase labels changes both differences and leaves the slope invariant. Molar entropy divided by molar volume has units of pressure per kelvin, as does latent heat divided by temperature and molar-volume change. For positive latent heat, a negative volume change predicts a negative melting-curve slope.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ${\energy{\mu}}$ | $\unit{J\,mol^{-1}}$ | Molar chemical potential | Gibbs free energy per mole. |
| ${\thermal{T}}$ | $\unit{K}$ | Temperature | Absolute temperature of the system. |
| ${\mechanical{P}}$ | $\unit{Pa}$ | Pressure | Thermodynamic pressure. |
| ${\thermal{s}}$ | $\unit{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ | Molar entropy | Entropy per mole of substance. |
| ${\mechanical{v}}$ | $\unit{m^3\,mol^{-1}}$ | Molar volume | Volume per mole in the stated phase. |
| ${\energy{L}}$ | $\unit{J\,mol^{-1}}$ | Molar latent heat | Heat absorbed per mole in the stated phase change. |
