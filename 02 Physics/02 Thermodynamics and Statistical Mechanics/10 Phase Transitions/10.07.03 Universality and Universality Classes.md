> [!TLDR]
> Systems can share critical exponents when dimension, symmetry, and interaction range agree.

The comparison concerns asymptotic equilibrium critical behavior, not equality of every thermodynamic function. Match the coarse order-parameter symmetry, dimension, and interaction range before comparing exponents. A finite measurement window may instead show crossover between descriptions, and anisotropy can matter even when it is weak on microscopic scales.

Universality means that physically different systems can have the same critical exponents. The main features that determine the class are:

1. Spatial dimension $d$.
2. Symmetry of the order parameter and its number of components $n$.
3. Range of the interactions, such as short-range or long-range interactions.

Read the table as a set of source benchmarks with different evidence status. Exact Ising values are mathematical results for a model; mean-field values follow from an approximation; RG values are theoretical estimates; experimental entries summarize measurements of materials. Agreement between these rows is a test of a model assignment, not a proof that the material has the same microscopic Hamiltonian.

As a check, the exact two-dimensional Ising values obey the scaling relations with a logarithmic heat-capacity singularity despite a zero heat-capacity exponent. A zero exponent therefore cannot be interpreted as a finite constant without further information. The mean-field row at four dimensions states leading powers; logarithmic factors can modify the asymptotic forms there. Keep the quoted rounded values rather than treating their last digits as a precision comparison.

## Main universality classes

- **Ising class:** one real component and $\mathbb Z_2$ symmetry. Examples are magnets with an easy axis, the liquid–gas critical point, and binary mixtures.
- **XY class:** two real components, or one complex component, with U(1) symmetry. Examples include superfluid helium-4. The two-dimensional XY case has a Berezinskii–Kosterlitz–Thouless transition; its essential singularity needs a different treatment from the simple power-law table.
- **Heisenberg class:** three real components with O(3) symmetry. The model describes an isotropic ferromagnet.
- **Mean-field regime:** for short-range models with a finite number of components, the upper critical dimension is usually four. Mean-field powers apply above it. At four dimensions, logarithmic corrections can occur.

The symbols in this article are dimensionless model parameters and exponents. No dimensional physical quantities are introduced.
