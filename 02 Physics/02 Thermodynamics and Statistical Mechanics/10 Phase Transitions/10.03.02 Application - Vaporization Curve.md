> [!TLDR]
> For an approximately ideal vapor and constant latent heat, the logarithm of vapor pressure is linear in inverse temperature.

Why does a small temperature change produce a large vapor-pressure change? Vaporization carries substantial latent heat, and equilibrium requires the pressure to adjust so that both chemical potentials stay equal. The result below is a local model for a pure substance in liquid–vapor equilibrium, with ideal vapor, negligible liquid volume, and nearly constant molar latent heat.

For liquid–vapor coexistence, vapor volume dominates the volume change. Approximate the vapor as an ideal gas and neglect the liquid molar volume:

$$
{\mechanical{v}}_{\mathrm{gas}}=\frac{{\thermal{R}}{\thermal{T}}}{{\mechanical{P}}},\qquad \Delta {\mechanical{v}}\approx\frac{{\thermal{R}}{\thermal{T}}}{{\mechanical{P}}}.
$$

Insert this into the Clausius–Clapeyron equation:

$$
\frac{\mathrm d{\mechanical{P}}}{\mathrm d{\thermal{T}}}=\frac{{\energy{L}}}{{\thermal{T}}({\thermal{R}}{\thermal{T}}/{\mechanical{P}})}=\frac{{\energy{L}}{\mechanical{P}}}{{\thermal{R}}{\thermal{T}}^2}.
$$

Separate the variables. If the molar latent heat is approximately constant over the temperature interval:

$$
\int_{P_0}^{{\mechanical{P}}}\frac{\mathrm d{\mechanical{P}}'}{{\mechanical{P}}'}=\frac{{\energy{L}}}{{\thermal{R}}}\int_{T_0}^{{\thermal{T}}}\frac{\mathrm d{\thermal{T}}'}{{\thermal{T}}'^2}.
$$

Integration gives:

$$
\ln\frac{{\mechanical{P}}}{{\mechanical{P}}_0}=-\frac{{\energy{L}}}{{\thermal{R}}}\left(\frac1{\thermal{T}}-\frac1{{\thermal{T}}_0}\right),
$$

$$
{\mechanical{P}}({\thermal{T}})={\mechanical{P}}_0\exp\left[-\frac{{\energy{L}}}{{\thermal{R}}}\left(\frac1{\thermal{T}}-\frac1{{\thermal{T}}_0}\right)\right].
$$

A plot of logarithmic vapor pressure against inverse temperature is therefore approximately a straight line with slope $-{\energy{L}}/{\thermal{R}}$. This permits an experimental estimate of the latent heat.

For positive latent heat, increasing temperature above the reference temperature makes the exponent positive, so the pressure increases. At the reference temperature the pressure returns exactly to its reference value. The exponential argument is dimensionless because latent heat divided by the molar gas constant has temperature units.

A proposed test is to plot the logarithm of the dimensionless pressure ratio against inverse absolute temperature. Curvature can reveal a changing latent heat or departure from ideal vapor behavior; it is not automatically a failure of the exact coexistence relation. Do not extrapolate the constant-latent-heat formula to the critical point, where the two phases merge and the latent heat vanishes. No vapor-pressure data or fitted slope is provided here.
