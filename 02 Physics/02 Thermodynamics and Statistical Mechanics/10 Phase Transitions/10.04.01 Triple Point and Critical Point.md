> [!TLDR]
> Three phases meet at a triple point; the liquid–gas coexistence curve ends at a critical point.

These two special points have different physical meanings. At a triple point three distinct equilibrium phases remain distinguishable. At a liquid–gas critical point two phases become indistinguishable. Assume a pure substance with temperature and pressure as the only independent intensive controls; extra components require a different coexistence count.

**Definition 10.2 — Triple point.** The triple point is the point in the temperature–pressure plane at which three phases coexist in equilibrium.

For water, the source gives:

$$
{\thermal{T}}_t=273.16\,\unit{K},\qquad {\mechanical{P}}_t=611.73\,\unit{Pa}.
$$

The triple-point temperature of water was used to define the kelvin until the SI revision in 2019.

**Definition 10.3 — Critical point.** The liquid–gas critical point is the endpoint of the liquid–gas coexistence curve. At this point, the density difference between the two phases vanishes.

> [!NOTE] Correction to the source
> The source states that the fluid is beyond the critical point when temperature exceeds its critical value **or** pressure exceeds its critical value. The conventional supercritical region requires **both** ${\thermal{T}}>{\thermal{T}}_c$ and ${\mechanical{P}}>{\mechanical{P}}_c$. A pressure above the critical pressure at a lower temperature does not by itself make the fluid supercritical.

A useful comparison is to approach each point along a coexistence line. At the triple point a third phase becomes possible without requiring the first two phases to have equal density. At the critical endpoint the liquid–gas density contrast tends to zero, so a latent-heat and volume-jump description must be taken as a limit rather than evaluated as a ratio of two zeros.

The numerical triple-point values above are retained source benchmarks. The historical definition statement is distinct from a present measurement: [NIST explains the kelvin redefinition and the continuing use of water triple-point cells](https://www.nist.gov/si-redefinition/kelvin/kelvin-present-realization). The two inequalities in the source-correction callout are both required for the stated supercritical region; crossing only a large-pressure threshold at low temperature is a useful counterexample.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ${\thermal{T}}_t$ | $\unit{K}$ | Triple-point temperature | Temperature at three-phase coexistence. |
| ${\mechanical{P}}_t$ | $\unit{Pa}$ | Triple-point pressure | Pressure at three-phase coexistence. |
| ${\thermal{T}}$ | $\unit{K}$ | Temperature | Absolute temperature of the system. |
| ${\thermal{T}}_c$ | $\unit{K}$ | Critical temperature | Temperature of the stated phase transition. |
| ${\mechanical{P}}$ | $\unit{Pa}$ | Pressure | Thermodynamic pressure. |
| ${\mechanical{P}}_c$ | $\unit{Pa}$ | Critical pressure | Pressure at the liquid–gas critical point. |
