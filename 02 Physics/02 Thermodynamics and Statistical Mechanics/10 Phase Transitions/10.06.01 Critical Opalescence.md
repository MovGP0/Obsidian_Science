> [!TLDR]
> Large density fluctuations scatter light strongly near the liquid–gas critical point.

The physical question is why a nearly uniform fluid can scatter so much light. Density fluctuations change its local optical response. Scattering at a chosen angle probes a wave-vector transfer, so it samples a spatial Fourier component of the density correlations. This is a weak-scattering equilibrium interpretation; strong multiple scattering can complicate a direct intensity-to-correlation comparison.

Near the liquid–gas critical point, a fluid can become strongly turbid to visible light. This is **critical opalescence**, observed by Andrews in 1869. The correlation length of the density fluctuations increases strongly near the transition.

The scattered intensity is proportional to the static structure factor, apart from optical and normalization factors:

$$
I(\mathbf {\signal{q}})\propto S(\mathbf {\signal{q}})=\int d^3{\mechanical{r}}\,e^{i\mathbf {\signal{q}}\cdot\mathbf {\mechanical{r}}}G(\mathbf {\mechanical{r}}).
$$

Here:

$$
G(\mathbf {\mechanical{r}})=\langle\delta{\mechanical{\rho}}(\mathbf {\mechanical{r}})\delta{\mechanical{\rho}}(0)\rangle.
$$

At criticality, the correlation function has a power-law decay instead of an exponential decay. The long-wavelength structure factor is related to the compressibility:

$$
S(0)\propto{\mechanical{\kappa}}_T.
$$

As the compressibility increases, long-wavelength density fluctuations become large and light scattering becomes strong. In this article, $S(\mathbf {\signal{q}})$ denotes a structure factor, not thermodynamic entropy.

The correlation uses density deviations from the equilibrium mean, so it removes the uniform background density. Its Fourier transform weights separations by their relative optical phase. At zero wave-vector transfer all phases agree, making the result sensitive to the spatially integrated correlations. This explains why a growing compressibility can enhance small-angle scattering.

As a normalization check, a correlation of mass-density fluctuations has squared-density units; integrating over three-dimensional separation adds a volume factor. A dimensionless structure factor would require additional density and particle-number normalization. The proportionality statements deliberately leave such factors unspecified and must not be used to extract a numerical compressibility without them. An angle-resolved intensity measurement could test the wave-vector dependence; no optical dataset is supplied here.

The zero-wave-number statement needs an ensemble limit. In a sealed finite sample with fixed mass, the fluctuation of the total mass is exactly zero. Here the compressibility relation refers to the bulk long-wavelength limit, or to fluctuations in an open observation region, rather than an unconstrained total-mass fluctuation of that sealed sample. [Höfling and Dietrich study the structure factor and particle-number fluctuations of such open observation regions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06083), including their finite-size corrections.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $I$ | $\unit{W\,m^{-2}}$ | Scattered intensity | Optical intensity at the specified wave-vector transfer. |
| $G$ | $\unit{kg^2\,m^{-6}}$ | Density correlation | Product average of two mass-density deviations. |
| $S$ | $\unit{kg^2\,m^{-3}}$ | Unnormalized structure factor | Spatial Fourier integral of the displayed density correlation. |
| ${\signal{q}}$ | $\unit{m^{-1}}$ | Scattering wave number | Magnitude of the wave-vector transfer in light scattering. |
| ${\mechanical{r}}$ | $\unit{m}$ | Separation | Distance between the two points of a correlation function. |
| ${\mechanical{\rho}}$ | $\unit{kg\,m^{-3}}$ | Mass density | Mass per volume in the stated phase. |
| ${\mechanical{\kappa}}$ | $\unit{Pa^{-1}}$ | Compressibility | Relative volume response to pressure. |
