> [!TLDR]
> A continuous transition has no jump in the first free-energy derivatives, but higher derivatives can be singular.

The absence of latent heat does not imply that heating produces a weak response. A continuous transition can have continuous entropy while the rate at which entropy changes with temperature becomes singular. The second derivatives below express this distinction for equilibrium bulk matter at fixed amount of substance.

Continuous transitions are often called *second-order transitions*. First derivatives of the Gibbs free energy are continuous. Second derivatives can be discontinuous or divergent. For example:

$$
{\thermal{C}}_P=-{\thermal{T}}\left(\frac{\partial^2{\energy{G}}}{\partial {\thermal{T}}^2}\right)_P,\qquad
{\mechanical{\kappa}}_T=-\frac1{\mechanical{V}}\left(\frac{\partial^2{\energy{G}}}{\partial {\mechanical{P}}^2}\right)_T.
$$

A magnetic susceptibility is also obtained from a second derivative with respect to the conjugate magnetic field.

$$
{\magnetic{\chi}}_T\propto-\left(\frac{\partial^2{\energy{G}}}{\partial {\magnetic{H}}^2}\right)_T.
$$

The proportionality factor depends on whether free energy and magnetization are total or per volume, and on the magnetic unit convention.

There is no latent heat. The order parameter approaches zero continuously. Fluctuations become critical and the correlation length diverges in the usual continuous critical transition.

**Remark 10.1.** The historical name *second order* can be misleading. The two-dimensional Ising model has a logarithmically divergent heat capacity, conventionally described by the exponent $\alpha=0$. Helium-4 has $\alpha\approx-0.013$ and a finite heat-capacity cusp. The preferred general term is *continuous transition*.

Check the pressure formula on a stable single-phase branch. Increasing pressure at fixed temperature reduces volume, so the second pressure derivative of the Gibbs free energy is negative and the compressibility is positive. Its dimensions are inverse pressure. The heat-capacity expression similarly has units of energy per kelvin. These checks detect a wrong sign or a missing volume factor without using critical exponents.

To distinguish a finite jump from a cusp or divergence, one needs response data on both sides of the transition and control of finite-size rounding. No such dataset is included here; the quoted exponent examples specify different possible singular forms, rather than fits performed in this note.
