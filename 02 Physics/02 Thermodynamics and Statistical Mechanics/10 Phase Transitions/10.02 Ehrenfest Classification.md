> [!TLDR]
> The Ehrenfest classification uses the first thermodynamic derivative that becomes nonanalytic.

The physical question is which equilibrium response first changes non-smoothly when a control parameter crosses a phase boundary. Choose the potential whose natural variables match the controls. At fixed temperature and pressure, the Gibbs free energy is appropriate; its first derivatives determine entropy and volume. A discontinuity in either derivative therefore has a direct calorimetric or volumetric meaning.

The classification concerns the equilibrium bulk free energy, not a transient jump during a rapid sweep. It also requires a specified path through the phase diagram: crossing a coexistence line and approaching its endpoint need not produce the same singular behavior.

Paul Ehrenfest proposed this classification in 1933.

**Definition 10.1 — Ehrenfest classification.** A transition has order $n$ if derivatives of a thermodynamic potential, such as the Gibbs free energy, are continuous below order $n$, while a derivative of order $n$ is discontinuous or singular.


Use the first-order note to connect a derivative jump to latent heat. Then compare the continuous-transition note, where no such first-derivative jump occurs, with the limitations note. A heat-capacity peak alone does not identify a finite Ehrenfest order: one must determine whether it is a finite jump, a divergence, or rounding in a finite sample. This is a classification of thermodynamic behavior, not a microscopic explanation of why the phases exist.
