> [!TLDR]
> The epsilon expansion calculates critical exponents near four spatial dimensions.

The method replaces an uncontrolled expansion at a strongly fluctuating three-dimensional critical point by an expansion near four dimensions, where the interacting fixed point is weakly coupled. The dimension is continued as a mathematical parameter; no fractional-dimensional specimen is assumed. The formulas concern short-range models with an n-component order parameter near this fixed point.

Wilson and Fisher developed an expansion in the distance from the upper critical dimension:

$$
\epsilon=4-d.
$$

For an $n$-component order parameter, the leading results are

$$
\nu=\frac12+\frac{n+2}{4(n+8)}\epsilon+O(\epsilon^2),
$$

$$
\eta=\frac{n+2}{2(n+8)^2}\epsilon^2+O(\epsilon^3).
$$

Here $n$ counts field components. It is not a particle density. The parameter $\epsilon$ is dimensionless.

[Wilson and Fisher's original paper](https://journals.aps.org/prl/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevLett.28.240) explicitly formulates the critical-exponent calculation for small epsilon. That is the controlled regime. Setting epsilon to one is an extrapolation and does not make the next omitted term small by definition.

Check the expansion at zero epsilon: the correlation-length exponent returns to its Gaussian value of one half and the anomalous exponent vanishes. The anomalous exponent starts at second order, so a first-order calculation cannot determine a nonzero value for it. When using scaling relations with truncated series, expand the combined result to the same order; otherwise products generate selected higher-order terms without their missing partners.

For the Ising model, $n=1$. In three dimensions, $\epsilon=1$, so the first-order estimate is

$$
\nu\simeq\frac12+\frac{3}{4\cdot9}
=\frac12+\frac1{12}\simeq0.583.
$$

The source compares this with $\nu\simeq0.630$. The difference shows the limit of the leading approximation at $\epsilon=1$. Higher-order terms and resummation improve the estimate.
