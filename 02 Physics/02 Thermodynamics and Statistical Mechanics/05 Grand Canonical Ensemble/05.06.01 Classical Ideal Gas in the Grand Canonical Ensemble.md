> [!TLDR]
> The classical ideal-gas grand partition function is exponential and gives an exact Poisson particle-number distribution.

Write the canonical partition function as:

$$
Z_N=\frac1{{\mechanical{N}}!}\left(\frac {\mechanical{V}}{{\signal{\lambda}}_T^3}\right)^{\mechanical{N}}
=\frac{\xi^{\mechanical{N}}}{{\mechanical{N}}!},\qquad \xi=\frac {\mechanical{V}}{{\signal{\lambda}}_T^3}.
$$

Insert this into Eq. (5.6). The exponential series sums exactly:

$$
\Xi=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\frac{(z\xi)^{\mechanical{N}}}{{\mechanical{N}}!}=e^{z\xi},\qquad
\ln\Xi=\frac{z{\mechanical{V}}}{{\signal{\lambda}}_T^3}.
$$

## Mean particle number and pressure

$$
\langle {\mechanical{N}}\rangle=z\frac{\partial\ln\Xi}{\partial z}=\frac{z{\mechanical{V}}}{{\signal{\lambda}}_T^3},
\qquad z={\mechanical{n}}{\signal{\lambda}}_T^3,\qquad {\mechanical{n}}=\frac{\langle {\mechanical{N}}\rangle}{{\mechanical{V}}}.
$$

Since $z=e^{\beta{\energy{\mu}}}$, this reproduces Eq. (5.2). The pressure is:

$$
{\mechanical{P}}=\frac{{\thermal{k}_{B}} {\thermal{T}}}{{\mechanical{V}}}\ln\Xi=\frac{{\thermal{k}_{B}} {\thermal{T}}z}{{\signal{\lambda}}_T^3}
=\frac{\langle {\mechanical{N}}\rangle {\thermal{k}_{B}} {\thermal{T}}}{{\mechanical{V}}}.
$$

## Mean energy

Hold fugacity fixed during the inverse-temperature derivative. Since ${\signal{\lambda}}_T^{-3}\propto\beta^{-3/2}$:

$$
\langle {\energy{E}}\rangle=-\left(\frac{\partial\ln\Xi}{\partial\beta}\right)_{V,z}
=\frac{3}{2\beta}\frac{z{\mechanical{V}}}{{\signal{\lambda}}_T^3}
=\frac32\langle {\mechanical{N}}\rangle {\thermal{k}_{B}} {\thermal{T}}.
$$

## Probability distribution of particle number

$$
p({\mechanical{N}})=\frac{e^{\beta{\energy{\mu}} {\mechanical{N}}}Z_N}{\Xi}
=e^{-\langle {\mechanical{N}}\rangle}\frac{\langle {\mechanical{N}}\rangle^{\mechanical{N}}}{{\mechanical{N}}!}.
$$

This is the **Poisson distribution**, with variance equal to its mean.

**Example 5.2 — Poisson probability.** For $\langle {\mechanical{N}}\rangle=100$:

$$
p(110)=e^{-100}\frac{100^{110}}{110!}\approx0.0234,
\qquad \Delta {\mechanical{N}}=\sqrt{100}=10.
$$

Thus 110 particles are exactly one standard deviation above the mean. For mean particle number $10^{23}$, the same relative deviation of 10% has a negligible probability.

The factorial is essential: it counts indistinguishable classical particles once. Removing it changes the exponential sum to a geometric one and destroys the Poisson law. Conversely, the exponential sum is exact within the classical dilute model; it does not make the classical approximation exact for a degenerate quantum gas or a box with sparse translational levels.

For normalization, sum the number probabilities above using the exponential series; the result is one. In the very dilute small-volume limit, the vacuum sector dominates and one-particle events give the leading correction. The quoted probability for 110 is for exactly that count, not the probability of the whole upper tail. A proposed test would compare repeated equilibrium counts in one fixed observation volume with the predicted mean and variance; correlated samples need enough time between observations to estimate that distribution reliably.
