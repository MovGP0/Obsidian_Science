> [!TLDR]
> The Ising model describes interacting binary spins and provides a basic model for phase transitions and critical phenomena.

The Ising model is a basic model for the study of phase transitions and critical phenomena. Wilhelm Lenz introduced it in 1920. His student Ernst Ising solved the one-dimensional model in 1925. Lars Onsager found an exact solution in two dimensions in 1944. The model remains a basic test for analytical and numerical methods.

Its uses extend beyond magnetism. With suitable changes in interpretation, it describes binary metallic alloys, Hopfield neural networks, lattice gases, and protein folding. This chapter covers the model definition, exact solutions in low dimensions, the mean-field approximation, and the related critical phenomena.

The central question is how a local preference for parallel spins can produce a macroscopic magnetization. Thermal disorder competes with this preference. The model keeps only two orientations per site and nearest-neighbour interactions; it does not describe every feature of a real magnet.

Start with the Hamiltonian and its symmetry in [[11.01 Model Definition]]. Then use [[11.02 Ising Model in One Dimension]] as an exact check: a local alignment preference alone does not guarantee a transition at positive temperature. Compare that result with [[11.03 Mean-Field Approximation]], which neglects correlated fluctuations and predicts a transition even for the chain. The exact square-lattice results in [[11.04 Ising Model in Two Dimensions]] show which mean-field predictions survive and which critical exponents change. Finally, [[11.05 Spontaneous Symmetry Breaking]] explains why the infinite-system limit must precede removal of the selecting field.

Use [[11.07 Worked Examples]] to practise derivatives and limiting cases. Before moving to [[11.06 Extensions of the Ising Model]], identify what changes when a spin has more than two possible orientations. These extensions require a new state space, rather than a change of lattice coordination alone.
