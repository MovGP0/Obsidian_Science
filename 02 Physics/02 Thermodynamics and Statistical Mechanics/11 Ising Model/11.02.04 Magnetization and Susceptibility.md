> [!TLDR]
> The one-dimensional chain has zero spontaneous magnetization and an exponentially increasing zero-field susceptibility as temperature tends to zero.

The magnetization per spin follows from the field derivative of the free energy:

The derivative holds temperature and exchange coupling fixed. Its minus sign follows from the field term in the Hamiltonian: a positive field lowers the energy of positive spins. This magnetization is the mean normalized spin, rather than a magnetic moment per volume.

$$
\magnetic{m}=-\frac{\partial\energy{f}}{\partial\energy{h}}
=\frac{e^{\thermal{\beta}\energy{J}}\sinh(\thermal{\beta}\energy{h})}
{\sqrt{e^{2\thermal{\beta}\energy{J}}\sinh^2(\thermal{\beta}\energy{h})
+e^{-2\thermal{\beta}\energy{J}}}}.
$$

At $\energy{h}=0$, $\magnetic{m}=0$ for every $\thermal{T}>0$. There is no spontaneous magnetization.

The expression is odd in field and has magnitude below one. At $\energy{J}=0$, it reduces to $\magnetic{m}=\tanh(\thermal{\beta}\energy{h})$, the independent-spin result. At fixed nonzero field and vanishing temperature, a ferromagnetic chain saturates with the sign of the field. Thus the low-temperature and zero-field limits need not commute.

The zero-field susceptibility is

$$
\magnetic{\chi}=\left.\frac{\partial\magnetic{m}}{\partial\energy{h}}\right|_{h=0}
=\thermal{\beta}e^{2\thermal{\beta}\energy{J}}.
$$

For a ferromagnetic coupling, it diverges exponentially as $\thermal{T}\to0$. The source calls this precursor of the zero-temperature transition a **critical crossover**.

An increasing susceptibility means that a smaller field can produce an appreciable response; it does not imply nonzero magnetization at exactly zero field and positive temperature. Linear response uses the slope at zero field and cannot be extended to arbitrarily large fields, because the normalized magnetization must saturate. At zero coupling the slope is $\thermal{\beta}$, which checks its inverse-energy unit and its sign.

> [!note] Field convention
> Here $\energy{h}$ has units of energy. Thus $\magnetic{\chi}$ has units of inverse energy. A susceptibility defined with respect to a magnetic field in SI units requires the corresponding magnetic-moment factors.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\magnetic{m}$ | $1$ | Magnetization per spin | The mean normalized spin; subscripts identify particular or spontaneous values. |
| $\energy{f}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Free energy per spin | The Helmholtz free energy per spin; subscript 0 marks a reference value. |
| $\energy{h}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Field parameter | The applied field multiplied by the spin magnetic moment. |
| $\thermal{\beta}$ | $\unit{J^{-1}}$ | Inverse temperature | The inverse of the thermal energy. |
| $\energy{J}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Exchange coupling | The energy scale of a nearest-neighbour interaction. |
| $\thermal{T}$ | $\unit{K}$ | Temperature | The absolute temperature; a c subscript marks a critical value. |
| $\magnetic{\chi}$ | $\unit{J^{-1}}$ | Susceptibility per spin | The derivative of magnetization with respect to the field energy. |
