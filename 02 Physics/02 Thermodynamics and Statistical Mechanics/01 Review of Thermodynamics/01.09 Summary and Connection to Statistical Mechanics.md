> [!TLDR]
> Statistical mechanics explains thermodynamic quantities through microscopic states, probabilities, and partition functions. It connects energy conservation, entropy growth, and low-temperature behavior to microscopic physics.

The following table summarizes the chapter's key concepts and the statistical descriptions developed in later chapters.

**Table 1.2** Correspondence between thermodynamic quantities and statistical mechanics.

| Thermodynamics | Statistical mechanics |
| --- | --- |
| Internal energy $\energy{U}$ | Ensemble mean energy $\langle\energy{E}\rangle$ over microstates. |
| Entropy $\thermal{S}$ | $\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\ln\Omega$ (Boltzmann), or $-\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\sum_i p_i\ln p_i$ (Gibbs). |
| Temperature $\thermal{T}$ | Controls the energy distribution. |
| Pressure $\mechanical{P}$ | $\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}(\partial\ln Z/\partial\mechanical{V})_{\thermal{T},\mechanical{N}}$. |
| Helmholtz free energy $\energy{F}$ | $-\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}\ln Z$ in the canonical ensemble. |
| Grand potential $\energy{\Phi}=-\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}$ | $-\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}\ln\Xi$ in the grand canonical ensemble. |
| $\thermal{C}_V\geq0$ | Energy variance $\langle\energy{E}^{\,2}\rangle-\langle\energy{E}\rangle^2\geq0$. |

The thermodynamic laws were introduced here as phenomenological postulates. Statistical mechanics gives them a microscopic explanation:

- The **first law** is conservation of Hamiltonian energy.
- The **second law** and increasing entropy reflect the statistical tendency toward more probable macrostates. The source refers to the Boltzmann $H$-theorem and Chapter 3 in this connection.
- The **third law** is connected to the quantum energy spectrum. As $\thermal{T}\to0$, the system approaches its ground state.

> [!note] Scope of the summary
> The microscopic first-law statement assumes an isolated system with a time-independent Hamiltonian. The ground-state argument for zero limiting entropy also needs the degeneracy qualification in [[01.04 Third Law of Thermodynamics]]. Discreteness of the spectrum alone does not exclude residual entropy.

The dictionary has conditions. The partition function $Z$ uses a fixed-temperature, fixed-volume, fixed-particle-number canonical model; $\Xi$ permits particle exchange at fixed chemical potential. The pressure derivative keeps the microscopic Hamiltonian model and the other ensemble controls fixed. The bulk identity $\energy{\Phi}=-\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}$ uses extensivity and can need extra terms when surface effects are important.

Also distinguish two entropy descriptions. For equally probable accessible states, put $p_i=1/\Omega$ into the Gibbs sum in Table 1.2. The sum has $\Omega$ equal terms and gives the Boltzmann formula below. For unequal probabilities the simple count alone is insufficient. Since a probability and a state count are dimensionless, the Boltzmann constant supplies the entropy unit.

An especially useful result is the **Boltzmann formula**:

$$
\thermal{S}=\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\ln\Omega,
$$

where $\Omega$ is the number of microstates, or microscopic configurations, compatible with the thermodynamic macrostate. It explains why entropy tends to increase in spontaneous processes. Macrostates with more compatible microstates are more probable, so the system is statistically more likely to enter them.

A single accessible microstate gives zero Boltzmann entropy; independent systems have multiplying state counts and therefore additive entropies. These checks explain the logarithm's role. They do not prove that every individual microscopic trajectory has monotonically increasing entropy. The macroscopic second law needs a statistical description, accessible-state assumptions, and a distinction between typical behavior and fluctuations. Nor does the equilibrium dictionary by itself give a relaxation timescale.

This formula is engraved on Boltzmann's tombstone at Vienna's Central Cemetery. The source presents it as one of the most beautiful equations of theoretical physics.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\energy{U},\energy{E}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Internal and microscopic energies | Thermodynamic energy and energy of an ensemble microstate. |
| $\thermal{S}$ | $\unit{J\,K^{-1}}$ | Entropy | Statistical measure of accessible microstates or their probability distribution. |
| $\thermal{T}$ | $\unit{K}$ | Temperature | Thermal variable controlling energy probabilities. |
| $\mechanical{P}$ | $\unit{Pa}$ | Pressure | Thermodynamic variable obtained from a partition-function derivative. |
| $\mechanical{V}$ | $\unit{m^3}$ | Volume | Variable conjugate to pressure. |
| $\mechanical{N}$ | $1$ | Particle number | Particle count held fixed in the canonical pressure derivative. |
| $\energy{F}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Helmholtz free energy | Canonical potential determined by the partition function. |
| $\energy{\Phi}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Grand potential | Grand canonical potential, equal to minus pressure times volume for the homogeneous bulk system. |
| $\thermal{C}_V$ | $\unit{J\,K^{-1}}$ | Constant-volume heat capacity | Response related to nonnegative canonical energy variance. |
| $\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}$ | $\unit{J\,K^{-1}}$ | Boltzmann constant | Converts statistical quantities to entropy and thermal energy. |
| $p_i$ | $1$ | Microstate probability | Probability assigned to microstate i in Gibbs entropy. |
