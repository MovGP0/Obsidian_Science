> [!TLDR]
> A Legendre transformation replaces a variable by its conjugate derivative. In thermodynamics, this changes the natural variables of an energy potential.

## Definition 1.9 — Legendre transformation

Let $f(x)$ be a convex function. Its **Legendre transform** is:

$$
g(p)=\sup_x\left[px-f(x)\right].
$$

At a differentiable interior maximizer, $p=f'(x)$ is the **conjugate variable**. The transformation is an involution under the usual closed-convex assumptions:

$$
\mathcal L[\mathcal L[f]]=f.
$$

At a smooth point where $p=f'(x)$ can be inverted, differentiate the stationary expression $g=px-f$:

$$
\mathrm dg=x\,\mathrm dp+p\,\mathrm dx-f'(x)\,\mathrm dx=x\,\mathrm dp.
$$

Thus $g'(p)=x$: the derivative records the old variable. Strict convexity makes this local inversion unambiguous. A linear segment or a nondifferentiable point needs the supremum formulation rather than a single inverse derivative.

The thermodynamic sign convention is easy to miss. At fixed volume, the mathematical transform of internal energy with respect to entropy is temperature times entropy minus internal energy. Helmholtz free energy is its negative. The conventional differential still makes temperature the new natural variable. At phase coexistence, the derivative map can cease to be one-to-one, so the smooth inverse construction must be used with care.

In thermodynamics, Legendre transformations are applied to $\energy{U}(\thermal{S},\mechanical{V})$. They give potentials with natural variables $(\thermal{T},\mechanical{V})$, $(\thermal{S},\mechanical{P})$, or $(\thermal{T},\mechanical{P})$. Together with internal energy, these are the four fundamental thermodynamic potentials.

> [!note] Mathematical scope
> The source gives $p=f'(x)$ and the involution property without stating their regularity assumptions. The supremum definition also applies when a derivative does not exist. The thermodynamic potentials below use the conventional signs for replacing the relevant conjugate variables.
