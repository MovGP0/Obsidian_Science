> [!TLDR]
> A system is the part of the universe selected for study. Its boundary determines whether it can exchange heat, work, or matter with its surroundings.

In thermodynamics, a **system** is the part of the universe selected for study. The rest is called the **surroundings**, or a **reservoir**, depending on the context. The surface that separates the system from its surroundings is its **wall** or **boundary**.

## Definition 1.1 — Types of thermodynamic systems

A thermodynamic system is:

- **Isolated** if it exchanges neither energy nor matter with its surroundings.
- **Closed** if it can exchange energy as heat or work, but no matter.
- **Open** if it can exchange both energy and matter.

The surroundings are not necessarily a reservoir. An ideal reservoir is large enough that the permitted exchange does not appreciably change its relevant intensive properties. A small second body generally changes temperature when it supplies heat.

An adiabatic wall alone does not make a system isolated: a movable insulated piston can still transfer work. Conversely, a rigid wall prevents pressure–volume work but can permit electrical work through leads. Classify all permitted transfers before selecting a model.

The system type helps determine which thermodynamic potential is most useful for equilibrium and which statistical ensemble is appropriate for a microscopic description. For an isolated equilibrium system with fixed energy, volume, and particle number, the microcanonical ensemble is appropriate. Thermal contact with a heat bath at fixed volume and particle number leads to the canonical ensemble. Contact with a heat and particle reservoir at fixed volume leads to the grand canonical ensemble. A general open system with sustained flows need not be in any equilibrium ensemble. The source introduces this connection here and develops it later, with the canonical treatment in Chapter 4.

Walls are classified by what they permit:

- **Adiabatic walls** prevent heat transfer. A Dewar vessel provides an approximate example.
- **Diathermal walls** permit heat transfer until thermal equilibrium is reached.
- **Rigid walls** prevent volume changes and therefore prevent pressure–volume work.
- **Movable walls**, such as pistons, permit volume changes and pressure–volume work.
- **Permeable walls** permit molecules of one or more chemical species to pass through them.
