> [!TLDR]
> At fixed internal energy, volume, and particle number, an isolated system reaches equilibrium when its entropy is maximal.

One powerful statement of the second law is the **principle of maximum entropy**.

## Theorem 1.5 — Principle of maximum entropy

Consider all states accessible to an isolated system with fixed internal energy $\energy{U}$, volume $\mechanical{V}$, and particle number $\mechanical{N}$. The **equilibrium state** is the state that maximizes entropy $\thermal{S}$.

For every spontaneous process in an isolated system:

$$
\mathrm d\thermal{S}\geq0.
$$

The comparison is over states compatible with the constraints and with any internal barriers that remain in place. An internal partition can prevent energy, volume, or particle exchange; removing one of these constraints enlarges the set of accessible states. The entropy maximum then determines the new equilibrium, rather than requiring each subsystem's energy to remain fixed.

For example, place two bodies at different temperatures inside one rigid, insulated enclosure and let them exchange heat. Their total internal energy remains fixed. Heat transfer stops when the total entropy is stationary with respect to moving a small amount of energy between them; stable equilibrium selects a maximum. This yields equal temperatures. The entropy of the hotter body can decrease while the total entropy increases.

A local maximum can describe a metastable state. Establishing the global equilibrium also requires comparison with other accessible states; entropy maximization does not give the relaxation time.

Statistical mechanics explains this principle. Equilibrium corresponds to the macrostate of greatest probability. Entropy is proportional to the logarithm of the number of microstates compatible with that macrostate, up to an additive constant. This connection is developed through the Boltzmann formula in Chapter 3.
