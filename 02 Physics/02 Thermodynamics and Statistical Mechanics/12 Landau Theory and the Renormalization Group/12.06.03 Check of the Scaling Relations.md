> [!TLDR]
> Representative critical exponents satisfy the scaling relations within the rounding used in the source.

**Table 12.1** Critical exponents for several systems. For mean-field theory, hyperscaling uses the effective dimension $d_{\mathrm{eff}}=4$.

| System | $\alpha$ | $\beta$ | $\gamma$ | $\delta$ | $\nu$ | $\eta$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mean field (Landau) | $0$ | $1/2$ | $1$ | $3$ | $1/2$ | $0$ |
| Ising 2D (exact) | $0$ (logarithmic) | $1/8$ | $7/4$ | $15$ | $1$ | $1/4$ |
| Ising 3D (numerical) | $0.11$ | $0.33$ | $1.24$ | $4.79$ | $0.63$ | $0.04$ |
| $O(3)$ in 3D (Heisenberg) | $-0.12$ | $0.37$ | $1.40$ | $4.78$ | $0.71$ | $0.04$ |

The two-dimensional Ising heat capacity diverges logarithmically.

For the rounded three-dimensional Ising values,

$$
\alpha+2\beta+\gamma=0.11+0.66+1.24=2.01\approx2.
$$

This verifies Rushbrooke scaling to the precision of the listed values.

> [!note] Source precision
> These numerical entries reproduce the source values. They are approximate and rounded, so the scaling relations need not hold exactly for the printed decimals.

Use the exact rows first. For the two-dimensional Ising values, Rushbrooke gives $0+2/8+7/4=2$, and Fisher gives $1(2-1/4)=7/4$. These are exact arithmetic checks of the quoted exponents, not an independent derivation of them. For mean field, the effective dimension in the caption is a way to check Josephson at four dimensions; it does not change the physical spatial dimension of a sample.

For the printed three-dimensional Ising decimals, Fisher gives $0.63(2-0.04)=1.2348$ compared with $1.24$, and Josephson compares $1.89$ with $2-0.11=1.89$. The small Fisher residual is compatible with rounding of the inputs. If each two-decimal input was rounded to the nearest hundredth, its rounding interval is one half of one hundredth; this is a statement about printed precision, not an experimental error bar. Exponent agreement cannot establish the universality class unless the equilibrium regime and assumptions also match.
