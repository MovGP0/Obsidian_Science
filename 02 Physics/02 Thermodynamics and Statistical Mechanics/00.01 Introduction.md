> [!TLDR]
> Statistical mechanics connects microscopic laws with macroscopic thermodynamic properties. This text develops the tools for equilibrium systems, quantum statistics, and phase transitions.

Statistical mechanics is one of the foundations of modern physics. It provides the conceptual and mathematical connection between the microscopic description of physical systems and their observable macroscopic properties. Classical mechanics and quantum mechanics determine the microscopic description. Thermodynamics studies the macroscopic properties.

Consider a gas in a rigid container that can exchange heat with a large environment. Why can we predict its pressure or heat capacity without knowing every molecular trajectory? Statistical mechanics replaces that missing microscopic information with a probability distribution constrained by the preparation of the system. For this example, the canonical ensemble describes equilibrium at the bath temperature, with fixed volume and particle number. The gas energy can fluctuate. See [David Tong, Statistical Physics, Sections 1.1–1.3](https://www.damtp.cam.ac.uk/user/tong/statphys/statmechhtml/S1.html).

This text aims to give a systematic, rigorous, and accessible explanation of the foundations of equilibrium statistical mechanics. Its structure follows an established approach in university teaching. It starts with a review of thermodynamics to establish the terminology and reference quantities. It then introduces the basic concepts of phase space, statistical entropy, and ensembles.

The treatment of the microcanonical, canonical, and grand canonical ensembles covers both the formal principles and their practical applications. Worked examples and exercises support each step. The text then moves to quantum statistics. Chapters on the Fermi–Dirac and Bose–Einstein distributions explain their main physical consequences. These applications range from the free electron gas to Bose–Einstein condensation, and from blackbody radiation to the Debye model of solids.

The final part of the book studies phase transitions. It covers the van der Waals equation, the Ising model, Landau theory, and the basic principles of the renormalization group.

The appendices summarize the main mathematical tools used in the text.

## What the model must specify

An equilibrium calculation needs both microscopic physics and statistical assumptions. A Hamiltonian specifies the energies and interactions in the model. The ensemble specifies the constraints and the probability weights. Choosing an ideal gas, for example, omits interparticle interactions; choosing equilibrium omits the process by which the gas relaxes. Neither choice follows from algebra alone.

This text assumes basic classical mechanics, elementary quantum states and energy levels, multivariable calculus, and probability. You should be able to differentiate a function of several state variables, normalize a distribution, and calculate an average. The appendices supply more specialized integration and counting methods when they first become necessary.

Use the ideal gas as a repeated check as you move between ensembles. Under the appropriate large-system conditions, predictions for the same equilibrium state should agree. Fluctuations and finite-size effects need a separate comparison; agreement of mean values does not make the distributions identical.

To test an application, first identify an observable such as pressure or heat capacity and the conditions held fixed in its measurement. Then compare the model prediction with data taken under those conditions. No dataset is supplied in this introduction. These comparisons are a method for testing the later models, not a report of measured agreement. For systems driven far from equilibrium, an equilibrium ensemble alone does not determine transport or relaxation.

The presentation keeps mathematical rigor and physical intuition together. It explains the reason for each important result and derives each formula through its main steps. Many figures help the reader visualize the concepts.
