> [!TLDR]
> The exclusion principle determines the ground state, thermal response, and degeneracy pressure of an ideal Fermi gas.

The ideal Fermi gas is an important model in modern physics. The Pauli exclusion principle prevents two identical fermions from occupying the same complete quantum state. This produces macroscopic results that differ strongly from classical statistics, even at very low temperature.

Conduction electrons in metals, nucleons in nuclei, electrons in white dwarfs, and neutrons in neutron stars are examples of degenerate fermionic systems. The ideal gas supplies a useful starting model; interactions are important in several of these applications.

The central question is why a gas can have pressure and kinetic energy when thermal motion has reached its minimum. For identical fermions, the answer starts with the available states, not with classical equipartition. First count the states in the box, then fill them subject to exclusion. The occupied momentum range sets the energy scale for every later calculation.

Read the chapter in three stages. Use [[07.02 Density of States and Total Energy at T = 0]] to connect state counting to energy and pressure. Next use [[07.03 Fermi Gas at Finite Temperature - Sommerfeld Expansion]] and [[07.04 Electronic Specific Heat]] to see why only states near the occupation boundary change at low temperature. Finally compare the magnetic, transport, and stellar applications: they share exclusion statistics, but they need different additional assumptions about fields, scattering, and gravity. A successful ideal-gas calculation in one application does not establish those additional assumptions in another.

This chapter starts at zero temperature, adds finite-temperature corrections with the Sommerfeld expansion, and then examines important applications. Start with [[07.01 Fermi Gas at Zero Temperature]]. The chapter ends with [[07.10 Summary]] and [[07.99 Exercises]].
