> [!TLDR]
> The Sommerfeld model predicts a thermal-to-electrical conductivity ratio proportional to temperature.

The electronic thermal conductivity in the simple isotropic relaxation-time model is

$$
\begin{aligned}
{\thermal{\kappa}}_e&=\frac13{\thermal{c}}_V^{({\electric{e}})}{\mechanical{v}}_F{\mechanical{\ell}}_F
=\frac13\frac{\pi^2{\mechanical{n}}{\thermal{k}}_B^2{\thermal{T}}}{2{\energy{\varepsilon}}_F}\,{\mechanical{v}}_F^2{\semiconductor{\tau}}\\
&=\frac{\pi^2{\mechanical{n}}{\thermal{k}}_B^2{\thermal{T}}{\semiconductor{\tau}}}{3{\mechanical{m}}_e}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The factor one third is the angular average for an isotropic three-dimensional velocity distribution. The heat capacity counts the states able to carry a thermal perturbation; the squared Fermi speed and relaxation time set how far that perturbation moves before relaxation. Use the heat capacity per volume here, because the resulting heat conductivity is a bulk quantity.

Combine this with ${\electric{\sigma}}={\mechanical{n}}{\electric{e}}^2{\semiconductor{\tau}}/{\mechanical{m}}_e$. The **Lorenz number** is

$$
{\thermal{L}}_0=\frac{{\thermal{\kappa}}_e}{{\electric{\sigma}} {\thermal{T}}}=\frac{\pi^2{\thermal{k}}_B^2}{3{\electric{e}}^2}
\approx2.44\times10^{-8}\,\unit{W\,\Omega\,K^{-2}}.
$$

The cancellation of density, mass, and relaxation time gives the Wiedemann–Franz law in this model. The source quotes the following representative metal data:

| Metal | κₑ (W m⁻¹ K⁻¹) | σ (S m⁻¹) | L (10⁻⁸ W Ω K⁻²) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cu | 401 | 6.0 × 10⁷ | 2.23 |
| Ag | 429 | 6.2 × 10⁷ | 2.31 |
| Au | 317 | 4.5 × 10⁷ | 2.35 |
| Al | 237 | 3.7 × 10⁷ | 2.14 |
| Theory | — | — | 2.44 |

**Remark 7.3 — Conditions of use.** The law is often a good approximation near room temperature and in the low-temperature elastic-scattering limit of normal metals. At intermediate temperatures, inelastic phonon scattering can relax heat and charge currents differently, and the ratio can deviate.

> [!note] Data interpretation
> The source table's ratios correspond approximately to 300 K. The theoretical ratio refers to electronic heat transport. A measured total thermal conductivity can contain a lattice contribution, so direct comparison requires care.

The cancellation requires the same effective relaxation-time description for heat and charge transport in the narrow energy range that contributes. It is not an independent law for arbitrary scattering. As a source-data check, dividing the copper entry 401 by the product of its electrical conductivity and 300 K gives about $2.23\times10^{-8}\,\unit{W\,\Omega\,K^{-2}}$, as tabulated. Compare the electronic heat conductivity with the prediction; adding lattice heat transport changes the measured ratio without changing this electronic derivation.
