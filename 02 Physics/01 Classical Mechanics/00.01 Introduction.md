> [!TLDR]
> Classical mechanics describes the motion of bodies and provides the concepts of phase space, action, and symmetry that support quantum mechanics and field theory.

Classical mechanics was the first modern physical theory. It still provides the framework for most observed macroscopic phenomena. Galileo, Newton, Euler, Lagrange, Hamilton, and Jacobi developed it over three centuries. The theory describes the motion of bodies, gives precise predictions, and introduces phase space, action, and continuous symmetries. Quantum mechanics and field theory build on these concepts.

The book presents classical mechanics as one subject for two groups of readers. For students in their first university courses in physics, mathematics, or engineering, it covers the standard Physics I curriculum. The topics range from kinematics to rigid-body dynamics and include oscillations, collisions, gravitation, and fluids. For students in a second, advanced course, it develops analytical mechanics. It covers Hamilton's principle, the Euler–Lagrange and Hamilton equations, Poisson brackets, canonical transformations, the Hamilton–Jacobi equation, and normal modes.

The mathematical tools require the preparation of a standard first university course. They include differential calculus, ordinary differential equations, linear algebra, and eigenvalue problems.

The book has seven parts.

**Part I** (Chapter 1) develops the kinematics of a point particle. It covers position, velocity, acceleration, motion along straight and curved paths, circular motion, and relative motion.

**Part II** (Chapters 2 and 3) introduces Newton's three laws, fundamental forces, work, kinetic and potential energy, and conservation of mechanical energy.

**Part III** (Chapters 4 to 8) presents the main systems of vector mechanics. It covers oscillators, collisions and momentum, gravitation and motion under central forces, rigid bodies, and fluids.

**Part IV** (Chapter 9) introduces constraints, generalized coordinates, virtual work, and d’Alembert’s equation in symbolic form.

**Part V** (Chapters 10 and 11) develops Lagrangian mechanics. It covers the Euler–Lagrange equations, Hamilton's principle, continuous symmetries, and Noether's theorem.

**Part VI** (Chapters 12 and 13) develops Hamiltonian mechanics. It covers the Legendre transformation, Hamilton's equations, Poisson brackets, phase space, canonical transformations, and the Hamilton–Jacobi equation.

**Part VII** (Chapter 14) covers small oscillations and normal modes. It also introduces nonlinear systems and chaos.

Each chapter ends with a summary and exercises at three levels: basic, intermediate, and advanced. All exercises give a final result. [[15.00 Complete Solutions to the Exercises|Chapter 15]] gives complete solutions and repeats each problem before its solution.

Parts I to III form a complete, self-contained Physics I course based on Newton's vector equations and conservation principles. Part IV covers constraints and d’Alembert’s principle. Parts V to VII cover the Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formulations and normal modes. These parts develop analytical mechanics. They revisit the same problems in generalized coordinates and show the advantages of the Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formulations. The **Analytical Mechanics** boxes in these chapters show how the results relate to those from the vector methods.
