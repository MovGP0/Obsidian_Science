---
title: "Free-Body Diagram"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Isolate a body and draw the forces that act on it before writing Newton's second law for each axis.

A **free-body diagram** shows all the forces that act on one body. In the point-particle model, the force arrows start at the center of mass. The diagram helps set up the equations in Newtonian dynamics.

1. Isolate the body.
2. List all the forces that act on it.
3. Write each force in components along the selected axes.
4. Apply $\mechanical{\vec F}=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{\vec a}$ to each component.
5. Solve the resulting equations for the unknowns.

### Example — Atwood Machine

Two bodies of masses $\mechanical{m}_1<\mechanical{m}_2$ hang from an ideal cord that passes over a massless pulley. Let $\mechanical{a}$ be the upward acceleration of the lighter body and the downward acceleration of the heavier one.

$$
\mechanical{T}-\mechanical{m}_1\mechanical{g}=\mechanical{m}_1\mechanical{a},
\qquad
\mechanical{m}_2\mechanical{g}-\mechanical{T}=\mechanical{m}_2\mechanical{a}.
$$

Add the equations to find the acceleration. Substitute it into either equation to find the tension.

$$
\mechanical{a}=\frac{(\mechanical{m}_2-\mechanical{m}_1)\mechanical{g}}{\mechanical{m}_1+\mechanical{m}_2},
\qquad
\mechanical{T}=\frac{2\mechanical{m}_1\mechanical{m}_2\mechanical{g}}{\mechanical{m}_1+\mechanical{m}_2}.
$$

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\mechanical{\vec F}$ | $\unit{N}$ | Net force | The sum of the forces acting on the isolated body. |
| $\mechanical{m}, \mechanical{m}_1, \mechanical{m}_2$ | $\unit{kg}$ | Mass | The mass of the body or of either load in the Atwood machine. |
| $\mechanical{\vec a}, \mechanical{a}$ | $\unit{m\,s^{-2}}$ | Acceleration | The acceleration of the isolated body, or its magnitude in the Atwood machine. |
| $\mechanical{T}$ | $\unit{N}$ | Tension | The force transmitted by the cord. |
| $\mechanical{g}$ | $\unit{m\,s^{-2}}$ | Acceleration due to gravity | The magnitude of the downward acceleration in free fall. |
