> [!note] Description
When observed from an inertial reference frame, the net force on a particle is equal to the time rate of change of its linear momentum: 
> $$F = \frac{d}{dt}(mv)$$
> For a body that gains or loses mass (for example a rocket), this form alone is not correct: include the momentum that the added or ejected mass carries (see [[05.08 Variable-Mass Systems and the Rocket Equation]]).

> [!note] Alternate Description
> When mass is constant, force equals mass times acceleration:
> $$F = ma$$ 
> The net force on an object is equal to the mass of the object multiplied by its acceleration:
> $$F_{\text{net}} = \frac{d}{dt} p$$
> $$F = ma$$

The net force on a body is the product of its mass and acceleration.

The acceleration is the response to a force in a given direction:

$$
\vec{F} = m \vec{a}
$$

When the net force is zero, the body is in **translational equilibrium**: its center of mass stays at rest or moves with constant velocity. For full equilibrium of an extended body, the net torque must also be zero.
