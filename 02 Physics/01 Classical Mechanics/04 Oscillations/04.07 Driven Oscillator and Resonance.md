title: "Driven Oscillator and Resonance"
---

> [!TLDR]
> A damped oscillator driven by a sinusoidal force approaches a steady response with frequency-dependent amplitude and phase.

An external harmonic force $\mechanical{F}(t)=\mechanical{F}_0\cos(\signal{\Omega}t)$ gives

$$
\mechanical{\ddot x}+2\signal{\gamma}\mechanical{\dot x}+\signal{\omega}_0^2\mechanical{x}
=\frac{\mechanical{F}_0}{\mechanical{m}}\cos(\signal{\Omega}t).
$$

The general solution is the sum of a transient solution of the homogeneous damped equation and a steady solution. After the transient decays, only the steady response remains.

$$
\mechanical{x}(t)=\mechanical{A}(\signal{\Omega})\cos(\signal{\Omega}t-\signal{\delta}(\signal{\Omega})),
$$

with

$$
\mechanical{A}(\signal{\Omega})=
\frac{\mechanical{F}_0/\mechanical{m}}
{\sqrt{(\signal{\omega}_0^2-\signal{\Omega}^2)^2+4\signal{\gamma}^2\signal{\Omega}^2}},
\qquad
\tan\signal{\delta}=\frac{2\signal{\gamma}\signal{\Omega}}{\signal{\omega}_0^2-\signal{\Omega}^2}.
$$

Choose the phase lag between zero and $\pi$. In the underdamped regime, the transient decays on the timescale $1/\signal{\gamma}$.

**Resonance.** For sufficiently weak damping, the amplitude reaches a maximum at $\signal{\Omega}_r=\sqrt{\signal{\omega}_0^2-2\signal{\gamma}^2}\approx\signal{\omega}_0$. The peak amplitude is approximately

$$
\mechanical{A}_{\max}\approx\frac{\mechanical{F}_0}{2\mechanical{m}\signal{\gamma}\signal{\omega}_0}
=\frac{\mechanical{F}_0\signal{Q}}{\mechanical{m}\signal{\omega}_0^2}
=\frac{\signal{Q}\mechanical{F}_0}{\mechanical{k}}.
$$

A large quality factor $\signal{Q}$ gives a large resonant amplitude.

**Physical meaning.** At $\signal{\Omega}=\signal{\omega}_0$, the external force is in phase with velocity. The average power transferred to the oscillator is greatest there. Resonance occurs in electrical LC circuits, structural vibrations, and spectroscopic absorption.

> [!note] Source wording
> The source calls both effects resonance. With nonzero damping, the displacement peak is at $\signal{\Omega}_r$, and the average power peak is at $\signal{\omega}_0$.

### Example — RLC Circuit

A circuit with resistance $\circuit{R}$, inductance $\circuit{L}$, and capacitance $\circuit{C}$ is driven by voltage $\electric{V}_0\cos(\signal{\Omega}t)$. The capacitor charge $\electric{Q}(t)$ obeys

$$
\circuit{L}\electric{\ddot Q}+\circuit{R}\electric{\dot Q}+\frac{\electric{Q}}{\circuit{C}}
=\electric{V}_0\cos(\signal{\Omega}t).
$$

This has the same form as the mechanical equation, with $\signal{\omega}_0=1/\sqrt{\circuit{L}\circuit{C}}$ and $\signal{\gamma}=\circuit{R}/(2\circuit{L})$. At current resonance, $\signal{\Omega}=\signal{\omega}_0$, the peak current is $\electric{V}_0/\circuit{R}$. It is independent of the individual values of $\circuit{L}$ and $\circuit{C}$ if they give the desired $\signal{\omega}_0$.

## Transient motion and steady response have different origins

After a sinusoidal drive is switched on, write the displacement as the sum of a homogeneous transient and a particular sinusoidal response. The initial displacement and velocity set the transient coefficients. With positive viscous damping the transient decays, leaving motion at the drive frequency. When the drive frequency equals the undamped natural frequency, the steady displacement is one quarter of a period behind the force. The drive is then in phase with the velocity and replenishes the energy lost to damping. With zero damping at exact resonance, the forcing frequency is also a homogeneous mode. A particular solution then has an amplitude envelope that grows linearly with time within the linear model.
