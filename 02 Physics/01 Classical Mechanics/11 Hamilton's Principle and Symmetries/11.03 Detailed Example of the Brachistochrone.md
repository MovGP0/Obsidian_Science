---
title: "Detailed Example of the Brachistochrone"
---

> [!TLDR]
> The brachistochrone is the path of shortest descent time under uniform gravity for a particle released from rest.

Johann Bernoulli posed the brachistochrone problem in 1696. Newton, Leibniz, L’Hôpital, and the Bernoullis solved it.

This problem helped develop the calculus of variations. Given an upper point $A$ and a lower, horizontally displaced point $B$, find the curve $\mechanical{y}(\mechanical{x})$ that a ball starting from rest at $A$ travels in the shortest time.

**Functional.** From energy conservation, $\mechanical{v}=\sqrt{2\mechanical{g}(\mechanical{y}_A-\mechanical{y})}$. The travel time is

$$
T=\int_A^B\frac{d\mechanical{s}}{\mechanical{v}}
=\int_A^B\frac{\sqrt{1+(\mechanical{y}')^2}}{\sqrt{2\mechanical{g}(\mechanical{y}_A-\mechanical{y})}}\,dx.
$$

**Euler equation.** Write the integrand as $F(\mechanical{y},\mechanical{y}')$. It has no explicit dependence on $\mechanical{x}$. The Beltrami first integral gives $F-\mechanical{y}'\,\partial F/\partial\mechanical{y}'=\mathrm{const}$.

**Solution.** Substitute the integrand and use a parameter $\theta$ such that $\mechanical{y}'(\mechanical{x})=-\cot(\theta/2)$. The solution is a **cycloid**:

$$
\mechanical{x}(\theta)=\mechanical{R}(\theta-\sin\theta),
\qquad
\mechanical{y}_A-\mechanical{y}(\theta)=\mechanical{R}(1-\cos\theta).
$$

The location of $B$ determines $\mechanical{R}$. A point on the rim of a rolling wheel traces a cycloid.

**Tautochrone.** Balls released from rest at different points on a cycloidal arc take the same time to reach its lowest point. Huygens used this property in cycloidal pendulum clocks.

## Fixed endpoints and the choice of coordinate

The variational problem holds the release and arrival points fixed and varies the curve between them. For a smooth segment on which vertical depth increases, it is possible to use depth as the independent variable and horizontal position as the dependent variable. This makes the horizontal coordinate cyclic, so its conjugate quantity is constant by the Euler–Lagrange equation. On a branch with a turning point in depth, use separate segments or the cycloidal parameter to keep the full path. See [[13.05 Parameterized curves and surfaces#Arc length from a Cartesian graph|the graph arc-length formula]] for the geometric factor that goes into the travel-time functional.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y},\mechanical{y}_A$ | $\unit{m}$ | Position | The horizontal coordinate, height, and starting height. |
| $\mechanical{s}$ | $\unit{m}$ | Arc length | The length measured along the path. |
| $\mechanical{v}$ | $\unit{m\,s^{-1}}$ | Speed | The speed at the current height. |
| $\mechanical{g}$ | $\unit{m\,s^{-2}}$ | Acceleration due to gravity | The magnitude of the uniform downward acceleration. |
| $T$ | $\unit{s}$ | Travel time | The time along the candidate path. |
| $\mechanical{R}$ | $\unit{m}$ | Cycloid radius | The radius of the wheel that generates the cycloid. |
