## Exercise 5.4 — Transverse Dirac delta

Show that
$$
\delta_T^{ij}(\mechanical{\mathbf x})
=\int\frac{d^3\mechanical{\mathbf k}}{(2\pi)^3}
\left(\delta^{ij}
-\frac{\mechanical{k}^i\mechanical{k}^j}{\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf k}\rvert^2}\right)
e^{i\mechanical{\mathbf k}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf x}}
$$
satisfies $\partial_i\delta_T^{ij}(\mechanical{\mathbf x})=0$ and
$$
\delta_T^{ij}(\mechanical{\mathbf x})+\delta_L^{ij}(\mechanical{\mathbf x})
=\delta^{ij}\delta^{(3)}(\mechanical{\mathbf x}),
$$
where $\delta_L^{ij}$ is the corresponding longitudinal distribution. Calculate $\delta_T^{ij}(\mechanical{\mathbf x})$ explicitly through derivatives of $1/\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf x}\rvert$.

The goal is the identity kernel on transverse vector fields, as required by Coulomb-gauge commutators. Interpret the Fourier transform as a distribution acting on smooth test fields, not as a finite matrix-valued function at the origin. The Coulomb inverse below assumes free-space boundary behavior; a bounded domain requires its own inverse-Laplacian kernel.

## Solution 5.4 — Transverse Dirac delta

The transverse delta distribution is
$$
\delta_T^{ij}(\mechanical{\mathbf x})
=\int\frac{d^3\mechanical{\mathbf k}}{(2\pi)^3}
\left(\delta^{ij}
-\frac{\mechanical{k}^i\mechanical{k}^j}{|\mechanical{\mathbf k}|^2}\right)
e^{i\mechanical{\mathbf k}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf x}}.
$$

### Vanishing divergence

Since $\partial_i e^{i\mechanical{\mathbf k}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf x}}
=i\mechanical{k}_i e^{i\mechanical{\mathbf k}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf x}}$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_i\delta_T^{ij}
&=\int\frac{d^3\mechanical{\mathbf k}}{(2\pi)^3}
i\mechanical{k}_i
\left(\delta^{ij}-\frac{\mechanical{k}^i\mechanical{k}^j}{|\mechanical{\mathbf k}|^2}\right)
e^{i\mechanical{\mathbf k}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf x}}\\
&=\int\frac{d^3\mechanical{\mathbf k}}{(2\pi)^3}
i(\mechanical{k}^j-\mechanical{k}^j)e^{i\mechanical{\mathbf k}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf x}}=0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Spatial indices in these projectors use the Euclidean three-dimensional metric.

### Transverse and longitudinal completeness

Define
$$
\delta_L^{ij}(\mechanical{\mathbf x})
=\int\frac{d^3\mechanical{\mathbf k}}{(2\pi)^3}
\frac{\mechanical{k}^i\mechanical{k}^j}{|\mechanical{\mathbf k}|^2}
e^{i\mechanical{\mathbf k}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf x}}.
$$
Then
$$
\delta_T^{ij}(\mechanical{\mathbf x})+\delta_L^{ij}(\mechanical{\mathbf x})
=\delta^{ij}\int\frac{d^3\mechanical{\mathbf k}}{(2\pi)^3}
e^{i\mechanical{\mathbf k}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf x}}
=\delta^{ij}\delta^{(3)}(\mechanical{\mathbf x}).
$$

### Position-space expression

Use
$$
\frac{\mechanical{k}^i\mechanical{k}^j}{|\mechanical{\mathbf k}|^2}
e^{i\mechanical{\mathbf k}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf x}}
=-\partial_i\partial_j
\frac{e^{i\mechanical{\mathbf k}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf x}}}{|\mechanical{\mathbf k}|^2}
$$
and the massless static Green function
$$
\int\frac{d^3\mechanical{\mathbf k}}{(2\pi)^3}
\frac{e^{i\mechanical{\mathbf k}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf x}}}{|\mechanical{\mathbf k}|^2}
=\frac1{4\pi|\mechanical{\mathbf x}|}.
$$
Consequently,
$$
\delta_L^{ij}(\mechanical{\mathbf x})
=-\partial_i\partial_j\frac1{4\pi|\mechanical{\mathbf x}|},
$$
$$
\delta_T^{ij}(\mechanical{\mathbf x})
=\delta^{ij}\delta^{(3)}(\mechanical{\mathbf x})
+\partial_i\partial_j\frac1{4\pi|\mechanical{\mathbf x}|}.
$$
For $\mechanical{\mathbf x}\ne0$,
$$
\delta_T^{ij}(\mechanical{\mathbf x})
=\frac{3\hat x^i\hat x^j-\delta^{ij}}
{4\pi|\mechanical{\mathbf x}|^3}.
$$

> [!note]
> The last expression applies away from the origin. The derivative form above defines the full distribution, including its contact term at the origin. The source denotes that contribution only as “contact term.”

A position-space check uses the distributional Laplacian of the Coulomb kernel: it is minus the three-dimensional delta. Taking a divergence of the derivative form therefore cancels the derivative of the explicit contact delta. Omitting the origin would hide the term that makes this cancellation work. When the kernel is convolved with a decaying transverse field, it returns that field; convolving it with a pure gradient removes that longitudinal part. These are the operational meanings of transverse completeness.
