---
title: "Grassmann Variables"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Grassmann generators anticommute and square to zero. A function of one generator has only a constant and a linear term.

Fermion fields in a functional integral require anticommuting numbers, called **Grassmann variables**.
A set of $n$ generators $\{\theta_i\}$ satisfies
$$
\{\theta_i,\theta_j\}
=\theta_i\theta_j+\theta_j\theta_i=0,
\qquad\text{for all }i,j.
\tag{11.17}
$$
In particular, $\theta_i^2=0$: each generator is nilpotent.

Setting the two indices equal gives twice the square equal to zero, over the real or complex coefficient field. Distinct generators can have a nonzero product, so nilpotency does not remove every higher-degree term. With finitely many generators, a basis contains the ordered products with each generator present at most once, including the empty product.
A function of one Grassmann variable therefore terminates at first order:
$$
f(\theta)=a+b\theta.
$$

All higher powers vanish.
For two independent Grassmann variables $\theta$ and $\bar\theta$,
$$
f(\theta,\bar\theta)
=a+b\theta+c\bar\theta+d\theta\bar\theta.
$$

The order in the last term is part of its definition: writing the generators in the opposite order changes its sign. Odd elements anticommute, whereas even bilinears commute with other even elements. This allows the fermionic action to be even while its elementary variables remain odd.

For a two-generator check, expand the exponential of $-a\bar\theta\theta$, with commuting $a$. The square of the bilinear vanishes, so the answer is $1-a\bar\theta\theta$ exactly. This algebraic truncation is the reason that the next Gaussian calculation requires no convergence assumption for finitely many Grassmann variables. It is distinct from the regulator needed for infinitely many field modes.
