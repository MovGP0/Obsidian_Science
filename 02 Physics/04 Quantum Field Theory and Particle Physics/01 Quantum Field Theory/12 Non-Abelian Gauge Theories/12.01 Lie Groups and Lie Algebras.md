> [!TLDR]
> Lie groups describe continuous symmetries, and Lie algebras describe their infinitesimal generators. Non-Abelian gauge symmetries lead to interacting gauge bosons.

The question is how to compare internal states at different spacetime points when the allowed internal rotations do not commute. First read [[12.01.01 General Structure]] and [[12.01.02 Representations]] to separate the symmetry algebra from the matrices that act on a field. The two concrete groups then fix the normalization used in the gauge-field construction.

Local symmetry supplies a consistent interaction structure; it does not by itself select the gauge group or matter content found in nature. Follow the chapter from the classical connection to gauge fixing, then to perturbative QCD. The final confinement discussion requires a different level of evidence: growth of a perturbative coupling signals loss of perturbative control, and is not a proof that all non-Abelian theories confine.

## Chapter introduction

> “It is only slightly overstating the case to say that physics is the study of symmetry.”
>
> — Philip W. Anderson, *Science* **177** (1972)

Non-Abelian gauge theories, also called Yang–Mills theories, were introduced by Chen-Ning Yang and Robert Mills in 1954 as a generalization of QED.
They form the mathematical basis of the Standard Model.
Quantum chromodynamics (QCD), which describes the strong interaction, is an $SU(3)$ gauge theory.
The electroweak sector is based on $SU(2)\times U(1)$.

The main difference from QED is that the gauge bosons interact with one another.
Gluons carry color charge and therefore couple to each other.
This produces three- and four-boson vertices with no direct QED counterpart.

Together with quantum mechanics, these self-interactions produce asymptotic freedom.
At high energies, the strong coupling becomes small and perturbation theory can be used.
At low energies, the coupling grows and quarks are confined.

> [!note]
> Asymptotic freedom also depends on the matter content. Gauge-boson self-interactions give the antiscreening contribution, but too many matter fields can change the sign of the beta function.
