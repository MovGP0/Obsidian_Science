---
title: "The Majorana Field"
---

> [!TLDR]
> A Majorana field equals its charge conjugate and describes a neutral fermion that is its own antiparticle.

A Majorana field satisfies
$$
\signal{\psi}^{\,c}=C\bar{\signal{\psi}}^{\,T}=\signal{\psi}. \tag{4.70}
$$
The Majorana condition requires a Lorentz-compatible real structure on the spinor representation. A Majorana fermion is neutral and is identical to its antiparticle. The source gives the neutrino as a possible candidate.

In mode variables, the condition imposes $d_{\mathbf p}^{s}=b_{\mathbf p}^{s}$ and reduces the number of independent degrees of freedom.

> [!note]
> A real structure does not mean that every spinor component is real in every matrix representation. Whether neutrinos satisfy a Majorana condition is a physical question; this construction does not decide it.

Self-conjugacy identifies the particle and antiparticle operator families after compatible spinor basis and phase choices. The simple equality of mode operators is convention dependent, but the reduction in independent modes is not. A massive field retains two physical spin states.

Neutrality here means absence of the Dirac continuous phase charge for a single self-conjugate field. A general phase rotation does not preserve (4.70): its charge conjugate has the opposite phase. This rules out the ordinary nonzero electric charge of a Dirac electron; it does not imply absence of all interactions. The distinction between a charged Dirac pair and a neutral self-conjugate fermion is discussed in [Dreiner, Haber, and Martin, sections 3 and 5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0812.1594#page=23).

To check the current algebra and the factor one half in the action, use [[04.18.11 The Majorana field]]. Its vanishing vector-current argument depends on anticommuting field components. Replacing them by commuting numerical spinors changes the bilinear symmetry argument.
