---
title: "Equation Satisfied by the Propagator"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Acting with the Dirac operator on its Feynman propagator gives i times the spacetime delta function and spinor identity.

The propagator satisfies

$$
(i\not{\partial}_x-\mechanical{m})\signal{S}_{F}(\mechanical{x}-\mechanical{y})=i\delta^{(4)}(\mechanical{x}-\mechanical{y})\mathbf1_4. \tag{4.48}
$$

In momentum space,

$$
(\not p-\mechanical{m})\frac{i(\not p+\mechanical{m})}{\mechanical{p}^{2}-\mechanical{m}^{2}+i\varepsilon}=\frac{i(\mechanical{p}^{2}-\mechanical{m}^{2})}{\mechanical{p}^{2}-\mechanical{m}^{2}+i\varepsilon}\longrightarrow i\mathbf1_4. \tag{4.49}
$$

Taking the regulator to zero verifies the source equation.

This is an inverse equation with Feynman boundary conditions. Away from the source event the propagator satisfies the homogeneous Dirac equation. At coincidence the contact term is essential: discarding it would turn a Green's function into an ordinary homogeneous solution.

Equation (4.49) is a distributional limit under the Fourier integral, not a pointwise cancellation at a pole. Its factor of the imaginary unit follows from the definition in (4.42); some references absorb that factor into the propagator's name. The dimensions also match: a derivative acting on the coordinate propagator has four energy powers, as does the four-dimensional delta function. Retarded and advanced inverses satisfy the same source equation but different boundary conditions.
