---
title: "Limits of the Canonical Tensor"
---

> [!TLDR]
> The canonical tensor is not generally symmetric and can depend on the electromagnetic gauge.

The canonical tensor (2.35) has two limitations. It is not generally symmetric: $\energy{T}^{\mu\nu}\ne\energy{T}^{\nu\mu}$. For electromagnetism it is not gauge invariant, although physical observables are gauge invariant.

One can add a total divergence, $\energy{T}^{\mu\nu}\to\energy{T}^{\mu\nu}+\partial_{\rho}B^{\rho\mu\nu}$, with $B^{\rho\mu\nu}=-B^{\mu\rho\nu}$, to address these problems.

Antisymmetry in the first two indices preserves conservation because $\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\rho}B^{\rho\mu\nu}=0$.

The conservation check uses commuting partial derivatives: their symmetric contraction with the antisymmetric first pair is zero. This is an algebraic identity, so it does not need the field equations. Conservation of the original tensor itself usually does need the equations of motion.

Equal divergence does not alone imply equal charges. Integrating the improvement in the temporal component leaves a surface contribution. It vanishes for suitable decay at infinity or suitable periodic boundary conditions, but it can matter at an actual finite boundary. Likewise, symmetry and gauge invariance are separate tests: an arbitrary antisymmetric improvement need not achieve either one.

For a physical interpretation, compare gauge-related potentials that give the same electric and magnetic fields. A local energy density expressed only through those fields assigns them the same energy. A canonical expression containing an uncontracted potential derivative need not make this property visible.
