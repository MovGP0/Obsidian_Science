---
title: "Lorentz Covariance"
---

> [!TLDR]
> A Lorentz-scalar Lagrangian density produces an invariant action when all Lorentz indices are contracted.

A relativistic action must be a Lorentz scalar. The spacetime measure $d^{4}\mechanical{x}$ is invariant because a Lorentz transformation has determinant $\pm1$. It is therefore sufficient for the Lagrangian density to be a scalar. All Lorentz indices in its terms must be contracted.

For a real scalar field, simple terms through fourth order are

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}=\frac12(\partial_{\mu}\signal{\phi})(\partial^{\mu}\signal{\phi})-\frac12\mechanical{m}^{2}\signal{\phi}^{2}-\frac{\lambda}{4!}\signal{\phi}^{4}+\cdots. \tag{2.6}
$$

Here $(\partial_{\mu}\signal{\phi})(\partial^{\mu}\signal{\phi})=g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\signal{\phi}\partial_{\nu}\signal{\phi}=\signal{\dot\phi}^{2}-|\nabla\signal{\phi}|^{2}$.

The metric sign makes the time-derivative term positive and the spatial-gradient term negative in the Lagrangian. The corresponding Hamiltonian reverses the gradient sign, giving positive gradient energy. Do not infer energy positivity by inspecting the Lagrangian alone.

In four spacetime dimensions, a canonically normalized scalar has one power of energy. Thus the kinetic, mass, and quartic terms each have four powers of energy, while the quartic coupling is dimensionless. Lorentz covariance does not by itself impose the even-field form in (2.6): this example also uses the symmetry under field sign reversal. A positive quartic coefficient bounds the potential at large field amplitude; a negative coefficient does not, unless additional stabilizing terms are included.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\mechanical{x}$ | $\unit{MeV^{-1}}$ | Spacetime position | The integration coordinate. |
| $\energy{\mathcal L}$ | $\unit{MeV^{4}}$ | Lagrangian density | The Lorentz-scalar dynamical density. |
| $\signal{\phi}$ | $\unit{MeV}$ | Scalar field | The canonically normalized real scalar field. |
