title: "Negative Probabilities in the Klein–Gordon Equation"
---

> [!TLDR]
> The conserved Klein–Gordon current has a negative time component for negative-energy modes, so it cannot be a probability current for all solutions.

A continuity equation is necessary for probability conservation, but positivity must be tested separately. For a complex scalar amplitude and real mass, multiply equation (1.11) by $\signal{\phi}^{*}$:

$$
\signal{\phi}^{*}\square\signal{\phi}
+\mechanical{m}^{2}\signal{\phi}^{*}\signal{\phi}=0. \tag{1.16}
$$

Subtract its complex conjugate:

$$
\signal{\phi}^{*}\square\signal{\phi}
-\signal{\phi}\square\signal{\phi}^{*}=0. \tag{1.17}
$$

This can be written as

$$
\partial_{\mu}\left(\signal{\phi}^{*}\partial^{\mu}\signal{\phi}
-\signal{\phi}\partial^{\mu}\signal{\phi}^{*}\right)=0. \tag{1.18}
$$

Define the four-current

$$
\signal{j}^{\mu}=i\left(\signal{\phi}^{*}\partial^{\mu}\signal{\phi}
-\signal{\phi}\partial^{\mu}\signal{\phi}^{*}\right), \tag{1.19}
$$

which satisfies $\partial_{\mu}\signal{j}^{\mu}=0$. The factor $i$ makes $\signal{j}^{\mu}$ real. In converting (1.17) to (1.18), the two products of first derivatives cancel by the product rule; the mass terms have already canceled because the mass is real. Its time component is

$$
\signal{j}^{0}=i\left(\signal{\phi}^{*}\partial_{\mechanical{t}}\signal{\phi}
-\signal{\phi}\partial_{\mechanical{t}}\signal{\phi}^{*}\right). \tag{1.20}
$$

One would like to interpret this component as a probability density. Evaluate it for the positive-energy solution $\signal{\phi}=e^{-i\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{x}}=e^{-i(\energy{\omega}_{\mathbf p}\mechanical{t}-\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf x})}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\signal{j}^{0}
&=i\left(e^{+i\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{x}}(-i\energy{\omega}_{\mathbf p})e^{-i\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{x}}
-e^{-i\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{x}}(+i\energy{\omega}_{\mathbf p})e^{+i\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{x}}\right)\\
&=2\energy{\omega}_{\mathbf p}>0.
\end{aligned} \tag{1.21}
$$

For a negative-energy solution $\signal{\phi}=e^{+i\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{x}}$, with $\mechanical{p}^{0}=\energy{\omega}_{\mathbf p}>0$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\signal{j}^{0}
&=i\left(e^{-i\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{x}}(+i\energy{\omega}_{\mathbf p})e^{+i\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{x}}
-e^{+i\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{x}}(-i\energy{\omega}_{\mathbf p})e^{-i\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{x}}\right)\\
&=-2\energy{\omega}_{\mathbf p}<0.
\end{aligned} \tag{1.22}
$$

The problem is that $\signal{j}^{0}$ can be negative. A negative probability of finding a particle in a spatial region has no physical meaning. This problem led physicists in 1926 to abandon the Klein–Gordon equation as a fundamental equation of relativistic single-particle quantum mechanics.

Chapter 3 resolves the problem by interpreting the Klein–Gordon field as a quantum field. The current is then a conserved charge current, $\electric{j}^{\mu}$. A negative $\electric{j}^{0}$ describes particles with the opposite charge, or antiparticles, rather than a negative probability.

## Example 1.1 — Dispersion Relation and Group Velocity

Consider a Klein–Gordon wave packet centered on momentum $\mechanical{\mathbf p}_0$:

$$
\signal{\phi}(\mechanical{t},\mechanical{\mathbf x})
=\int\frac{d^{3}\mechanical{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\signal{f}(\mechanical{\mathbf p}-\mechanical{\mathbf p}_0)
e^{-i(\energy{\omega}_{\mathbf p}\mechanical{t}-\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf x})}, \tag{1.23}
$$

where $\signal{f}(\mechanical{\mathbf k})$ decreases rapidly for $|\mechanical{\mathbf k}|\gg\mechanical{\delta p}$.

The phase velocity of a mode with momentum $\mechanical{\mathbf p}$ is

$$
\mechanical{v}_{\mathrm{phase}}
=\frac{\energy{\omega}_{\mathbf p}}{|\mechanical{\mathbf p}|}
=\frac{\sqrt{\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{2}+\mechanical{m}^{2}}}{|\mechanical{\mathbf p}|}>1. \tag{1.24}
$$

It exceeds the speed of light. However, the group velocity, which describes the propagation of the packet envelope, is

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf v}_{\mathrm{group}}
=\left.\nabla_{\mechanical{\mathbf p}}\energy{\omega}_{\mathbf p}\right|_{\mechanical{\mathbf p}=\mechanical{\mathbf p}_0}
=\frac{\mechanical{\mathbf p}_0}{\energy{\omega}_{\mathbf p_0}}
=\frac{\mechanical{\mathbf p}_0}{\sqrt{\mechanical{\mathbf p}_0^{2}+\mechanical{m}^{2}}},
\qquad |\mechanical{\mathbf v}_{\mathrm{group}}|<1. \tag{1.25}
$$

For a massive packet narrow enough in momentum that the dispersion can be linearized about its center, the group speed is below the speed of light. It tends to zero at zero central momentum and tends to one at momenta much larger than the mass. The massless limit gives speed one, so the strict inequalities in (1.24) and (1.25) presume nonzero mass. Envelope speed is a useful check, but a full causality test concerns signal response or local field commutators, not only the maximum of a packet. A phase speed above the speed of light therefore does not make the Klein–Gordon equation incompatible with relativity.

> [!note]
> Equation (1.25) writes the group velocity as a vector and applies the inequality to its magnitude. The source places the inequality directly after the vector expression.
