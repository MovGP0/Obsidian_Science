---
title: "Plane-Wave Solutions"
---

> [!TLDR]
> The Klein–Gordon equation has positive- and negative-energy plane waves, and a general solution is a superposition of both branches.

The plane-wave solutions of equation (1.11) are

$$
\signal{\phi}(\mechanical{x})
=e^{-i\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{x}}
=e^{-i(\energy{E}\mechanical{t}-\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf x})}, \tag{1.12}
$$

subject to the dispersion relation (1.7). The quadratic relation $\energy{E}^{2}=\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{2}+\mechanical{m}^{2}$ has two roots:

$$
\energy{E}=\pm\sqrt{\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{2}+\mechanical{m}^{2}}
\equiv\pm\energy{\omega}_{\mathbf p}, \tag{1.13}
$$

where $\energy{\omega}_{\mathbf p}>0$ by definition. Negative-energy solutions, $\energy{E}=-\energy{\omega}_{\mathbf p}$, are therefore mathematically allowed.

The full solution space of this second-order equation contains both frequency branches. If both were interpreted as energies of the same single particle, its energy spectrum would have no lower bound. That would create a stability problem once interactions that connect the branches are allowed. The free equation alone does not describe photon emission.

> [!note]
> The source's completeness argument needs qualification: a free positive-frequency subspace can be selected. The scalar equation is also not an electron-emission model. Local interacting fields require a broader description; see [Tong, sections 2.2 and 2.6](https://www.damtp.cam.ac.uk/user/tong/qft/qft.pdf).

The general Klein–Gordon solution is a superposition of modes:

$$
\signal{\phi}(\mechanical{x})
=\int\frac{d^{3}\mechanical{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{2\energy{\omega}_{\mathbf p}}
\left[\signal{a}(\mechanical{\mathbf p})e^{-i\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{x}}
+\signal{b}^{*}(\mechanical{\mathbf p})e^{+i\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{x}}\right]. \tag{1.14}
$$

Here $\mechanical{p}^{0}=+\energy{\omega}_{\mathbf p}$. The first term is the positive-energy solution, and the second term represents the negative-energy solutions.

The phase-space measure is Lorentz invariant, as can be seen from

$$
\int\frac{d^{3}\mechanical{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{2\energy{\omega}_{\mathbf p}}
=\int\frac{d^{4}\mechanical{p}}{(2\pi)^{4}}(2\pi)
\delta(\mechanical{p}^{2}-\mechanical{m}^{2})\theta(\mechanical{p}^{0}). \tag{1.15}
$$

> [!note]
> The source uses k for the three-dimensional integration variable and p for the momentum in the integrand. Equations (1.14) and (1.15) retain that notation. In using either integral, identify k with the three-momentum p; they are not independent integration variables.

Integrating the right side of (1.15) over the positive energy root gives the factor of one over twice the positive mode energy. This is the Jacobian of the mass-shell delta function. The positive-energy restriction is invariant under proper, orthochronous Lorentz transformations, which preserve the direction of time. A single plane wave is not spatially normalizable; use packets or a stated box normalization for probabilities.
