> [!TLDR]
> A negative squared mass produces unstable low-momentum modes around the chosen field configuration.

[[01.12 Exercises|Chapter 1 exercise index]]

## Exercise 1.3 — Klein–Gordon Equation with Complex Mass?

Consider $(\square+\mechanical{\mu}^{2})\signal{\phi}=0$ with $\mechanical{\mu}^{2}<0$. The mass is imaginary: $\mechanical{\mu}^{2}=-\mechanical{\lambda}^{2}$, where $\mechanical{\lambda}>0$ is real.

**(a)** Find the plane-wave solutions. What happens to the dispersion relation?

**(b)** Show that modes with exponential growth in time exist. What does this mean for vacuum stability?

**(c)** This type of field, called a tachyonic field, occurs in the Higgs potential. Give a qualitative explanation of how a stabilizing potential $\energy{V}(\signal{\phi})$ resolves the instability.

The physical question is stability of small fluctuations about a chosen stationary field, not whether an observed particle travels faster than light. The linear equation tests the curvature at that background; it cannot describe the later nonlinear evolution once the fluctuation becomes large.

## Solution 1.3 — Negative mass squared and vacuum instability

**(a)** The equation is
$$
(\Box-\mechanical{\lambda}^2)\signal{\phi}=0.
$$
For plane waves $\signal{\phi}\propto e^{-i\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{x}}$, with
$\mechanical{p}^\mu=(\energy{E},\mechanical{\mathbf p})$,
$$
-(\energy{E}^2-\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p}\rvert^2)-\mechanical{\lambda}^2=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\energy{E}^2=\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p}\rvert^2-\mechanical{\lambda}^2.
$$
If $\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p}\rvert^2<\mechanical{\lambda}^2$, then $\energy{E}^2<0$ and
$$
\energy{E}=\pm i\signal{\Omega},
\qquad
\signal{\Omega}=\sqrt{\mechanical{\lambda}^2-\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p}\rvert^2}.
$$
This is the tachyonic dispersion relation. There is no real-frequency mass shell for $\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p}\rvert<\mechanical{\lambda}$ about this unstable vacuum.

**(b)** In that momentum range, a mode has the form
$$
\signal{\phi}\propto
e^{\signal{\Omega}\mechanical{t}}
e^{i\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf x}}.
$$
It grows exponentially with time. There is also a decaying solution; generic initial data include the growing part. The spatially uniform mode has the largest growth rate, equal to the positive instability scale. At momentum equal to that scale the frequency is zero and the linear time dependence must be obtained from the zero-frequency equation, rather than treating two identical exponential roots as independent modes.
The configuration $\signal{\phi}=0$ is a local maximum, or an unstable stationary point, of
$$
\energy{V}(\signal{\phi})=-\frac12\mechanical{\lambda}^2\signal{\phi}^2.
$$
Small fluctuations grow.

**(c)** Add a stabilizing term, for example
$$
\energy{V}(\signal{\phi})
=-\frac12\mechanical{\lambda}^2\signal{\phi}^2
+\frac{\signal{\lambda}_4}{4!}\signal{\phi}^4,
\qquad
\signal{\lambda}_4>0.
$$
The true minima then occur at $\signal{\phi}=\pm\signal{v}$.

The vacuum magnitude is
$$
\signal{v}^2=\frac{6\mechanical{\lambda}^2}{\signal{\lambda}_4}.
$$
Choosing one minimum spontaneously breaks the $\mathbb Z_2$ symmetry.
For fluctuations $\signal{\phi}=\signal{v}+\signal{\eta}$,
$$
\mechanical{m}_\eta^2
=\energy{V}''(\signal{v})
=-\mechanical{\lambda}^2+\frac{\signal{\lambda}_4}{2}\signal{v}^2
=2\mechanical{\lambda}^2>0.
$$
The instability disappears about the true minimum in this classical small-fluctuation model. The positive curvature is the squared mass of the new fluctuation, not the original negative parameter. A stabilizing quartic term is essential: the negative quadratic potential alone is unbounded below and has no stable ground state.

> [!note]
> The source calls this the Higgs mechanism in the global case. Here the calculation shows discrete spontaneous symmetry breaking and a stable massive scalar fluctuation. The gauge-field Higgs mechanism additionally gives a vector boson its longitudinal mode and mass.
