---
title: "The Beginnings (1925–1932)"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Quantum field theory began alongside the development of quantum mechanics.

The birth of quantum field theory is closely connected with the birth of quantum mechanics itself. Heisenberg, Born, Jordan, and Schrödinger formulated quantum mechanics in 1925–1926. The question of how to treat the electromagnetic field quantum mechanically followed almost immediately. A prescribed classical wave can drive atomic transitions, but it has no photon-number state space. Quantizing its normal modes makes emission and absorption changes in field occupation as well as changes in the atom.

In 1925, Born and Jordan showed that the quantized harmonic oscillator, with position–momentum commutator $[\mechanical{\hat q},\mechanical{\hat p}]=i$, could be described with creation and annihilation operators $\hat a^{\dagger}$ and $\hat a$.

In 1927, Dirac quantized the free electromagnetic field. He identified photons as its excitation quanta and derived Einstein's coefficients for stimulated emission and absorption from quantum principles.

In 1928, Dirac introduced his first-order relativistic equation. It predicted the electron magnetic moment and, implicitly, antiparticles.

In 1929, Heisenberg and Pauli developed the first systematic formulation of quantum electrodynamics (QED), using the Lagrangian formalism and canonical quantization. This was fundamental work, but the theory contained infinities.

The free-electron equation and the quantized radiation field solved different parts of this problem. The former supplied relativistic spinor dynamics; the latter supplied photon creation and annihilation. Their combination required an interaction theory, not only a relativistic wave equation.

In 1932, Anderson observed the positron in cosmic-ray tracks in a cloud chamber, confirming Dirac's prediction. In the same year, Chadwick discovered the neutron, which helped establish nuclear physics.

A useful reading check is to separate the positron prediction from its observation: a theoretical second branch is not a cloud-chamber track. Identifying a track also requires the sign of curvature in a known magnetic field and evidence about the particle's motion and energy. This note supplies the source chronology, not the underlying measurement record.
