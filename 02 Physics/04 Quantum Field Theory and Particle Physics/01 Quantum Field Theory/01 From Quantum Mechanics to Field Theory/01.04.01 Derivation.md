title: "Derivation"

> [!TLDR]
> Applying the quantum energy and momentum operators to the relativistic dispersion relation gives the Klein–Gordon equation.

Consider a free scalar amplitude in flat spacetime, with a constant real mass and no external potential. The scalar assumption matters: the amplitude at a transformed event has the same value, with no spinor or vector component transformation. Start with the relativistic dispersion relation (1.7). Substitute $\energy{E}\to i\partial_{\mechanical{t}}$ and $\mechanical{\mathbf p}\to-i\nabla$:

$$
(i\partial_{\mechanical{t}})^{2}\signal{\psi}
=\left[(-i\nabla)^{2}+\mechanical{m}^{2}\right]\signal{\psi}. \tag{1.9}
$$

This can be written as

$$
\left(\partial_{\mechanical{t}}^{2}-\nabla^{2}+\mechanical{m}^{2}\right)\signal{\psi}=0, \tag{1.10}
$$

or, in covariant notation,

$$
\boxed{\left(\square+\mechanical{m}^{2}\right)\signal{\phi}=0.} \tag{1.11}
$$

Here $\square=\partial^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}=\partial_{\mechanical{t}}^{2}-\nabla^{2}$ is the d'Alembert operator. The notation changes from the wavefunction symbol to the scalar-field symbol without changing this equation.

The two factors of $i$ in (1.9) produce a minus sign on each side; moving the terms gives (1.10). Each derivative has energy dimension in natural units, so both the derivative terms and the mass term have energy-squared dimension times the amplitude. Unlike a first-order evolution equation, this equation requires both the amplitude and its first time derivative on an initial spacelike surface.

Oskar Klein and Walter Gordon proposed equation (1.11) independently in 1926, at almost the same time as the Schrödinger equation. It was soon abandoned as a single-particle wave equation because of the problems examined in the following subsections.

The equation is Lorentz covariant. It is expressed through the Lorentz-invariant operator $\square$ and therefore has the same form in every inertial frame.
