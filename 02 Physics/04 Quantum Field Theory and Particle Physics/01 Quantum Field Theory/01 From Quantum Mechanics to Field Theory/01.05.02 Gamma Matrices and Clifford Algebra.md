---
title: "Gamma Matrices and Clifford Algebra"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Gamma matrices express the Dirac equation in covariant form and satisfy the Clifford anticommutation algebra.

The Hamiltonian form separates time from space. Gamma matrices collect its coefficients so the derivative and mass terms can be written as one Lorentz-covariant equation. Covariance also requires the wavefunction to transform as a spinor; writing a repeated index alone does not establish that transformation law.

Define the gamma matrices by

$$
\gamma^{0}=\beta,\qquad
\gamma^{i}=\beta\alpha^{i}=\gamma^{0}\alpha^{i}. \tag{1.31}
$$

Equation (1.26) then becomes

$$
\boxed{(i\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}-\mechanical{m})\signal{\psi}=0,} \tag{1.32}
$$

or, with Feynman's slash notation $\not{p}=\gamma^{\mu}\mechanical{p}_{\mu}$,

$$
(i\not{\partial}-\mechanical{m})\signal{\psi}=0. \tag{1.33}
$$

Relations (1.28)–(1.30) give the Clifford algebra:

$$
\{\gamma^{\mu},\gamma^{\nu}\}=2g^{\mu\nu}\mathbf1_{4\times4}. \tag{1.34}
$$

This relation specifies the gamma-matrix algebra. The source describes the matrices as determined up to unitary transformations. More generally, a simultaneous invertible similarity change of the matrices and spinor preserves the algebra; unitary changes preserve the usual fixed Hermiticity convention and component inner product. Equation (1.34) immediately gives

$$
(\gamma^{0})^{2}=1,
$$

$$
(\gamma^{i})^{2}=-1,
$$

$$
\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{\nu}=-\gamma^{\nu}\gamma^{\mu}
\qquad\text{for }\mu\ne\nu.
$$

As a direct algebraic check, square the slashed four-momentum. Its symmetric momentum product removes the commutator part of the gamma product, leaving the Lorentz norm times the identity. Multiplying the momentum-space Dirac operator by its opposite-mass partner therefore gives the Klein–Gordon mass-shell factor. This is why the metric signs in (1.34) are essential.
