---
title: "Dark Matter"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Gravitational and cosmological observations indicate a large nonluminous matter component. The Standard Model has no established particle candidate that accounts for it.

Galactic rotation curves, gravitational lensing, the cosmic microwave background, and large-scale structure indicate that about 27% of the total cosmic energy density is in **dark matter**.

These observations constrain a gravitating component through different models and length scales. A rotation curve constrains the enclosed mass distribution under the chosen gravitational model; it does not determine a particle's mass. Cosmological density fractions come from fitting a cosmological model. For example, the [Planck 2018 parameter analysis](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.06209) states its cold-dark-matter density inference within the base ΛCDM model. The source's approximate 27% is a cosmological benchmark under such assumptions, rather than a direct particle count.

A viable candidate needs more than a mass in one listed range. Its abundance, lifetime, clustering behavior, and interactions must be compatible with the observations being explained. A proposed direct-detection signal would test a specified particle interaction, while gravitational evidence alone does not select a WIMP, axion, or sterile-neutrino model. Keep the inference of a missing matter component separate from these candidate identifications.

Dark matter produces gravitational effects but does not emit or absorb light in the way ordinary luminous matter does.
The Standard Model has no convincing candidate that accounts for this component.

The source discusses these candidate particles:

- **Weakly interacting massive particles (WIMPs):** Massive particles with interactions of approximately weak strength. It gives a typical mass range of about $10$–$10^4\,\unit{GeV}$. The supersymmetric neutralino is a standard example.
- **Axions:** Also motivated by the strong CP problem.
- **Heavy sterile neutrinos.**
- **Particles with masses below the $\unit{GeV}$ scale.**

> [!note]
> The source says that dark matter does not interact electromagnetically and is not made of Standard Model fermions. This describes its intended nonbaryonic candidate class. The observational statement is that ordinary baryonic matter and known active neutrinos cannot supply the required dominant dark-matter component. Candidate interactions and allowed masses depend on the model.
