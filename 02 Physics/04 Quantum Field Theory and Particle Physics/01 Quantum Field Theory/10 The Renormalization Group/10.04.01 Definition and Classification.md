title: "Definition and Classification"
---

> [!TLDR]
> At a fixed point the beta function vanishes. The sign of its first derivative determines the local infrared or ultraviolet stability of a single coupling.

An RG **fixed point** is a coupling value $\signal{g^*}$ such that
$$
\signal{\beta}(\signal{g^*})=0.
$$
The coupling does not change with scale at the fixed point.
The source describes the theory there as scale invariant.

Classify a fixed point by the nearby RG flow.
Write $\signal{g}=\signal{g^*}+\signal{\delta g}$ and linearize:
$$
\energy{\mu}\frac{\partial\signal{\delta g}}{\partial\energy{\mu}}
=\signal{\beta}'(\signal{g^*})\signal{\delta g}
+\mathcal{O}((\signal{\delta g})^2),
$$
where
$\signal{\beta}'(\signal{g^*})
=\left.\mathrm{d}\signal{\beta}/\mathrm{d}\signal{g}
\right|_{\signal{g}=\signal{g^*}}$.
The linearized solution is
$$
\signal{\delta g}(\energy{\mu})
=\signal{\delta g}(\energy{\mu_0})
\left(\frac{\energy{\mu}}{\energy{\mu_0}}\right)^{
\signal{\beta}'(\signal{g^*})}.
$$

- If $\signal{\beta}'(\signal{g^*})>0$, the perturbation grows toward the ultraviolet, $\energy{\mu}\to\infty$. The fixed point is **infrared stable**: it attracts the flow toward low energies. In the ultraviolet direction, the flow moves away from it.

> [!note]
> Scale invariance requires the full theory to be at a fixed point, with all relevant beta functions zero and dimensionful deformations absent or tuned appropriately. A zero of one coupling's beta function alone is not sufficient.

- If $\signal{\beta}'(\signal{g^*})<0$, the perturbation decreases toward the ultraviolet. The fixed point is **ultraviolet stable** and attracts the high-energy flow.
- A free-theory fixed point $\signal{g^*}=0$ with $\signal{\beta}'(0)=0$ is **marginal** at linear order. For $\signal{\beta}\sim\signal{g}^n$ with $n\ge2$, examine the first nonzero coefficient. A negative leading coefficient can give ultraviolet stability, as in QCD with $\signal{\beta}\sim-b_0\signal{g}^3$ and $b_0>0$. A positive coefficient gives infrared stability on the positive-coupling side, as in four-dimensional $\signal{\phi}^4$ theory with $\signal{\beta}\sim3\signal{\lambda}^2/(16\pi^2)$.

Use the explicit solution to check the stability labels: lowering the scale ratio below one suppresses a perturbation with a positive exponent. Raising the ratio suppresses one with a negative exponent. If the slope vanishes, this power-law test gives no answer; the first nonzero nonlinear term must decide the flow on each allowed side of the fixed point.

For several couplings, replace the single slope by the matrix of beta-function derivatives. Different perturbations can then have different stability directions. Thus an interacting fixed point can attract some perturbations and repel others. This local test does not establish that a distant initial theory lies in its basin of attraction.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\signal{g},\signal{g^*},\signal{\delta g}$ | $1$ | Coupling, fixed-point coupling, and perturbation | The running value, its fixed point, and the small deviation used in linearization. |
| $\signal{\beta}$ | $1$ | Beta function | The logarithmic scale derivative of the coupling. |
| $\energy{\mu},\energy{\mu_0}$ | $\unit{MeV}$ | Evolution and reference scales | The energy scales in the local stability solution. |
| $\signal{\lambda}$ | $1$ | Quartic scalar coupling | The positive coupling in the marginal scalar example. |
| $\signal{\phi}$ | $\unit{MeV}$ | Scalar field | The field in the four-dimensional quartic theory. |
