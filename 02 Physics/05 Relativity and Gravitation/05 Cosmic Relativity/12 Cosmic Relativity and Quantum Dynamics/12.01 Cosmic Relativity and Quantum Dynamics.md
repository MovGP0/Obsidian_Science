> [!TLDR]
> The source introduces quantum mechanics as statistical dynamics expressed through a wavefunction, then questions the physical status of that description.

## Physical question and model

Where does a classical trajectory model cease to predict quantum observations? Mass or size alone does not select the regime. Compare action scales, coherence, state preparation, and environmental coupling for the specific observable.

## Lecture account

The earlier discussion concerned phenomena usually classified as classical mechanics. Newton's equation $d\mechanical{v}/dt=\mechanical{F}/\mechanical{m}$ governs bodies treated as macroscopic. Mass and size are often used, sometimes interchangeably, to identify that regime.

For subatomic systems, the description changes. The source states that Newton's law does not describe an electron or even a slow atom ten thousand times heavier. Quantum dynamics instead uses the Schrödinger equation:

$$
\frac{d\signal{\psi}}{dt}=-\frac{i}{\energy{\hbar}}\hat H\signal{\psi}. \tag{12.1}
$$

The left side is the wavefunction's rate of change. The right side uses the Hamiltonian, normally the system's total energy, as an operator acting on the wavefunction. Here $\energy{\hbar}=\energy{h}/(2\pi)$, with $\energy{h}$ Planck's constant. The wavefunction is generally complex-valued and may have multiple components.

The wavefunction $\signal{\psi}(\mechanical{\mathbf x})$ depends on all particle coordinates in the problem. Early quantum theory associated it with matter waves, but the author regards that simple physical-wave interpretation as inconsistent. He describes the modern wavefunction as an abstract representation of a physical state, potentially of particles, light, or fields, rather than a physical history in ordinary three-dimensional space and time. He argues that its physical meaning remains unresolved.

Solutions nevertheless determine statistical averages of physical quantities. The source stresses that these averages refer to an imagined ensemble of identically prepared systems, not to a definite outcome of a single microscopic measurement. Quantum mechanics is therefore described as a statistical theory of dynamics.

The uncertainty principle limits dispersions of related quantities. The source gives position and momentum as an example:

$$
\Delta\mechanical{x}\,\Delta\mechanical{p}\ge\energy{\hbar}.
$$

Quantum dynamics does not specify a sharp particle trajectory. The wavefunction represents the state, and the Schrödinger equation evolves it. The author therefore questions how this description relates to motion in real space, although its time variable is ordinary physical time.

This raises the author's central question: how can real-space electromagnetic or gravitational fields act on a wavefunction that is not itself in real space? He notes that the Schrödinger equation does not explicitly display particle motion or the evolution of position and momentum. Instead, wavefunction evolution supplies statistical predictions. He contrasts the theory's experimental and practical success with what he regards as its lack of physical understanding.

The question is especially important for Cosmic Relativity because its proposed interaction between cosmic matter and a dynamical system occurs in real space. Without a precise relation between a microscopic system and its wavefunction, the author says that quantum dynamics would remain only provisionally incorporated into the paradigm.

He claims that parallel work on quantum mechanics removes this gap [1]. This chapter gives its main results relevant to Cosmic Relativity. He refers to a forthcoming monograph for a fuller account that he says resolves quantum theory's foundational questions [2].

## Interpretation and checks

The uncertainty relation concerns statistical spreads of specified observables, with a convention-dependent numerical bound; it is not a generic error bar on every classical path. Large composite systems can retain quantum coherence, while a small system can behave approximately classically after decoherence. The source's proposed universal law must predict both cases from a stated preparation rather than classify them only by mass.
