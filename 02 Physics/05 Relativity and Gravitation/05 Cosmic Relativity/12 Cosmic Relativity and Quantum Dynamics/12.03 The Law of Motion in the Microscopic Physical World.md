title: "The Law of Motion in the Microscopic Physical World"
---

> [!TLDR]
> The author proposes evolving a periodic function of action as a universal law for individual-system dynamics, distinct from the ensemble wavefunction.

## Physical question and model

What is added when classical action becomes a periodic complex amplitude? The phase action divided by the proposed action scale is dimensionless, but this representation does not itself specify how probabilities or particle paths respond.

## Lecture account

Experiments reveal periodic behaviour in particle dynamics, with an action scale $\energy{\hbar}=\energy{h}/(2\pi)$. The author identifies the relevant quantity as Hamilton's action, also present in the wavefunction representation of probability density. Hamilton's foundational equation is

$$
\frac{\partial\energy{S}}{\partial t}=-\energy{H}(\mechanical{x},\mechanical{p},t). \tag{12.17}
$$

For an ordinary dynamical system, $\energy{H}=\energy{T}+\energy{V}$, with kinetic energy $\energy{T}$ and potential energy $\energy{V}$. Hamilton's relation $\partial\energy{S}/\partial\mechanical{x}=\mechanical{p}$ makes this equivalent to Newton's equation:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial\mechanical{x}}\frac{\partial\energy{S}}{\partial t}=\frac{d\mechanical{p}}{dt}=-\frac{\partial\energy{V}}{\partial\mechanical{x}}=\mechanical{F}. \tag{12.18}
$$

The author looks to Hamilton's 1833 foundational paper [9] on stationary action for further insight. Hamilton generalised Fermat's optical principle to light and planetary paths and discussed its relation to Huygens' wave theory. In the source's account, interference between neighbouring action paths eliminates nonstationary paths. The physical path has action unchanged to first order under a small path variation.

Action is presented as the fundamental dynamical quantity. It pairs conjugate quantities such as momentum and position, or energy and duration, and accumulates along a history through

$$
\energy{S}=\int\left(\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot d\mechanical{\mathbf x}-\energy{E}\,dt\right).
$$

This reflects $\partial\energy{S}/\partial\mechanical{x}=\mechanical{p}$ and $\partial\energy{S}/\partial t=-\energy{H}$.

The author argues that Hamilton's later general mechanics [10] omitted the underlying periodicity that had motivated stationary action. He proposes that its full dynamical content emerges by evolving a periodic function of action rather than action alone. He calls the resulting universal relation the *modified Hamilton equation*, applying to individual systems at every scale and in classical, quantum, relativistic, or nonrelativistic regimes:

$$
\frac{\partial\signal{\zeta}(\energy{S})}{\partial t}=-\frac{i}{\energy{\varepsilon}}\hat H\signal{\zeta}. \tag{12.19}
$$

Here

$$
\signal{\zeta}(\mechanical{x},t)=\exp\left(\frac{i\energy{S}(\mechanical{x},t)}{\energy{\varepsilon}}\right)
$$

is the *action wave*, which the author assigns a direct physical presence. The Hamiltonian acts through first- and second-order spatial derivatives because physical quantities are extracted from action using differential operators. The fundamental action scale $\energy{\varepsilon}$ is empirically $\energy{\hbar}$ [1, 5]. The author states that this equation contains ordinary Hamiltonian dynamics but adds physical content.

Its resemblance to the Schrödinger equation is described as historically misleading. The ensemble wavefunction $\signal{\psi}=\sqrt{\signal{\rho}}\exp(i\energy{S}/\energy{\hbar})$ differs from the action wave. The latter has unit amplitude, $\signal{\zeta}\signal{\zeta}^*=1$, consistent with the Hamilton relations. The source states that the ensemble average $\langle\exp(i\energy{S}/\energy{\hbar})\rangle$ enters the Schrödinger wavefunction, and that loss of ensemble coherence leaves classical probability density alone.

The source says that loss of coherence removes interference visibility, distinguishing individual action-wave dynamics from the ensemble continuity equation.

For $\energy{H}=\energy{P}(\mechanical{x})+\energy{a}\nabla+\energy{b}\nabla^2$, with $\energy{P}$ a polynomial, it writes

$$
i\energy{\hbar}\frac{\partial\signal{\zeta}(\energy{S})}{\partial t}=-\signal{\zeta}\frac{\partial\energy{S}}{\partial t}=\left(\energy{P}(\mechanical{x})+\energy{a}\nabla+\energy{b}\nabla^2\right)\signal{\zeta}.
$$

Choosing $\energy{a}=-i\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}$ and $\energy{b}=-\energy{\hbar}^2/(2\mechanical{m})$ gives

$$
\frac{\partial\energy{S}}{\partial t}=-\left(\energy{P}(\mechanical{x})+\mechanical{c}\mechanical{p}+\frac{\mechanical{p}^2}{2\mechanical{m}}\right)+\frac{i\energy{\hbar}}{2\mechanical{m}}\nabla^2\energy{S}=-\energy{H}(\mechanical{x},\mechanical{p},t)+\frac{i\energy{\hbar}}{2\mechanical{m}}\nabla^2\energy{S}. \tag{12.20}
$$

The first term is familiar Hamiltonian mechanics. The extra term is proportional to $\energy{\hbar}$ and the spatial concentration of action, $\nabla^2\energy{S}$. With $\energy{\hbar}\approx10^{-34}\,\unit{kg\,m^2/s}$, it is negligible for large actions. The author argues that the correct macroscopic criterion is action relative to $\energy{\hbar}$, rather than mass or size. He estimates the action accumulation of a moving ant or falling dust grain as already about $10^{20}$ times $\energy{\hbar}$ per second.

For microscopic motion the extra term can matter. With $\mechanical{p}\simeq0$ and no interaction potential, the source gives

$$
\frac{\partial\energy{S}}{\partial t}=\frac{i\energy{\hbar}}{2\mechanical{m}}\nabla^2\energy{S}. \tag{12.21}
$$

Although diffusion-like, it is not diffusion of particles: there is one particle. The author interprets it as dephasing of the action waves.

The particle remains indivisible and localised, with dynamics linked to the local action-wave intensity and $\nabla\energy{S}=\mechanical{p}$. Action periodicity introduces a proposed intrinsic action uncertainty of $\pm\energy{\hbar}/2$. The source gives the universal statement as $\Delta\energy{S}\ge\energy{\hbar}/2$ and treats position–momentum or energy–time products as less fundamental forms.

Interference between action waves on different paths then determines particle arrival probabilities. Bright fringes correspond to maximal action-wave intensity, dark fringes to minimal intensity, and intermediate probabilities to intermediate intensity. Ensemble particle density is proportional to that local intensity, connecting it to repeated single-particle detections. The next section demonstrates the proposal.

The author also argues that action waves must be complex. A first-order time equation coupled to first- and second-order spatial derivatives needs both quadratures, $\cos(\energy{S}/\energy{\varepsilon})+i\sin(\energy{S}/\energy{\varepsilon})$. For constant $\energy{H}=\energy{V}$, a real cosine would have a time derivative proportional to $\sin(\energy{S}/\energy{\varepsilon})\,\partial\energy{S}/\partial t$, while the other side remains proportional to $i\energy{V}\cos(\energy{S}/\energy{\varepsilon})$.

He further states that a real periodic action wave would itself produce a spatially periodic intensity or probability density, which he rejects. In his account, complex action waves are fundamental at every scale and quantum mechanics inherits them. He presents this as explaining why complex functions appear in microscopic mechanics.

## Interpretation and checks

Differentiate the exponential explicitly: a Laplacian produces both an action-gradient-squared term and an imaginary action-Laplacian term. If action is constrained to be real and amplitude fixed, the proposed evolution imposes additional conditions. If action is complex, explain how its imaginary part changes amplitude. The Hamilton–Jacobi equation and a linear wave equation are not interchangeable without these steps. Check the constant-gradient free-particle limit before claiming a universal law.
