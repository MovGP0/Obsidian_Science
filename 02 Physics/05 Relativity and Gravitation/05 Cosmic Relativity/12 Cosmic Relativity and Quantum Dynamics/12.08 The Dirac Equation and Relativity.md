> [!TLDR]
> This section examines the Dirac equation's relativistic structure in the context of the author's individual-particle action dynamics.

## Physical question and model

When does a four-component equation have the physics of the Dirac equation? Matrix size alone is insufficient. The dispersion, transformation under boosts, operator algebra, probability current, and couplings must also match.

## Lecture account

The Dirac equation is usually regarded as a more general quantum dynamical equation than the Schrödinger equation. The author therefore examines its physical meaning in the same way, as part of the proposed connection with Cosmic Relativity.

Dirac sought an electron equation resembling a square root of the relativistic massive-field Klein–Gordon equation:

$$
i\energy{\hbar}\frac{\partial\signal{\psi}(\mechanical{x},t)}{\partial t}=\left(\mechanical{c}\sum_{j=1}^{3}\alpha_j\hat p_j+\beta\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2\right)\signal{\psi}(\mechanical{x},t). \tag{12.26}
$$

The source defines $\hat p_j=i\energy{\hbar}\,\partial/\partial\mechanical{x}_j$. The four-dimensional matrices $\alpha_j$ and $\beta$ obey specific algebraic relations and generalise the two-dimensional Pauli matrices. Their structure is commonly associated with four-dimensional spacetime and the relativistic relation $\energy{E}=\sqrt{\mechanical{p}^2\mechanical{c}^2+\mechanical{m}^2\mechanical{c}^4}$. With electromagnetic coupling, the equation describes a massive spin-½ particle.

The author instead interprets the Dirac equation as ensemble-averaged quantum dynamics, analogous to the Schrödinger equation, with the Hamiltonian form determined by energy. He states that its four components describe an ensemble, not one particle or one pair. He then argues that the matrix structure need not originate in relativity, using a nonrelativistic example.

Consider tunnelling between two potential wells. Nonzero tunnelling couples the wells and splits a degenerate level. The source gives ammonia, written NH₄, as an example. Let coupling energy be $\energy{E}_t=\energy{\hbar}\signal{\Omega}$. The two-level state is

$$
\signal{\psi}=c_1\signal{\psi}_1+c_2\signal{\psi}_2,\qquad c_1^2+c_2^2=1. \tag{12.27}
$$

The nonrelativistic Schrödinger equation gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
i\energy{\hbar}\frac{dc_1}{dt}&=\energy{E}_0c_1-\energy{\hbar}\signal{\Omega}c_2,\\
i\energy{\hbar}\frac{dc_2}{dt}&=-\energy{\hbar}\signal{\Omega}c_1+\energy{E}_0c_2.
\end{aligned} \tag{12.28}
$$

Setting the reference energy $\energy{E}_0=0$ gives

$$
i\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}c_1\\c_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-\signal{\Omega}\\-\signal{\Omega}&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_1\\c_2\end{bmatrix}. \tag{12.29}
$$

The source calls the eigenvalues $\pm\signal{\Omega}$ eigen-energies.

> [!note]
> The source writes NH₄ for ammonia; the usual ammonia formula is NH₃. Also, (12.29) has frequency units after division by $\energy{\hbar}$, so its eigen-energies are $\pm\energy{\hbar}\signal{\Omega}$ rather than $\pm\signal{\Omega}$.

Now give the tunnelling particle a quantised magnetic-moment projection $\pm\magnetic{\mu}$. Without tunnelling, a magnetic field in the z-direction gives energies $\pm\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}_z$. For the two spin amplitudes $u$ and $d$,

$$
i\energy{\hbar}\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}u\\d\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}_z&0\\0&-\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}_z\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}u\\d\end{bmatrix}. \tag{12.30}
$$

With both field and tunnelling, the author gives two energy values, $\energy{E}_{\pm}=\pm\sqrt{\signal{\Omega}^2\energy{\hbar}^2+\magnetic{\mu}^2\magnetic{B}_z^2}$, rather than four. For a general field direction $\mathbf n=(x,y,z)$,

$$
\energy{E}_{\pm}=\pm\sqrt{\signal{\Omega}^2\energy{\hbar}^2+\magnetic{\mu}^2\magnetic{B}_x^2+\magnetic{\mu}^2\magnetic{B}_y^2+\magnetic{\mu}^2\magnetic{B}_z^2}. \tag{12.31}
$$

The magnetic Hamiltonian $\magnetic{\mu}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\magnetic{\mathbf B}$ is

$$
\energy{H}_s=\begin{bmatrix}\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}_z&\magnetic{\mu}(\magnetic{B}_x-i\magnetic{B}_y)\\\magnetic{\mu}(\magnetic{B}_x+i\magnetic{B}_y)&-\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}_z\end{bmatrix}. \tag{12.32}
$$

The tunnelling Hamiltonian is

$$
\energy{H}_t=\energy{\hbar}\begin{bmatrix}0&-\signal{\Omega}\\-\signal{\Omega}&0\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The author argues that scalar tunnelling cannot be included in the two-by-two magnetic matrix. He proposes replicating the structure in a four-by-four form that retains the same tunnelling and magnetic physics:

$$
\energy{H}=\begin{bmatrix}
\energy{\hbar}\signal{\Omega}&0&\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}_z&\magnetic{\mu}(\magnetic{B}_x-i\magnetic{B}_y)\\
0&\energy{\hbar}\signal{\Omega}&\magnetic{\mu}(\magnetic{B}_x+i\magnetic{B}_y)&-\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}_z\\
\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}_z&\magnetic{\mu}(\magnetic{B}_x-i\magnetic{B}_y)&-\energy{\hbar}\signal{\Omega}&0\\
\magnetic{\mu}(\magnetic{B}_x+i\magnetic{B}_y)&-\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}_z&0&-\energy{\hbar}\signal{\Omega}
\end{bmatrix}. \tag{12.33}
$$

The source gives (12.31) as the eigenvalues of (12.33) and interprets the nonrelativistic problem as a four-component system. Its evolution is

$$
i\energy{\hbar}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{bmatrix}\signal{\psi}_1\\\signal{\psi}_2\\\signal{\psi}_3\\\signal{\psi}_4\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
\energy{\hbar}\signal{\Omega}&0&\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}_z&\magnetic{\mu}(\magnetic{B}_x-i\magnetic{B}_y)\\
0&\energy{\hbar}\signal{\Omega}&\magnetic{\mu}(\magnetic{B}_x+i\magnetic{B}_y)&-\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}_z\\
\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}_z&\magnetic{\mu}(\magnetic{B}_x-i\magnetic{B}_y)&-\energy{\hbar}\signal{\Omega}&0\\
\magnetic{\mu}(\magnetic{B}_x+i\magnetic{B}_y)&-\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}_z&0&-\energy{\hbar}\signal{\Omega}
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\signal{\psi}_1\\\signal{\psi}_2\\\signal{\psi}_3\\\signal{\psi}_4\end{bmatrix}. \tag{12.34}
$$

The author compares it with the Dirac structure

$$
i\energy{\hbar}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\begin{bmatrix}\signal{\psi}_1\\\signal{\psi}_2\\\signal{\psi}_3\\\signal{\psi}_4\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2&0&\mechanical{c}\mechanical{p}_z&\mechanical{c}(\mechanical{p}_x-i\mechanical{p}_y)\\
0&\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2&\mechanical{c}(\mechanical{p}_x+i\mechanical{p}_y)&-\mechanical{c}\mechanical{p}_z\\
\mechanical{c}\mechanical{p}_z&\mechanical{c}(\mechanical{p}_x-i\mechanical{p}_y)&-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2&0\\
\mechanical{c}(\mechanical{p}_x+i\mechanical{p}_y)&-\mechanical{c}\mechanical{p}_z&0&-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\signal{\psi}_1\\\signal{\psi}_2\\\signal{\psi}_3\\\signal{\psi}_4\end{bmatrix}. \tag{12.35}
$$

The nonrelativistic spin-and-tunnelling equation is summarised as

$$
i\energy{\hbar}\frac{\partial\signal{\psi}}{\partial t}=\left(\magnetic{\mu}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\magnetic{\mathbf B}+\energy{\hbar}\beta\signal{\Omega}\right)\signal{\psi}. \tag{12.36}
$$

Because its matrix form matches Dirac's, the author argues that the same ensemble interpretation applies.

Starting from $\energy{E}^2=\signal{\Omega}^2\energy{\hbar}^2+\magnetic{\mu}^2\magnetic{B}_x^2+\magnetic{\mu}^2\magnetic{B}_y^2+\magnetic{\mu}^2\magnetic{B}_z^2$, the source writes the matrix algebra in a normalised-looking form:

$$
(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\magnetic{\mathbf B}+\beta)(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\magnetic{\mathbf B}+\beta)=1+\magnetic{B}^2. \tag{12.37}
$$

Expanding,

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(\alpha_1\magnetic{B}_x+\alpha_2\magnetic{B}_y+\alpha_3\magnetic{B}_z)(\alpha_1\magnetic{B}_x+\alpha_2\magnetic{B}_y+\alpha_3\magnetic{B}_z)+\beta^2\\
&\quad+(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\magnetic{\mathbf B})\beta+\beta(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\magnetic{\mathbf B})=1+\magnetic{B}_x^2+\magnetic{B}_y^2+\magnetic{B}_z^2.
\end{aligned} \tag{12.38}
$$

This gives

$$
\alpha_i\alpha_j+\alpha_j\alpha_i=2\delta_{ij},\qquad\beta^2=1,\qquad\alpha_i\beta+\beta\alpha_i=0. \tag{12.39}
$$

These are the Dirac matrix relations, here obtained through the source's nonrelativistic example rather than through spacetime or Klein–Gordon arguments. In terms of Pauli matrices,

$$
\alpha_i=\begin{bmatrix}0&\sigma_i\\\sigma_i&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\beta=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}. \tag{12.40}
$$

The entries are two-by-two blocks. The compact eigen-equation is

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\signal{\Omega}-\energy{E}&\magnetic{\mu}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\magnetic{\mathbf B}\\\magnetic{\mu}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\magnetic{\mathbf B}&-\signal{\Omega}-\energy{E}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\signal{\psi}_u\\\signal{\psi}_d\end{bmatrix}=0. \tag{12.41}
$$

The source identifies a large component $\signal{\psi}_u$ and a small component $\signal{\psi}_d$, writing

$$
\signal{\psi}_u=\frac{\magnetic{\mu}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\magnetic{\mathbf B}}{\signal{\Omega}-\energy{E}}\signal{\psi}_d,\qquad\signal{\psi}_d=\frac{\magnetic{\mu}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\magnetic{\mathbf B}}{-\signal{\Omega}-\energy{E}}\signal{\psi}_u.
$$

For a small field it gives $\energy{E}\approx\signal{\Omega}+\magnetic{\mu}^2\magnetic{B}^2/(2\signal{\Omega})$, then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\signal{\psi}_u&=\frac{\magnetic{\mu}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\magnetic{\mathbf B}}{\magnetic{\mu}^2\magnetic{B}^2/(2\signal{\Omega})}\signal{\psi}_d,\qquad
\signal{\psi}_d=\frac{\magnetic{\mu}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\magnetic{\mathbf B}}{2\signal{\Omega}}\signal{\psi}_u,\\
\frac{\signal{\psi}_d}{\signal{\psi}_u}&=\frac{\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}}{2\signal{\Omega}}.
\end{aligned} \tag{12.42}
$$

> [!note]
> The source omits its earlier energy and field scale factors in (12.37)–(12.38) without defining the normalisation. From (12.41) onward, $\signal{\Omega}$ is used like an energy, whereas it earlier denoted a frequency with coupling energy $\energy{\hbar}\signal{\Omega}$. The displayed component relations also omit the minus signs obtained by rearranging (12.41); their signs then change in the small-field formulas. These printed conventions and expressions are retained.

The author concludes that the four-component form need not derive from STR spacetime or Lorentz transformations, and that the equation should not automatically be interpreted as a physical field's evolution. He offers the nonrelativistic example as guidance for an ensemble interpretation of the Dirac equation.

## Interpretation and checks

A tensor product of two independent two-state systems normally has commuting Hamiltonian contributions and additive eigenvalues. Anticommuting contributions instead give a square-root spectrum; replacing one by the other changes the physical model unless a justified mapping is supplied. Check the zero-field and zero-tunnelling limits and use modulus-squared normalisation for complex state coefficients. An algebraic analogy with Dirac matrices does not derive relativistic electron dynamics from an arbitrary double well.
