> [!TLDR]
> The author proposes that the wavefunction describes a statistical ensemble through the square root of probability density, rather than a single particle's physical wave.

## Physical question and model

Does probability continuity uniquely determine the Schrödinger equation? A continuity law constrains density and current but does not fully specify phase evolution. Many different dynamical laws can conserve the same total probability.

## Lecture account

The Schrödinger equation completed the usual nonrelativistic quantum framework after earlier isolated results and special rules. Schrödinger developed its first-order time evolution through four papers in 1926 [3], initially seeking a wave-like description inspired by de Broglie's proposal [4]. The final equation was

$$
i\energy{\hbar}\frac{\partial\signal{\psi}(\mechanical{x},t)}{\partial t}=-\frac{\energy{\hbar}^2}{2\mechanical{m}}\nabla^2\signal{\psi}(\mechanical{x},t)+\energy{V}\signal{\psi}. \tag{12.2}
$$

Here $\energy{V}(\mechanical{x})$ represents the interaction potential energy. The spatial operator was interpreted through kinetic energy $\energy{E}_k=\mechanical{p}^2/(2\mechanical{m})$, with momentum represented in the source by $i\energy{\hbar}\nabla$. This identifies the right side with the Hamiltonian in the general case.

The author questions that interpretation. In Schrödinger's wave-based derivation, $\nabla^2$ represented spatial curvature, as in a familiar second-order wave equation:

$$
\nabla^2\signal{\psi}(\mechanical{x},t)-\frac{\partial^2\signal{\psi}(\mechanical{x},t)}{\mechanical{u}^2\partial t^2}=0. \tag{12.3}
$$

Here $\mechanical{u}$ is wave speed. In that equation the Laplacian does not represent kinetic energy.

Despite interpretational difficulties, Schrödinger evolution accurately calculates statistical averages. Born's additional rule identifies the absolute square with probability density:

$$
|\signal{\psi}|^2=\signal{\psi}(\mechanical{x})\signal{\psi}^*(\mechanical{x})=\signal{\rho}(\mechanical{x}).
$$

This supplies statistical predictions for observations. The equation $\partial\signal{\psi}/\partial t=-(i/\energy{\hbar})\hat H\signal{\psi}$ resembles the Hamilton or Hamilton–Jacobi equation

$$
\frac{\partial\energy{S}(\mechanical{x},\mechanical{p},t)}{\partial t}=-\energy{H}. \tag{12.4}
$$

But the author stresses that the wavefunction is not the action $\energy{S}$, so the two equations are not obviously equivalent.

The initial interpretation of a physical matter wave had lasting influence on particle–wave duality. The source argues that experiments did not require identifying the material particle itself with a wave. They required only wave-like behaviour of its dynamics, with periodicity $\energy{\hbar}$ in action.

Born's rule gives $\signal{\psi}\signal{\psi}^*=\signal{\rho}(\mechanical{x},t)$, so the equation evolves a complex square root of probability density. The wavefunction is not a real spatial wave, and quantum predictions concern ensemble averages even when one particle is being studied. The author argues that attempts to interpret it as the dynamics of one system, analogous to Newton or Hamilton–Jacobi dynamics, have left serious foundational problems.

He therefore proposes taking the mathematical form directly: the wavefunction is the complex square root of a classical probability density. It would describe a statistical ensemble, rather than an individual system's dynamics or a hypothetical wave nature of matter.

The author takes this further: if the wavefunction belongs to an ensemble, the Schrödinger equation need not be an individual system's dynamical equation. He proposes to identify it with a classical continuity equation [5], describing evolution of ensemble probability density:

$$
\frac{\partial\signal{\rho}(\mechanical{x},t)}{\partial t}=-\nabla\cdot(\signal{\rho}\mechanical{\mathbf v}). \tag{12.5}
$$

Here $\mechanical{\mathbf v}$ is flow velocity and $\signal{\rho}\mechanical{\mathbf v}$ the density current. Its divergence measures the net flow out of a volume, giving the change in the density there. The author stresses the equation's direct conservation meaning and its first-order time structure, similar to Schrödinger evolution. He examines whether the wavefunction Laplacian can express the divergence of the probability current.

For positive density, write $\signal{\rho}=\signal{\psi}\signal{\psi}^*$ with a single-valued complex square root

$$
\signal{\psi}=\sqrt{\signal{\rho}}\exp\left(\frac{i\energy{\phi}}{\energy{\phi}_0}\right),\qquad\signal{A}=\sqrt{\signal{\rho}}.
$$

The wavefunction current contains velocity times amplitude. Its gradient is

$$
\nabla\signal{\psi}=\frac{i}{\energy{\phi}_0}\signal{A}\exp\left(\frac{i\energy{\phi}}{\energy{\phi}_0}\right)\nabla\energy{\phi}+\exp\left(\frac{i\energy{\phi}}{\energy{\phi}_0}\right)\nabla\signal{A}=\frac{i}{\energy{\phi}_0}\signal{\psi}\nabla\energy{\phi}+\signal{\psi}\frac{\nabla\signal{A}}{\signal{A}}. \tag{12.6}
$$

The first term contains the current. Hamiltonian mechanics gives $\nabla\energy{S}=\mechanical{\mathbf p}=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{\mathbf v}$. Identifying $\energy{\phi}=\energy{S}$ makes the phase-gradient term proportional to velocity. The additional amplitude-gradient term arises because density has been expressed through its square root. The full continuity equation is

$$
\frac{\partial(\signal{\psi}\signal{\psi}^*)}{\partial t}=\signal{\psi}^*\frac{\partial\signal{\psi}}{\partial t}+\signal{\psi}\frac{\partial\signal{\psi}^*}{\partial t}=-\nabla\cdot(\signal{\rho}\mechanical{\mathbf v}). \tag{12.7}
$$

With $\nabla\energy{\phi}=\nabla\energy{S}$, multiplying (12.6) by $\signal{\psi}^*$ gives

$$
\signal{\psi}^*\nabla\signal{\psi}=\frac{i}{\energy{\phi}_0}\signal{\rho}\nabla\energy{S}+\signal{A}\nabla\signal{A}. \tag{12.8}
$$

Its complex conjugate is

$$
\signal{\psi}\nabla\signal{\psi}^*=-\frac{i}{\energy{\phi}_0}\signal{\rho}\nabla\energy{S}+\signal{A}\nabla\signal{A}. \tag{12.9}
$$

Subtracting,

$$
\signal{\psi}^*\nabla\signal{\psi}-\signal{\psi}\nabla\signal{\psi}^*=\frac{2i}{\energy{\phi}_0}\signal{\rho}\nabla\energy{S}=\frac{2\mechanical{m}i}{\energy{\phi}_0}\signal{\rho}\mechanical{\mathbf v}. \tag{12.10}
$$

Thus the classical continuity equation becomes

$$
\signal{\psi}^*\frac{\partial\signal{\psi}}{\partial t}+\signal{\psi}\frac{\partial\signal{\psi}^*}{\partial t}=\nabla\cdot\left[\frac{i\energy{\phi}_0}{2\mechanical{m}}\left(\signal{\psi}^*\nabla\signal{\psi}-\signal{\psi}\nabla\signal{\psi}^*\right)\right]=\frac{i\energy{\phi}_0}{2\mechanical{m}}\left(\signal{\psi}^*\nabla^2\signal{\psi}-\signal{\psi}\nabla^2\signal{\psi}^*\right). \tag{12.11}
$$

The author stresses that this expression contains neither an explicit Hamiltonian nor a matter wave. The function $\signal{\psi}(\mechanical{x},t)$ is the complex square root of ensemble probability density. The Laplacian comes from current divergence, and mass appears through $\mechanical{\mathbf v}=\nabla\energy{S}/\mechanical{m}$. In this derivation, kinetic energy does not appear. The factor two arises from expressing current as a difference of two terms, rather than from $\energy{E}_k=\mechanical{p}^2/(2\mechanical{m})$.

Equation (12.11) is also the sum of the Schrödinger equation multiplied by $\signal{\psi}^*$ and its conjugate. The source identifies the free-particle equation as

$$
\frac{\partial\signal{\psi}}{\partial t}=\frac{i\energy{\phi}_0}{2\mechanical{m}}\nabla^2\signal{\psi}. \tag{12.12}
$$

The action scale $\energy{\phi}_0$ is identified empirically with $\energy{\hbar}$. The next section explains the author's proposed physical reason for that scale.

Adding $i\signal{C}(\mechanical{x},t)\signal{\psi}$ does not alter the density continuity equation, because its contributions cancel between the equation and its conjugate. The general form is then

$$
\frac{\partial\signal{\psi}}{\partial t}=\frac{i\energy{\hbar}}{2\mechanical{m}}\nabla^2\signal{\psi}+i\signal{C}(\mechanical{x},t)\signal{\psi}. \tag{12.13}
$$

The author identifies this with the full Schrödinger equation and concludes that it describes statistical density evolution, rather than the dynamical history of an individual particle. In standard form,

$$
i\energy{\hbar}\frac{\partial\signal{\psi}}{\partial t}=-\frac{\energy{\hbar}^2}{2\mechanical{m}}\nabla^2\signal{\psi}+\energy{V}(\mechanical{x},t)\signal{\psi},\qquad\energy{V}=-\energy{\hbar}\signal{C}. \tag{12.14}
$$

He presents this as resolving many wavefunction and quantum-foundation problems [1, 6, 7]. For Cosmic Relativity, the claimed significance is that particles and their dynamics occupy real space, while the wavefunction is not a physical object propagating there. It supplies only ensemble-averaged physical quantities.

The relation $\signal{\psi}\signal{\psi}^*=\signal{\rho}$ is taken as the mathematical definition of probability density. The source adds that the phase action is averaged over the statistical ensemble, and writes this explicitly as

$$
\signal{\psi}(\mechanical{x},t)=\sqrt{\signal{\rho}}\left\langle\exp\left(\frac{i\energy{S}}{\energy{\hbar}}\right)\right\rangle. \tag{12.15}
$$

Angle brackets denote the ensemble average. The author proposes that individual-particle dynamics is controlled by the periodic function $f(\energy{S})=\exp(i\energy{S}/\energy{\hbar})$, called an *action wave*. He identifies this as the only wave in quantum dynamics, rejecting a material matter wave [1]. Its ensemble average appears in the Schrödinger wavefunction. The next section examines this single-particle dynamical factor.

> [!note]
> Equation (12.15) and the preceding density identity are both retained. In general, the average of unit-modulus phase factors need not have unit modulus. Thus the displayed expression gives $|\signal{\psi}|^2=\signal{\rho}|\langle\exp(i\energy{S}/\energy{\hbar})\rangle|^2$ unless an additional normalisation or phase-coherence condition is imposed.

The source argues that this ensemble interpretation removes wavefunction collapse and the measurement problem. A single observation does not change an ensemble probability density (Fig. 12.1a). For a measuring apparatus, the joint state is interpreted as a joint probability density with correlations, rather than a physical system–apparatus state that collapses. A single observation selects one permitted pair from the ensemble without changing the ensemble description (Fig. 12.1b).

For the two-path case, the source uses

$$
|\signal{\psi}(s)\rangle=\frac{|x_1\rangle+|x_2\rangle}{\sqrt2},
$$

and for the correlated system–apparatus ensemble,

$$
|\signal{\psi}_j(s,A)\rangle=\frac{|x_1\rangle|A_1\rangle+|x_2\rangle|A_2\rangle}{\sqrt2}.
$$

Here the state labels identify paths and apparatus outcomes. The source says that interpreting such a wavefunction as one particle's individual history creates the conventional collapse problem. It next considers a single particle with two dynamical alternatives, $\signal{\psi}=a\signal{\psi}_1+b\signal{\psi}_2$.

The two path probabilities are written as $a^2$ and $b^2$. Because the particle is indivisible, a detection occurs in only one path. In a single-system reading, finding path 2 changes the subsequent state to $\signal{\psi}'=\signal{\psi}_2$ and removes the path-1 term. The source describes this unexplained collapse as an additional rule outside Schrödinger dynamics.

In the proposed ensemble account, the particle always occupies one path while its action waves extend through both. Detection reveals the occupied path. The author states that action waves carry neither energy nor momentum, so no energy–momentum conservation condition forces them to collapse into the detected path. Blocking or redirecting an action wave changes interference probabilities but, in his account, creates no physical inconsistency. An individual result does not alter the ensemble wavefunction.

For measurement, let system states be $s_1,s_2,s_3,\ldots$ and correlated apparatus responses $A_1,A_2,A_3,\ldots$. The apparatus may be a macroscopic instrument or an observer. After interaction, the standard joint state is

$$
\signal{\Psi}_j=a\signal{\psi}(s_1)\signal{\psi}(A_1)+b\signal{\psi}(s_2)\signal{\psi}(A_2)+c\signal{\psi}(s_3)\signal{\psi}(A_3)+\cdots. \tag{12.16}
$$

One observation shows one apparatus result. If it is $A_2$, the usual collapsed joint state is $\signal{\Psi}'_j=\signal{\psi}(s_2)\signal{\psi}(A_2)$. The unresolved question, in the source's account, is how that one pair becomes realised.

The author instead assigns the joint wavefunction to the ensemble. The apparatus senses the actual system state in an event and gives its uniquely correlated response. The joint superposition describes the ensemble rather than one observation. Its probabilities are supplemented by the averaged action factor $\langle\exp(i\energy{S}/\energy{\hbar})\rangle$. Coherent action waves with a fixed phase relation retain this averaged factor. The source states that otherwise the average reduces to $\exp(i0)=1$.

> [!note]
> The source's incoherent-limit statement is retained. Averaging phase factors is not generally equivalent to setting their phase to zero. For example, a uniform phase distribution gives $(2\pi)^{-1}\int_0^{2\pi}e^{i\theta}\,d\theta=0$, whereas $\exp(i0)=1$.

The source concludes that collapse and measurement problems disappear because the wavefunction refers to a statistical ensemble, not a single quantum system or history.

One central question remains: if a particle has no physical wave nature and does not pass through both slits, why do individual arrivals build an interference pattern? The author identifies this as the remaining “only mystery” of quantum mechanics [11], to be addressed by individual-particle dynamics.

The source also cites Einstein's repeated support for an ensemble interpretation. In “Physics and Reality” [8], Einstein wrote:

> The $\signal{\psi}$-function does not in any way describe a condition which could be that of a single system; it relates rather to many systems, to ‘an ensemble of systems’ in the sense of statistical mechanics. If, except for certain special cases, the $\signal{\psi}$-function furnishes only statistical data concerning measurable magnitudes, the reason lies not only in the fact that the operation of measuring introduces unknown elements, which can be grasped only statistically, but also because of the very fact that the $\signal{\psi}$-function does not, in any sense, describe the condition of one single system. The Schrödinger equation determines the time variations which are experienced by the ensemble of systems which may exist with or without external action on the single system.

Einstein recognised that an ensemble interpretation alone did not fully explain quantum dynamics. Interference still requires physical processes in real space and time. The author identifies that as the next task, while maintaining that assigning the wavefunction to an ensemble already removes collapse and measurement problems because one outcome does not alter ensemble probability density.

If Schrödinger evolution is not an individual microscopic system's dynamical law, what equation does evolve that system's physical state? The next section asks for the atomic and subatomic counterpart of Newton's law.

## Interpretation and checks

Deriving continuity from Schrödinger evolution is valid for the stated Hermitian Hamiltonian. Reversing that implication requires extra assumptions about the current, linearity, phase dynamics, and potential. The polar decomposition also yields a real phase equation, not only continuity. Check normalisation after averaging complex phases: the mean of unit-modulus factors need not have unit modulus. An ensemble interpretation alone does not provide a complete single-run measurement model or resolve all correlated-outcome tests.
