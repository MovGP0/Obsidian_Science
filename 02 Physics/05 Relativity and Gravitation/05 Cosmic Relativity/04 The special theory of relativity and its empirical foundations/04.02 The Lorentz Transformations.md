> [!TLDR]
> The Lorentz transformations relate spatial and time coordinates while preserving the postulated light speed between inertial frames.

## Physical question and model

Which transformation preserves the spacetime interval between inertial descriptions? Assume coincident origins, relative motion along one axis, linearity from homogeneity, spatial isotropy, and a common vacuum light speed. Light rays preserve the null condition, not their spatial distance or travel duration separately.

## Lecture account

Once the invariance of the relative velocity of light is assumed, the formal expressions of the LT follow. The light postulate translates to the invariance of the distance $d\mechanical{l}$ travelled by light in a duration $dt$. Then the quantities $d\mechanical{l}$ and $dt$ should both transform in a way that preserves their ratio as a constant. In one frame O, we have

$$
d\mechanical{l}=\sqrt{d\mechanical{x}^2+d\mechanical{y}^2+d\mechanical{z}^2}=\mechanical{c}dt \tag{4.1}
$$

In another frame O′ that is in relative motion at the velocity $\mechanical{v}$, we demand

$$
d\mechanical{l}'=\sqrt{d\mechanical{x}'^2+d\mechanical{y}'^2+d\mechanical{z}'^2}=\mechanical{c}dt'. \tag{4.2}
$$

Clearly, the required transformations will be very different from the Galilean transformations because the time coordinate also needs to transform to keep the ratio $d\mechanical{l}/dt$ an invariant. $d\mechanical{l}/dt=d\mechanical{l}'/dt'=\mechanical{c}$ for light is possible only if both spatial and temporal intervals transform in the same way when one goes from a stationary frame to another frame that is moving at the velocity $\mechanical{v}$. We already know the first order part of the transformation of space as $\mechanical{x}'=\mechanical{x}-\mechanical{v}t=\mechanical{c}t(1-\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c})$, for motion along the x-axis. Then, with $\mechanical{x}=\mechanical{c}t$ for light, the first order part of the transformation of time must be $t'=t(1-\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c})=t-\mechanical{v}\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{c}^2$.

I want to highlight the conceptual shift introduced at this stage. In a Galilean picture one would have said that light would have reached the distance $\mechanical{x}'=\mechanical{c}'t'=\mechanical{c}'t$ where the relative velocity of light is $\mechanical{c}'=\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{v}$. The distance is then $\mechanical{x}'=\mechanical{c}t-\mechanical{v}t$. Instead, one is insisting that light reached the distance $\mechanical{x}'$ at the relative velocity $\mechanical{c}$ (instead of $\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{v}$). So, the relative distance travelled by light remains the same, to first order in $\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$. But it took less time, $t'=t-\mechanical{v}t/\mechanical{c}$, at the same invariant relative velocity. *Since the observer has no access to the light that has gone past, this claim can be maintained as a postulate*. But, this requires that the time $t'$ in the physical clocks in the frame O′ is very different from the time $t$ in the frame O.

The complete linear transformations that agree with the postulate that $d\mechanical{l}^2=d\mechanical{l}'^2$ are now easily found:

$$
\begin{aligned}
d\mechanical{x}'^2+d\mechanical{y}'^2+d\mechanical{z}'^2-\mechanical{c}^2dt'^2
&=a^2(d\mechanical{x}-\mechanical{v}dt)^2+d\mechanical{y}^2+d\mechanical{z}^2-a^2\mechanical{c}^2(dt-\mechanical{v}\,d\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{c}^2)^2\\
&=a^2d\mechanical{x}^2+d\mechanical{y}^2+d\mechanical{z}^2+a^2\mechanical{v}^2dt^2-2a^2\mechanical{v}\,d\mechanical{x}\,dt-a^2\mechanical{c}^2dt^2-a^2\mechanical{v}^2d\mechanical{x}^2/\mechanical{c}^2+2a^2\mechanical{v}\,d\mechanical{x}\,dt\\
&=a^2(1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2)d\mechanical{x}^2+d\mechanical{y}^2+d\mechanical{z}^2-a^2\mechanical{c}^2(1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2)dt^2.
\end{aligned} \tag{4.3}
$$

We get $a=1/\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}$. This factor is conventionally represented by the symbol $\gamma$. The transformations that follow from the light hypothesis, $d\mechanical{l}'=d\mechanical{l}$, are then

$$
\mechanical{x}'=\gamma(\mechanical{x}-\mechanical{v}t) \tag{4.4}
$$

$$
\mechanical{y}'=\mechanical{y} \tag{4.5}
$$

$$
\mechanical{z}'=\mechanical{z} \tag{4.6}
$$

$$
t'=\gamma(t-\mechanical{v}\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{c}^2) \tag{4.7}
$$

> [!note] Source interval symbol
> The source uses $d\mechanical{l}$ for spatial distance in Eqs. (4.1)–(4.2), then states $d\mechanical{l}'=d\mechanical{l}$ when deriving the invariant space-time quadratic form in Eq. (4.3). The source's symbol is retained.

The $\gamma$ factor brings a velocity-dependent correction to the lengths and durations in a moving frame. This depends on the ‘speed’ $\mechanical{v}$ of motion. We have already discussed (in [[03.06 Experiments Designed to Detect the Stationary Ether|Chapter 3]]) the role of the additional first order term, $-\mechanical{v}\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{c}^2$, in the Lorentz transformation of time. In Lorentz's theory, it amounted to a position-dependent formal ‘adjustment’ of the time of the clocks in the relatively moving frame O′, to keep Maxwell's equations invariant to first order in $\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$. Its origin was clarified by Poincaré as the residual position-dependent real delay due to the synchronisation using light signals, done in a frame moving at a velocity $\mechanical{v}$ *without being aware of such inertial motion* [6]. But in Einstein's theory, the relative velocity of light is an invariant constant and there are no such delays, ‘by postulate’. Then this term assumes a puzzling reality—even if all clocks are synchronised in the frame O′, according to the observers in O′, they have a real position-dependent asynchronous nature, quantified by the term $-\mechanical{v}\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{c}^2$, relative to the stationary frame O. A clock in O′ at the position $\mechanical{x}$ of the frame O runs behind a clock at the origin $\mechanical{x}=0$ by the amount $\mechanical{v}\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{c}^2$.

It is important to mention a fact that is usually not realised in the discussions of the Lorentz transformations, either in textbooks or in research papers. The term $-\mechanical{v}\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{c}^2$ in the LT for time is *not testable* because it is not accessible in any experiment! It is a purely hypothetical term, representing the time in a clock at distance $\mechanical{x}$, relative to the time in a local clock at $\mechanical{x}=0$, and moving at the relative velocity $\mechanical{v}$. To understand the significance of this, consider an infinitesimal separation $d\mechanical{x}$. To verify that the time in a clock at the distance $d\mechanical{x}$ is $\gamma(t-\mechanical{v}\,d\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{c}^2)$, one needs to “see” the clock (know the time) at the distance $d\mechanical{x}$. But, this is not possible unless a signal (light) is brought back from $\mechanical{x}+d\mechanical{x}$ to $\mechanical{x}$. Now, the difficulty arises that we need to assume the nature of propagation of light to correct for the duration of propagation, to ensure a correct assessment of time at the distant clock. The whole frame could be inertially moving at some velocity, which cannot be known because of the PoR. Then the duration of propagation depends on whether the true propagation is Galilean or not. Clearly, there is no way to uniquely and unambiguously assign a time to the distant clock independent of the assumption regarding the propagation of light. The assumption of an invariant relative velocity gives one consistent result, and the assumption of the Galilean propagation gives another consistent result. Therefore, the full Lorentz transformations cannot be tested in an experiment in a manner that is independent of the hypothesis used in a particular theory of relativity. We will discuss this in more depth in the next chapter.

## Interpretation and checks

Expand the transformed interval and cancel the mixed terms. Matching its spatial and temporal coefficients fixes the common scale factor to the Lorentz factor. Check the inverse transformation by changing the sign of relative velocity. At low speed the spatial rule approaches the Galilean one, but the relativity-of-simultaneity term still matters for separated events. Preserving null rays alone also permits an overall conformal scale, so reciprocity and the interval normalisation must be specified.
