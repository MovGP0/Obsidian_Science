## Physical question and model

Can unipolar induction be described without an absolute velocity? Specify the complete circuit and the frame of each field measurement. The local Lorentz force acts on charges with a velocity defined in that same frame; a second frame uses transformed electric and magnetic fields.

## Lecture account

Going further with the results in electrodynamics in the 1905 paper, Einstein concluded that the unipolar induction problem, which was one of the starting points in the paper, was also solved. After establishing the relations between the electric and magnetic fields in a moving system, Einstein wrote,

> We can see that in the theory developed, the electromotive force merely plays the role of an auxiliary concept, whose introduction is due to the circumstance that the electric and magnetic forces do not have an existence independent of the state of motion of the coordinate system.
>
> It is further clear that the asymmetry mentioned in the Introduction when considering the currents produced by the relative motion of a magnet and a conductor, disappears. Questions as to the “seat” of the electrodynamic electromotive forces (unipolar machines) also become pointless.

For Einstein, this must have been a remarkable triumph. In fact, while writing about the ideas and methods that led to the General Theory of Relativity several years later [7], Einstein wrote (italics are mine),

> The following idea on Faraday's magneto-electric induction—so far not mentioned—played a leading role for me when I established the theory of special relativity.
>
> According to Faraday, during the movement of a magnet relative to an electric circuit, an electric current is induced in the latter. *It is irrelevant whether the magnet or the conductor is moved; what counts only is the relative motion. But according to the Maxwell-Lorentz theory, the theoretical interpretation of the phenomenon is very different for the two cases*:
>
> If the magnet is moved, there exists in space a magnetic field variable with time, which, according to Maxwell, forms closed lines of an electric force, i.e., a physically real electric field; this electric field then puts the movable electric masses in the conductor into motion.
>
> However, no electric field arises if the magnet is at rest and the circuit is moved; instead, the current in the conductor is created because the electricities moving with it due to the (mechanically forced) movement relative to the magnetic field suffer an electromotive force, which Lorentz introduced hypothetically.
>
> *The idea that these two cases should essentially be different was unbearable to me. According to my conviction, the difference between the two could only lie in the choice of the point of view, but not in a real difference (in the reality of nature)*. As seen from the magnet, there was certainly no electric field; whereas seen from the circuit there certainly was an electric field. Therefore, the existence of the electric field was a relative one, depending on the state of motion of the coordinate system used; and only the electric and magnetic fields combined, aside from the state of motion of the observer or coordinate system, could be granted a kind of objective reality. This phenomenon of magneto-electric induction forced me to postulate the principle of (special) relativity.

Little did Einstein seem to have been aware of the extensive set of experiments Faraday and several others did with disk magnets and conductors in azimuthal relative motion, where the reciprocity is fully broken [2, 10]. There is indeed an intriguing asymmetry observed in the relative motion between a magnet and a conductor, as Faraday extensively wrote and commented about. I discussed this in the previous chapter ([[03.09 Experiments on the Unipolar Induction|Section 3.9]]).

The unipolar induction remains mysterious and it is not adequately explained to this date. By and large, textbooks avoid a full discussion, especially the cases when there is a relative motion without the physical effect of an induction of a current, and when there is no relative motion at all, but there is an electric field that is proportional to the absolute velocity. The former case is when the conductor is stationary near a pole of the cylindrical magnet, and the magnet is rotated at a uniform angular velocity. The latter situation occurs when the conductor and the magnet are rotated together. The enigma presented by the unipolar induction was unbearable to Faraday because of the puzzling asymmetry observed in the rotational case, in contrast to the simple symmetry in the case of the linear motion. Faraday tried to reason that the magnetic field did not rotate with the magnet. With such an ad hoc reasoning one can of course explain the results, but this reasoning can be consistent only when the magnetic field is absolutely uniform. Any azimuthal variation in the magnetic field has to necessarily follow the material magnet. Thus, Faraday's ad hoc solution is not generally consistent. Also, it is perhaps important to state that whether the relative motion is inertial or not is immaterial for the Lorentz force law for a charge $\electric{q}$ in the conductor. The law of the transformation of a magnetic field to an electric field in the frame of the charge, $\electric{\mathbf E}=\mechanical{\mathbf v}(\mechanical{\mathbf r})\times\magnetic{\mathbf B}$, requires only the local magnetic field $\magnetic{\mathbf B}$ and the instantaneous relative velocity $\mechanical{\mathbf v}$. Then the force on a charge $\electric{q}$ in the conductor is $\mechanical{\mathbf F}=\electric{q}\electric{\mathbf E}$. This can easily be checked in a real experiment.

So, the phenomenon of unipolar induction is indeed a vexing problem, hidden under a several rug in physics for more than a century, resisting a consistent and complete discussion in scientific discourses. The fact that this has been a serious counter-example to a theory of relativity based on relative velocities will become clear when we analyse the experiments and provide the complete solutions later, in the framework of the theory of Cosmic Relativity. In fact, when I first saw the enormous currents that flow in a circuit consisting of *a conductor and a magnet rotating together*, the conceptual implication was unbearable to me as well. The only solution was what was already suggested by the Maxwell-Lorentz distinction of absolute real motion of the conductor and the magnet—the one that Einstein rejected, as we see in the quoted passage. For the time being, let us note that Einstein would not have been able to assert, had he been aware of the full set of experiments done by Faraday and Ampère in this context, that there were no phenomena in mechanics or electrodynamics that required the concept of absolute rest in a preferred global frame—the unipolar induction in its details is a counter example, just as Newton's pseudo-forces in rotating frames are. The way it is dealt with in present day physics is inadequate and incomplete, to say the least.

## Interpretation and checks

Compute a potential difference between named contacts, including the moving conductor and return path. Magnet rotation is not equivalent to translating a nonuniform field pattern. An axially symmetric rotating magnet can leave its external field stationary. A useful boundary check is zero conductor velocity in a static magnetic field: the magnetic part of the motional emf vanishes, but electrostatic fields from charge redistribution may remain.
