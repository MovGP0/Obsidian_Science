> [!TLDR]
> The author distinguishes tests of the Principle of Relativity from tests of one-way light-speed invariance.

## Physical question and model

What part of a light-speed statement is directly tested by a given apparatus? Round-trip speed uses one clock, whereas a one-way coordinate speed needs a rule for comparing separated clocks. Source independence, isotropy, and the synchronisation convention are related but distinct issues.

## Lecture account

It is of great importance to notice and realise that the demarcating feature of Einstein's STR, namely the invariance of the velocity of light relative to all inertial observers, has never been tested in any experiment in more than a century after the theory. The usual misconception that the Michelson-Morley experiment is the proof of the invariance of the relative velocity of light, repeated in most of modern textbooks, is unfortunate and seriously incorrect. If that were the case, Einstein could have taken it as a fact, 18 years after the results were known, and the physicists of the time could have dismissed the Lorentz-Poincaré approach as erroneous and inconsistent. That was not how things unfolded in the history of these theories. The M-M experiment proved that the motion of the Earth through the ether could not be detected by comparing the two-way propagation of light in different directions in a moving frame [12]. It can be interpreted as proving that *the two-way relative speed of light is an invariant constant*. But, it still does not imply that the genuine one-way relative speed of light is an invariant constant.

It seems very important to emphasise that the STR is derived from two distinct postulates, the PoR and the light hypothesis. The PoR is supported by all available empirical evidence, especially the experiments in the class of the M-M experiment. The same experiment, with its single null result, cannot be the experimental evidence for the light hypothesis as well. Otherwise, the two hypotheses could have been reduced to one. Hence, the frequently made statement that the M-M experiment is the experimental proof for the invariance of the relative velocity of light is blatantly incorrect. It exposes the thorough confusion prevalent regarding the empirical basis of the theories of relativity. The mysterious nature of the light hypothesis was well articulated by the astronomer C. Nordmann [13],

> ... there is still something infinitely troubling in the Einsteinian system. This system is admirably coherent, but it rests on a particular conception of the propagation of light. How are we to imagine that the propagation of a ray of light could be identical for an observer who flies away from it, and for an observer who rushes forward to meet it? If this is possible, it is in any case inconceivable to our customary mentality, and no matter how hard we try, we cannot make the mechanism and nature of that propagation intelligible.
>
> It must be confessed that there lies a mystery which eludes us. The whole Einsteinian synthesis, as coherent as it is, rests on a mystery, exactly like the revealed religions.

In fact, every physicist is troubled by the light hypothesis even though they routinely use the LT for all their calculations, and find them effective and satisfactory. However, one remains unaware whether one is using the STR or the Lorentz-Poincaré theory with an absolute frame because the LT is common to both.

It is relevant to state that Einstein himself did not take the null result of the Michelson-Morley experiment as an evidence for the constancy of the velocity of light. This is easily seen from Einstein appraisal of his theory of relativity in the Jahrbuch article in 1907, where he stated,

> It is by no means self-evident that the assumption made here, which we will call “the principle of the constancy of the velocity of light”, is actually realised in nature...

In the same article, he stated also what the real implication of the M-M experiment was,

> Suppose that the physical laws are ascertained with respect to the reference system S that is at first at rest relative to the sun. Let then the reference system S be accelerated by some external cause for awhile, and, finally, let it return to a nonaccelerated state. What will the physical laws look like when these processes are referred to the system S that is now in another state of motion? We now make the simplest possible assumption, which is also suggested by the Michelson and Morley experiment: The physical laws are independent of the state of motion of the reference system, at least if the system is not accelerated. In the ensuing considerations, we will base ourselves on this assumption, which we call “the principle of relativity,” as well as on the principle of the constancy of the velocity of light set forth above.

The paragraphs just quoted are extremely important in the context of the experimental findings discussed in this book later ([[11.04 The Experiment on the One-Way Relative Velocity of Light|Chapter 11]]). It is especially significant that Einstein himself treated his light hypothesis as a postulate that was not confirmed in any experiment, including the M-M experiment.

Since the light hypothesis is the defining physical input for the STR, we need to take a close look at how this counter-intuitive assertion was incorporated into the physical theory. This will be done in detail in the next chapter, which is the continuation of our scrutiny of the central elements of Einstein's STR. We have to be very watchful because there are some subtle issues involved. *Since the velocity of light is the ratio of a spatial interval and a temporal duration, the common second order factor of $\gamma$ (time dilation and length contraction) in the LT cancels out in the determination of the velocity; thus, the second order relativistic effects are not relevant for the main discussion of the one-way relative velocity of light*. The important point is how the synchronisation of ‘time’ is defined for a distant clock—the simultaneity of clocks is obviously of central importance if we want to define and measure a relative velocity meaningfully and reliably. In the first part of his 1905 paper, where he discussed the definition of simultaneity, Einstein wrote,

> Thus far we have only defined an “A-time” and a “B-time” but not a “time” common to A and B. The latter can now be determined by establishing by definition that the “time” needed for the light to travel from A to B is equal to the “time” it needs to travel from B to A. For, suppose a ray of light leaves from A toward B at “A-time” $t_A$, is reflected from B toward A at “B-time” $t_B$, and arrives back at A at “A-time” $t'_A$. The two clocks are synchronous by definition if $t_B-t_A=t'_A-t_B$. We assume that it is possible for this definition of synchronism to be free of contradictions, and to be so for arbitrarily many points...

This then is the ‘Einstein convention’ of synchronising clocks in the rest frame of a reference system. Then he stated,

> Based on experience, we also postulate that the quantity
>
> $$
> \frac{2\overline{AB}}{t'_A-t_A}=\mechanical{c} \tag{4.24}
> $$

We recognise that the relevant configuration is that of an arm of the Michelson interferometer. The expression “based on experience” is for the two-way speed of light between points A and B, which is the invariant constant $\mechanical{c}$. However, it will be physically unjustified to extrapolate from this to the conclusion that the one-way velocity $\overline{AB}/(t_B-t_A)=\mechanical{c}$. There is no empirical experience or logical implication that allows us to claim the constancy or otherwise of the one-way speed of light. This is because the asymmetry in the one-way propagation nearly cancels out in the two-way propagation. The conceptual jump, from the ‘experience’ of the invariance of the two-way propagation to the assertion that the relative one-way velocity itself is an invariant, was just a hypothesis and it remains so even today. Therefore, let us go ahead with the clear understanding that the constancy of the relative velocity of light demanded in the STR is a postulate that is subject to verification or refutation by real experiments.

By now, it is clear that the postulate of the invariance of the velocity of light relies on the first order ($\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$) part of the LT: $t'=t-\mechanical{v}\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{c}^2$. The measurement of a relative velocity requires a measurement of the spatial distance between two points A and B, and a measurement of the duration to traverse that distance. *The duration in this case is the difference between the time in two separate clocks at A and B, which requires synchronised clocks*. However, there is no way to know what the time is in a clock that is at ‘$\mechanical{x}$’ distance away, except by getting a signal propagating back through the distance ‘$\mechanical{x}$’, taking the duration $\delta t=\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{c}$, when the frame is at rest. If the frame is in inertial motion at the velocity $\mechanical{v}$ (relative to a global frame), $\mechanical{v}\delta t=\mechanical{v}\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{c}^2$ is the duration the signal saves in travelling back to reach the reference origin, but the observer remains unaware of the inertial motion and this gap in the duration. This intermingling of the time in spatially separated clocks and the propagation of light is why the term $-\mechanical{v}\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{c}^2$ in the LT remains hypothetical, and inaccessible to the scrutiny and verification in an experiment. This observation that I reiterate, is very important—the Lorentz transformations cannot be tested fully in any conventional experiment. For the same reason, the conventional methods of measuring a relative velocity are not capable of testing the light hypothesis. We will see in [[11.04 The Experiment on the One-Way Relative Velocity of Light|Chapter 11]] how we overcome such limitations and unambiguously test the light hypothesis by measuring the genuine one-way velocity of light relative to an inertially moving reference frame.

> [!note] Source delay expression
> The source writes $\mechanical{v}\delta t=\mechanical{v}\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{c}^2$ as a duration. With the quantities defined in the text, the left side has length units and the right side has time units. This expression is retained as printed.

> [!note]
> The invariance of the relative one-way velocity of light (the light hypothesis) is a postulate that remains unverified by a direct experiment. The null result of the Michelson-Morley experiment is a proof for the Principle of Relativity, and not the proof of the light hypothesis.

## Interpretation and checks

The lecture's statement that the light postulate has never been tested is too broad: experiments constrain round-trip isotropy and source-motion dependence. The remaining one-way convention does not make all relativistic predictions conventional. Compare [Einstein Online's clock-definition analysis](https://www.einstein-online.info/en/spotlight/now/) with the source account. A proposed anisotropy must give an invariant instrument effect after the same synchronisation rule is applied to both models.
