> [!TLDR]
> The Lorentz transformations give length contraction and time dilation for measurements between relatively moving frames.

## Physical question and model

Which pairs of events define a measured length or a moving clock interval? A rod length uses its endpoints at the same time in the measuring frame. A clock elapsed time uses two events on that one clock's worldline. These are different constraints on the Lorentz equations.

## Lecture account

Einstein derived the physical consequences of the STR from the LT. It is straightforward to derive the consequences of the LT for the spatial and temporal intervals, which I denote as $(\mechanical{\Delta x}',\Delta t')$ and $(\mechanical{\Delta x},\Delta t)$. Since the measurement of the length demands a simultaneous measurement ($\Delta t=0$) of the two ends (coordinates) of the spatial interval, the LT (Eq. (4.11)) gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{\Delta x}'&=\gamma\mechanical{\Delta x}\\
\mechanical{\Delta x}&=\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}\mechanical{\Delta x}'
\end{aligned} \tag{4.8}
$$

Thus, the length measure $\mechanical{L}'$ in the frame O′ that moves at the relative velocity $\mechanical{v}$ is equal to a shorter length $\mechanical{L}=\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}\mechanical{L}'$ in the frame O, chosen as the reference rest frame.

To find the relation between the time intervals in two frames O and O′ that are relatively moving, we turn to Einstein's derivation in his 1916 monograph [3]. Two readings that are 1 second apart ($\Delta t'=1\,\unit{s}$) in a clock at the origin of the O′ system have the $t'$-coordinates 0 and 1 (and $\mechanical{x}'=0$). The inverse transformation of the LT equation $t'=\gamma(t-\mechanical{v}\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{c}^2)$ is $t=\gamma(t'+\mechanical{v}\mechanical{x}'/\mechanical{c}^2)$; that is, the sign of the relative velocity is reversed. The transformed readings in the O frame are 0 and $\gamma$, because $\mechanical{x}'=0$. Therefore, the durations in the two frames are related as

$$
\Delta t'=\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}\,\Delta t. \tag{4.9}
$$

The time interval in the moving O′ frame is shorter. We could of course get this result from the ‘standard’ way of applying the LT. The interval $\Delta t'$ of the stationary clock in the O′ frame moves the distance $\mechanical{\Delta x}=\mechanical{v}\Delta t$ in the O frame. Then the LT (Eq. (4.14)) gives

$$
\Delta t'=\gamma(\Delta t-\mechanical{v}\mechanical{\Delta x}/\mechanical{c}^2)
=\gamma\Delta t(1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2)
=\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}\,\Delta t. \tag{4.10}
$$

I showed both simple derivations to point out that, in Einstein's theory, the local time $t'(\mechanical{x},\mechanical{v})=t-\mechanical{v}\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{c}^2$ is necessarily the real time of the clocks in a moving frame. Only then it fulfils its double role of keeping the relative velocity of light an invariant quantity and also of predicting the correct factor of the dilated time, i.e., shorter intervals in the moving clocks. This means that the previously synchronised clocks (at the points $\mechanical{x}_i$) in a relatively stationary frame become mysteriously unsynchronised if the frame moves at some velocity, according to the expression $t'_i=t_i-\mechanical{v}\mechanical{x}_i/\mechanical{c}^2$. Later, we will see that the physical justification for this in Einstein's scheme relies on his General Theory of Relativity and gravity, in spite of being in an empty space where there isn't any source of gravity. No such issue arises in a theory of relativity in an absolute privileged frame. We will postpone a discussion of this bizarre feature until we examine Einstein's own resolution of the twin-paradox ([[09.10 Einstein's Gravitational Resolution of the Paradox|Chapter 9]]).

In contrast to Lorentz's theory, there is a symmetric flip side for the physics of the STR. *The frame O′ can be chosen as the rest frame, with equal right*. Then, O is the (relatively) moving frame. The LT now are very similar, with the direction of the relative velocity reversed:

$$
\mechanical{x}=\gamma(\mechanical{x}'+\mechanical{v}t') \tag{4.11}
$$

$$
\mechanical{y}=\mechanical{y} \tag{4.12}
$$

$$
\mechanical{z}=\mechanical{z} \tag{4.13}
$$

$$
t=\gamma(t'+\mechanical{v}\mechanical{x}'/\mechanical{c}^2) \tag{4.14}
$$

> [!note] Source inverse transformations
> The source prints $\mechanical{y}=\mechanical{y}$ and $\mechanical{z}=\mechanical{z}$ in Eqs. (4.12)–(4.13), without primed coordinates. It also cites Eq. (4.14) before Eq. (4.10), although Eq. (4.14) is the inverse time transformation. These forms and references are retained.

With the LT taking the reverse role, the velocity-dependent changes in the distance and duration also flip sides—the length measure in the frame O is now shorter, compared to a similar standard in O′, and the relatively moving clocks in O run slower. A clock is a physical gadget in which the rate of a periodic physical phenomenon controlled by a fundamental interaction is regulated and monitored. It records the elapsed number of repetitions of the periodic process as a ‘duration’. *The relative time is the difference in the durations recorded by two different and independently operating clocks*. The peculiarity that the rate of such a physical clock depends on the arbitrary choice of the rest frame is the unavoidable consequence of the ‘inertial democracy’ in the STR. There is no true motion and the altered rate of a clock in the STR is not a physical effect with its origin in any physical reason, like an interaction. The length contraction and time dilation in the STR are frame-dependent kinematical effects without any identifiable physical reason. Yet, time being an accumulated quantity, the motional history of the altered time should be retrievably present in a clock, even after the relative motion ceases. Otherwise, the theory will not be consistent. We will examine later whether the STR can handle this requirement of consistency.

In the 1905 paper, Einstein correctly emphasised the motional modification of time and wrote about the time dilation effect,

> If there are two synchronous clocks in A, and one of them is moved along a closed curve with constant velocity until it has returned to A, which takes, say, $t$ sec, then this clock will lag on its arrival at A $t\mechanical{v}^2/2\mechanical{c}^2$ sec behind the clock that has not been moved.

The length contraction and time dilation in the STR were already discussed in the Lorentz-Poincaré theory, except that in the STR the effects depend on the relative velocity and they are completely reciprocal. One can choose either of the frames as the rest frame O and then the other frame is the moving frame O′. The length contraction and the time dilation are reduced to coordinate effects that change from frame to frame, depending on which frame is chosen as the frame at rest. This ‘equal choice’ is the conceptual hallmark of the STR.

Einstein wrote about the length contraction (Einstein 1916) [3],

> Thus for a coordinate system moving with the earth the mirror system of Michelson and Morley is not shortened, but it is shortened for a coordinate system which is at rest relatively to the sun.

Similarly, in Einstein's theory, there is no time dilation in a clock sharing the same frame with the observer. In Lorentz's theory, there is a physical time dilation in any clock that moves relative to the ether, irrespective of whether an observer is comoving or not. But it is undetectable *relative* to another clock in the same frame *because all clocks have the identical time dilation*.

Though Einstein had asserted that “the special theory of relativity is nothing but a contradiction-free amalgamation of the results of Maxwell-Lorentz electrodynamics and those of classical mechanics” (Ref. [7], p. 113), this is not factually true because of the light hypothesis and the rejection of an absolute reference frame.

An important consequence of the LT was a new law of the addition of velocities that is different from the Galilean addition. If a body moves the distance $d\mechanical{x}'$ in the frame O′ in the duration $dt'$ (the measurements in the frame O′), the velocity of the body in the frame O′ is $\mechanical{u}'=d\mechanical{x}'/dt'$. The O′ frame is moving in the x-direction at the velocity $\mechanical{v}$ relative to the O frame. Then, in the O frame, the body has the velocity,

$$
\mechanical{u}\equiv\frac{d\mechanical{x}}{dt}
=\frac{\gamma(d\mechanical{x}'+\mechanical{v}dt)}{\gamma(dt'+\mechanical{v}\,d\mechanical{x}'/\mechanical{c}^2)}
=\frac{\mechanical{u}'+\mechanical{v}}{1+\mechanical{v}\mechanical{u}'/\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{4.15}
$$

I have retained the ‘prime’ on the velocity $\mechanical{u}'$ to emphasise that it is the velocity inferred relative to the O′ frame, whereas $\mechanical{v}$ is the velocity of the O′ frame as inferred from the O frame. Of course, this is consistent with the light hypothesis: if $\mechanical{u}=\mechanical{c}$ in O, then $\mechanical{u}'=\mechanical{c}(1+\mechanical{v}\mechanical{u}'/\mechanical{c}^2)-\mechanical{v}$, which gives $\mechanical{u}'=\mechanical{c}$ in O′.

Einstein went on to derive the expressions for the aberration of light, Fizeau drag, Doppler shift, etc. The general equation for the aberration (discussed in the previous chapter) was found to be

$$
\cos\mechanical{\phi}'=\frac{\cos\mechanical{\phi}-\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}}{1-\mechanical{v}\cos\mechanical{\phi}/\mechanical{c}}. \tag{4.16}
$$

This reduces to $\cos\mechanical{\phi}'=-\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$ when light arrives from the zenith, $\cos\mechanical{\phi}=\pi/2$.

As for the Fresnel drag, if $\mechanical{c}/\signal{n}$ is the velocity in a medium, and if the medium is moving at the velocity $\mechanical{v}$, then the resultant velocity is $\mechanical{c}'=\frac{\mechanical{c}/\signal{n}+\mechanical{v}}{1+\mechanical{v}\mechanical{c}/(\signal{n}\mechanical{c}^2)}\approx(\mechanical{c}/\signal{n}+\mechanical{v})(1-\mechanical{v}/\signal{n}\mechanical{c})\approx\mechanical{c}/\signal{n}+\mechanical{v}-\mechanical{v}/\signal{n}^2$; this matches Fresnel's theory, as verified by Fizeau ([[03.04 The Speed of Light in Moving Media - Fresnel Drag|Section 3.4]]).

> [!note] Source velocity and angle notation
> Equation (4.15) prints $dt$ rather than $dt'$ in the numerator of its middle fraction. The sentence after Eq. (4.16) gives $\cos\mechanical{\phi}=\pi/2$ for zenith arrival, rather than $\mechanical{\phi}=\pi/2$. Both source forms are retained.

## Interpretation and checks

Apply simultaneity in the measuring frame for length contraction and fixed comoving position for time dilation. Interchanging those event pairs produces misleading reciprocal claims. Velocity addition follows by dividing transformed differentials using the same time differential in numerator and denominator. Check that a light ray remains at light speed and that zero relative frame velocity returns the original velocity. The printed transverse inverse coordinates contain missing prime marks and should be read with that distinction in mind.
