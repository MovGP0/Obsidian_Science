> [!TLDR]
> Lodge investigated possible ether dragging, while Michelson proposed a rotating-Earth loop experiment that avoided the one-way clock-synchronisation problem.

## Physical question and model

What did early rotating-interferometer proposals seek to distinguish? The measurable quantity was a phase or return-time difference associated with rotation. Ether dragging and spacetime-coordinate descriptions were competing interpretations of that readout.

## Lecture account

The Michelson–Morley null result suggested that neither first-order effects in $\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$ nor second-order propagation effects could reveal Earth's ether motion. Complete entrainment of ether by Earth could not explain this, because Fizeau had found only partial dragging by moving refractive media, with factor $1-1/\signal{n}^2$.

In 1893, Oliver Lodge examined aberration and light or ether dragging near moving bodies [15]. The source describes FitzGerald's proposed longitudinal contraction factor $\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}$ as the only consistent account then available. Lodge considered whether ether had viscosity and could be strongly dragged by rapidly moving bodies. His interferometric experiments sent counterpropagating beams around a closed path near a rotating object's surface. They found no viscous dragging. He also speculated about a gravitational analogue detectable only near very massive rotating bodies. The source compares this qualitatively with the later Lense–Thirring effect [16].

In 1904, Michelson proposed another test of Earth's motion relative to a stationary ether [17]. The author highlights its recognition of the connection between light propagation and clock synchronisation. Michelson began with W. Wien's proposal for a one-way ether test, writing:

> The flaw in the proposed method—as was pointed out by Simon Newcomb as long ago as 1880—lies in the fact that the effect which it is proposed to measure is exactly the same as the effect on the light which is to furnish the test of synchronism.

The source compares this with the later Ruderfer problem in [[11.01.01 Past Experiments on the One-Way Propagation of Light]]. Michelson then proposed:

> Suppose it were possible to transmit two pencils of light in opposite directions around the earth parallel to the equator, returning the pencils to the starting point. If the rotation of the earth does not entrain the ether, it is clear that one of the two pencils will be accelerated and the other retarded (relatively to the observing apparatus) by a quantity proportional to the velocity of the earth's surface, and to the length of the parallel of latitude at the place; so that a measurement of the difference of time required for the two pencils to traverse the circuit would furnish a quantitative test of the entrainment.
>
> But it is not necessary that the path should encircle the globe, for there would still be a difference in time for any position of the circuit. This difference is given by the formula $T=(2/\mechanical{V}^2)\oint\mechanical{v}\cos\theta\,d\mechanical{s}$, where $\mechanical{V}$ is the velocity of light, $\mechanical{v}$ the velocity of the earth's surface at the element of path $d\mechanical{s}$, and $\theta$ the angle between $\mechanical{v}$ and $d\mechanical{s}$.

Michelson proposed a total interferometer path of $2\,\unit{km}$. The author compares this with gravitational-wave detector scales and describes the scheme as feasible but costly and difficult. It was realised about two decades later. Meanwhile, Georges Sagnac conceived a more feasible laboratory test in Paris.

## Interpretation and checks

Reconstruct the complete path geometry and any comparison loop before treating a historical coefficient as universal. Area, orientation, and the detector's motion all matter. A round-trip translation null and a rotation signal are compatible because the relevant velocity circulation differs. Historical disagreement supplies context, not an additional quantitative datum.
