title: "Cosmic Relativity and Clock Synchronisation"

> [!TLDR]
> The author contrasts one-way clock synchronisation based on invariant light speed with a Galilean propagation correction depending on receiver motion.

## Physical question and model

How does a navigation system assign transmit and receive times? Use a specified coordinate time, satellite and receiver worldlines, local clock corrections, and a propagation model. A receiver changes position during signal travel even in standard relativity.

## Lecture account

Clocks are practically synchronised by electromagnetic signals. Two-way synchronisation can follow a chosen convention. Some applications use only one-way transmission, notably satellite navigation such as GPS and Galileo. The author argues that these systems cite STR as their theoretical basis but require timing steps beyond it. This section introduces Cosmic Relativity's proposed difference for separated-clock one-way synchronisation.

In STR, relative light speed is invariant and independent of source and receiver motion. For emission at source time $t_s$ and reception at $t_r$, the source gives the emission-to-reception distance as

$$
\mechanical{D}_{sr}=\mechanical{c}(t_r-t_s),
$$

independent of the endpoints' instantaneous velocities.

For Cosmic Relativity, propagation is Galilean. The author treats light speed as independent of source motion but linearly dependent on observer motion. If receiver velocity is $\mechanical{\mathbf v}_r$, relative signal velocity is $\mechanical{\mathbf c}-\mechanical{\mathbf v}$. The distance is written

$$
\mechanical{D}_{sr}=|\mechanical{\mathbf c}-\mechanical{\mathbf v}|(t_r-t_s)=\mechanical{c}(t_r-t_s)\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{\mechanical{v}^2}{\mechanical{c}^2}-\frac{2\mechanical{\mathbf c}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf v}}{\mechanical{c}^2}}\right). \tag{11.14}
$$

For $\mechanical{v}\ll\mechanical{c}$, the source gives

$$
\mechanical{D}_{sr}=\mechanical{c}(t_r-t_s)\left(1+\frac{\mechanical{v}^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}-\frac{\mechanical{\mathbf c}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf v}}{\mechanical{c}^2}\right)\approx\mechanical{c}(t_r-t_s)-\frac{\mechanical{\mathbf D}_{sr}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf v}}{\mechanical{c}}. \tag{11.15}
$$

It describes the correction as essentially $\Delta t\mechanical{v}\cos\theta/\mechanical{c}$, first order in $\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$, where $\theta$ is the angle between propagation and receiver velocity. The author calls this a large correction to STR and states that GNSS/GPS supplies clear evidence for it.

> [!note]
> The source's $\Delta t\mechanical{v}\cos\theta/\mechanical{c}$ has time units. In the distance equation, the corresponding correction is $\Delta t\mechanical{v}\cos\theta$; dividing this distance by $\mechanical{c}$ gives the quoted timing correction.

## Interpretation and checks

An interception equation can contain receiver velocity without changing the locally measured light speed. Transforming to Earth-fixed rotating coordinates adds Sagnac-related terms. The statement that a one-way algorithm uses motion corrections therefore does not identify Galilean local propagation. Compare the complete standard treatment in [Ashby and Weiss's GPS analysis](https://www.nist.gov/publications/global-positioning-system-receivers-and-relativity), with the same coordinate definitions.
