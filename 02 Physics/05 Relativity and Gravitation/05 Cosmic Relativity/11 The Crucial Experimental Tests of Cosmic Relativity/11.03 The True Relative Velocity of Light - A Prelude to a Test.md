> [!TLDR]
> The proposed method brings emission and reception near one another by looping the light path, allowing one clock to time one-way propagation.

## Physical question and model

Does bending a one-way path until its endpoints nearly meet avoid all synchronisation issues? It changes the experiment into a nearly closed-path timing or phase comparison. The remaining endpoint separation and the motion of every path segment must be included.

## Lecture account

To avoid separated clocks, bring emission point A and reception point B close together while preserving one-way propagation and their common inertial motion. The source proposes looping the path until B approaches A along a straight section of the loop. A single clock can then replace the two endpoint clocks, analogous to comparing runners' speeds around a stadium. Figure 11.2 illustrates the scheme.

![[cosmic-relativity-11-02-looped-one-way-path.svg|**Fig. 11.2** Proposed determination of one-way relative light velocity, recreated schematically. 1: A and B move inertially together at velocity v while light covers path length L. Separated clocks cannot be synchronised independently of the assumed theory; the apparatus moves by v Δt during propagation. 2: looping the one-way path brings the endpoint clocks close together while A and B continue to move inertially. 3: at reception after Δt, the complete apparatus has translated by v Δt.]]

For a straight path, A and B are separated by propagation distance $\mechanical{L}$. They move together at $\mechanical{v}$ while light travels between them. A chases the light and B moves away from the approaching light. Clocks moving with the endpoints measure the magnitude of the relative one-way velocity.

The author argues that the loop makes exactly the same measurement. Its propagation distance remains $\mechanical{L}$, but the endpoints can have nearly zero spatial separation. He describes A as still chasing light and B as moving away from it, just as in the straight case. A clock moving with the nearby endpoints measures the relative one-way propagation duration.

Before presenting the decisive light experiment, he proposes to validate these claims through calculation and an auxiliary experiment using sound, whose propagation is unambiguously Galilean.

## Interpretation and checks

For uniform translation with a constant velocity vector, the first-order directional correction around a closed rigid vacuum path is proportional to the line integral of that velocity around the loop, which vanishes. Rotation can give a nonzero circulation instead. A small endpoint gap leaves a correspondingly small open-path contribution. Thus co-locating the clock does not preserve the large open-path first-order effect without a further physical mechanism.
