> [!TLDR]
> The source distinguishes source-independent light speed from observer-independent speed and explains the clock-synchronisation obstacle to a direct one-way test.

## Physical question and model

Which timescale must a light-propagation experiment resolve? The baseline travel time and the motion-dependent difference are different quantities. Estimate the latter using the actual path, speed, and hypothesised order in the speed ratio.

## Lecture account

Light has supplied central ideas and formal results throughout modern physics, including relativity and quantum mechanics. Despite extensive study, the author regards its propagation as a major unresolved question.

He attributes the common belief in experimentally established invariant relative light speed to a misinterpretation of Michelson–Morley. That experiment compared the durations of two-way light propagation in equal perpendicular arms; see Chapter 3. The author treats its null result as a test of the principle of relativity rather than of one-way light-speed invariance. It can be interpreted as invariant two-way relative light speed in an inertial frame, from which one may postulate invariant one-way speed. A direct one-way experiment is needed to test that further assumption.

The source argues that the opposite postulate, Galilean one-way relative velocity $\mechanical{c}\pm\mechanical{v}$ for an observer moving at $\mechanical{v}$, is also consistent with physical situations. It therefore calls for an appropriate experiment, rather than an empirical-independent logical argument, to decide between the two proposals.

Source independence must also be distinguished from observer independence. Experiments establish that light speed does not depend on the emitting source's velocity. This is not STR's distinctive light hypothesis: source independence also holds for sound and other waves. Sound speed depends on the medium, while its relative velocity depends linearly on receiver velocity. The distinctive STR statement is that light's relative velocity does not depend on observer velocity. The author argues that this requires replacing a universal medium or absolute frame with empty space isotropic in every moving or stationary frame.

Relative velocity is propagation distance divided by propagation duration in a chosen inertial frame. To test first-order variation in $\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$, much smaller second-order effects in $\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2$ can be neglected. Length contraction is therefore not the primary issue. The experimental distance can be fixed, and its exact value is not essential if only variation with frame velocity is measured. The conceptual difficulty lies in measuring propagation time [1].

At one location, a duration is found from two readings of the same stable clock. Over distance $\Delta\mechanical{x}=\mechanical{x}_2-\mechanical{x}_1$, two identical clocks at the endpoints measure emission time $t_1$ and reception time $t_2$, giving $\Delta t=t_2-t_1$. Their rates can be matched accurately when they are at relative rest. But how can one establish that they show the same time? This is the synchronisation problem.

Light takes about $3.3\,\unit{ns}$ to travel $1\,\unit{m}$:

$$
\delta t=\frac{\mechanical{L}}{\mechanical{c}}=\frac{1}{3\times10^8}\,\unit{s}.
$$

The Galilean travel time has the first-order expansion

$$
\frac{\mechanical{L}}{\mechanical{c}\pm\mechanical{v}}\approx\frac{\mechanical{L}}{\mechanical{c}}\mp\frac{\mechanical{L}\mechanical{v}}{\mechanical{c}^2}.
$$

For a frame speed of $300\,\unit{m/s}$, the change over a metre is only a few femtoseconds. The author argues that synchronising the clocks with light would make the test circular: the experiment is intended to determine the propagation law that the synchronisation procedure would already have to assume.

Another option is to synchronise both clocks at the same location, $\mechanical{x}_1$, then transport one to $\mechanical{x}_2$. The source argues that this also fails to ensure synchronisation because transport changes its rate [1]. Its velocity relative to the laboratory is known, so the associated dilation can be corrected. But under the principle of relativity, the laboratory itself may have an unknown inertial velocity $\mechanical{u}$.

For an absolute-frame theory and speeds $\mechanical{v},\mechanical{u}\ll\mechanical{c}$, the source gives

$$
\delta t=-T\frac{(\mechanical{\mathbf v}+\mechanical{\mathbf u})^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}=-T\left(\frac{\mechanical{v}^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}+\frac{\mechanical{u}^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}+\frac{\mechanical{\mathbf u}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf v}}{\mechanical{c}^2}\right). \tag{11.1}
$$

The first term is known from transport velocity. The second is common to both clocks and cancels in their comparison. The cross term is problematic because the laboratory's absolute inertial velocity is unknown. It leaves an unknown synchronisation gap if nature uses an absolute frame. The author concludes that this theory dependence prevents a decisive one-way propagation test using spatially separated emission and reception clocks.

Nevertheless, many earlier experiments claimed to establish invariant one-way light velocity. The author regards this as inconsistent with the stated synchronisation obstacle. The next section reviews those experiments and their interpretation.

## Interpretation and checks

A short detector response does not alone establish the same absolute timing accuracy. Cable delays, path drift, dispersion, trigger definitions, and clock calibration also enter. The leading directional correction reverses sign when the path reverses under the stated model. For clock-transport terms, compare the difference of both clocks' rates; do not subtract a reference rate twice.
