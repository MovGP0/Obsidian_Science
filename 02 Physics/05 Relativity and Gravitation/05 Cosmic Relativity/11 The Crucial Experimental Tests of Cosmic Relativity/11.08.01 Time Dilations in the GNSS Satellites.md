> [!TLDR]
> The source compares gravitational and motional satellite-clock shifts, then contrasts its STR and Cosmic Relativity expressions for receiver-dependent timing.

## Physical question and model

How do motion and gravitational height compete for a satellite clock? In a weak-field Earth-centred chart, a higher potential speeds a stationary ideal clock relative to a lower one, while its orbital motion decreases proper time per coordinate time. Include the ground clock's rotation too.

## Lecture account

The known gravitational shift of a satellite clock relative to a surface clock is

$$
\frac{\Delta T_g}{T}=\frac{T_s-T_0}{T_0}=\frac{\mechanical{\phi}_s-\mechanical{\phi}_0}{\mechanical{c}^2}=\frac{\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}}{\mechanical{c}^2\mechanical{R}_0}-\frac{\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}}{\mechanical{c}^2\mechanical{R}_s}=\frac{\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}}{\mechanical{c}^2\mechanical{R}_0}\left(1-\frac{\mechanical{R}_0}{\mechanical{R}_s}\right). \tag{11.19}
$$

The source gives about $0.55\,\unit{ns/s}$, with the satellite clock faster than the ground clock, and $\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}/(\mechanical{R}_0\mechanical{c}^2)\approx7.3\times10^{-10}$. Its approximate STR motional shift is

$$
\frac{\Delta T_v}{T}\approx-\frac{\mechanical{v}_s^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{11.20}
$$

This is about $-0.07\,\unit{ns/s}$, a motion-induced clock loss. Navigation positions are obtained from timing observations relative to multiple satellites. A timing error of $1\,\unit{ns}$ corresponds to about $0.3\,\unit{m}$ of receiver-position error. Updates typically occur every few seconds, so timing algorithms must continually correct the relativistic effects.

The author states that gravitational corrections use the expected GTR expression, including Earth's equatorial bulge through its quadrupole potential. He argues that motional corrections cannot use satellite–receiver relative speed alone: receiver velocity also enters explicitly. He regards this as unexpected in a receiver rest-frame STR account.

For a Keplerian orbit, satellite speed relative to Earth's centre is $\mechanical{v}_s=\sqrt{\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R}_s}$. For observer velocity $\mechanical{\mathbf v}_o$, the satellite's relative velocity is $\mechanical{\mathbf v}_r=\mechanical{\mathbf v}_s-\mechanical{\mathbf v}_o$. The source then gives its STR motional comparison as

$$
\frac{\delta T_{\mathrm{STR}}}{T}=-\frac{\mechanical{v}_r^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}=-\frac{\mechanical{v}_s^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}-\frac{\mechanical{v}_o^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}+\frac{\mechanical{\mathbf v}_s\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf v}_o}{\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{11.21}
$$

In contrast, Cosmic Relativity compares the independent absolute dilations of the satellite clock and the receiver's comoving clock:

$$
\frac{\delta T_{\mathrm{CR}}}{T}=-\frac{\mechanical{v}_s^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}-\frac{-\mechanical{v}_o^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{11.22}
$$

The author emphasises the difference from (11.21), particularly its scalar-product term. A sufficiently fast guided vehicle could accumulate a navigational error of a metre in $10\,\unit{s}$. He states that GNSS algorithms actually use (11.22), which he regards as an absolute-frame formula, rather than his STR expression. He argues that empirical use of this formula avoids errors and provides evidence against STR and for a unique absolute dynamical frame.

## Interpretation and checks

Subtract both worldline rates in that common chart. A formula based only on their instantaneous relative velocity is not the full standard result for separated clocks in a gravitational field. Check the zero-height, zero-relative-path limit and the sign of a higher stationary clock. The cosmic interpretation must be distinguished from this standard calculation, not from an incomplete relative-speed substitute.
