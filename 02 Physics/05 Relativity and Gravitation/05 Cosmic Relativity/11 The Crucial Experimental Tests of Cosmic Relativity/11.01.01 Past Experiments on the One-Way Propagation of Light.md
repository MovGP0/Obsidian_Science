> [!TLDR]
> The author reviews Mössbauer-based one-way light tests, beginning with Ruderfer's proposal to seek a rotation-dependent frequency shift.

## Physical question and model

What do previous one-way experiments actually compare? Resonance shifts, separated-clock signals, and rotating interferometers each depend on distinct assumptions about clocks and source motion. Identify the complete experimental observable before calling a measurement one-way.

## Lecture account

Before 1930, important one-way propagation experiments in rotating frames included those of Sagnac and A. A. Michelson. Later sections describe them. This section first examines post-1960 experiments intended to distinguish ether-based Lorentz relativity with Galilean propagation from Einstein's invariant relative light speed.

The first gamma-ray experiment was inspired directly by M. Ruderfer's 1960 proposal [2] to use the recently discovered Mössbauer effect for precision measurement. Ruderfer argued that previous two-way experiments could not decide between Galilean ether propagation and STR. Recoil-free gamma emission and absorption [3] might provide enough sensitivity for a one-way test between a fixed emitter and an absorber at fixed distance in a frame with possible absolute velocity $\mechanical{u}$.

The proposed emitter is at the centre of a rotating disc. The absorber is at the rim, moving instantaneously at $\mechanical{v}=\mechanical{R}\signal{\omega}$ perpendicular to their joining line. Rotation periodically changes the angle between the frame's fixed absolute velocity and the photon direction (Fig. 11.1).

Without rotation, the source gives travel time $\tau=\mechanical{R}/\mechanical{c}$. With rotation and Galilean propagation, it prints $\tau(t)=\mechanical{R}/\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{u}\cos\theta(t)$. The periodic travel-time variation changes phase $\signal{f}\tau$ and reception frequency:

$$
\frac{\delta\signal{f}}{\signal{f}}=-\frac{d\tau}{dt}\approx\frac{\signal{\omega}\mechanical{R}\mechanical{u}\sin\theta}{\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{11.2}
$$

> [!note]
> The printed time-dependent travel-time expression subtracts a velocity from a time. The intended denominator is consistent with $\tau(t)=\mechanical{R}/(\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{u}\cos\theta(t))$, which gives a first-order modulation of the stated form, subject to angle and sign conventions.

Ruderfer proposed using Mössbauer resonance to seek the shift with fractional sensitivity $10^{-15}$. The source states that agreement with (11.2) would rule out STR and support an absolute-frame theory, while a null result would validate Einstein's STR.

Champeney et al. performed an experiment close to Ruderfer's proposal in 1963 [4]. It reported a null result, corresponding to an upper limit near $3\,\unit{m/s}$ on an absolute-frame velocity effect.

The author states that this and related experiments [5] are widely treated as decisive evidence against an absolute frame and for invariant one-way light velocity. He challenges that interpretation. The experimental paper cited two Ruderfer papers: the original proposal and an erratum published a few months later [6]. According to the source, the erratum corrected a crucial error and showed that the proposed experiment could not distinguish STR from an absolute-frame theory. Both predict the observed null result.

He criticises the experimenters' interpretation of that citation and argues that the same mistake recurs when later null tests are claimed to prove invariant one-way propagation. The section next discusses a modern laser experiment with atomic clock standards.

What was Ruderfer's correction? The proposal considered propagation and its periodic phase change alone. But emission and absorption are themselves atomic or nuclear physical processes whose rates must also be considered.

Emission and absorption rates undergo relativistic time dilation, as already discussed around equation (5.6). The emitter and absorber share the frame velocity, so their common dilation cancels in their relative comparison. The second-order rim-speed term $\mechanical{v}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)$ can be neglected. The source gives a cross term $-\mechanical{u}\mechanical{v}(t)/\mechanical{c}^2=-\mechanical{u}\signal{\omega}\mechanical{R}\sin\theta$, which it says exactly cancels the positive propagation term. Both theories therefore give a null result. Ruderfer concluded that “the proposed experiment is not a crucial experiment for deciding between the two theories” [6].

> [!note]
> The second expression in the source's cross-term equality omits $\mechanical{c}^{-2}$. Including that denominator gives the dimensionless term needed to compare with the fractional frequency shift in (11.2).

C. Møller made a similar proposal in 1962 [7]. The author argues that Møller included dilation but treated propagation incorrectly, and did not subsequently publish an erratum. A later group including C. M. Will used laser light over $21\,\unit{km}$ of stable optical fibre [8], with Earth's rotation supplying the rotating frame. The author argues that this analysis omitted dilation entirely. Its null result, predicted by both accounts, was nevertheless interpreted as invariant relative light speed. He also disputes that paper's introductory claim that Michelson–Morley established light-speed constancy or isotropy in inertial frames.

In this modern test, two identical H-maser clock standards supplied stable $100\,\unit{MHz}$ signals to modulate a communication laser. Light passed through the fibre over a $21\,\unit{km}$ baseline. Received phase was monitored against the local maser. Constant relative light speed gives fixed delay $\signal{n}\mechanical{L}/\mechanical{c}$, with $\signal{n}$ the effective refractive index. The sought signal was variation about that delay, modulated by Earth's rotation at the local latitude, approximately $35^\circ$.

Observed propagation-phase variation was below $1.5^\circ$. This was interpreted as invariant one-way relative light velocity to $0.02\%$. The author compares this with the earlier Mössbauer claim and states that both neglected the relevant clock dilation [1]. He argues that reading and applying Ruderfer's erratum would have prevented the repeated interpretation.

## Interpretation and checks

A result can constrain a particular anisotropic clock or propagation model without being convention-free evidence for an absolute one-way speed. Inspect any cited erratum as well as the initial proposal. Check the zero-rotation and zero-separation limits and the sign of the expected modulation. A statement about frequency shift needs a dimensionless ratio with a clearly defined reference rate.
