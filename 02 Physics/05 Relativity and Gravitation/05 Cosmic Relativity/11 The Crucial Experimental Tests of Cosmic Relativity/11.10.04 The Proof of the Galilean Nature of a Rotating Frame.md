title: "The Proof of the Galilean Nature of a Rotating Frame"
---

> [!TLDR]
> The author constructs a rotating-coordinate metric from Galilean transformations to argue that a metric description alone does not establish invariant light speed.

## Physical question and model

Does a rotating metric prove Galilean physics? A flat-spacetime metric in rotating coordinates has cross terms and direction-dependent coordinate light speeds. Local orthonormal measurements still give the usual vacuum speed.

## Lecture account

The source argues that Langevin's metric treatment [21] weakened recognition of Sagnac's result. It disputes the belief that describing a system by a GTR-style metric necessarily makes it consistent with STR and the light hypothesis.

Consider one-way propagation over length $\mechanical{L}$ from A to B in a frame moving at $\mechanical{v}$ (Fig. 11.2). The source prints the stationary-frame formal spacetime element as

$$
d\mechanical{s}^2=d\mechanical{x}^2-\mechanical{c}\,dt^2.
$$

It explicitly assumes Galilean propagation: relative light speed $\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{v}$ from A to B, and $\mechanical{c}+\mechanical{v}$ in reverse. The transformations $\mechanical{x}'=\mechanical{x}\pm\mechanical{v}t$ and $t'=t$ give $d\mechanical{x}'/dt=d\mechanical{x}/dt\pm\mechanical{v}$. The source writes the moving-frame line element as

$$
d\mechanical{l}'^2=d\mechanical{x}^2-2\mechanical{v}\,d\mechanical{x}\,dt-\mechanical{c}^2\left(1-\frac{\mechanical{v}^2}{\mechanical{c}^2}\right)dt^2.
$$

> [!note]
> The source's first line element prints $\mechanical{c}\,dt^2$ rather than the dimensionally required $\mechanical{c}^2dt^2$. It then changes the interval notation from $d\mechanical{s}^2$ to $d\mechanical{l}'^2$. These printed forms are retained.

The two propagation durations are $\mechanical{L}/(\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{v})$ and $\mechanical{L}/(\mechanical{c}+\mechanical{v})$, giving

$$
\delta T=\frac{\mechanical{L}}{\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{v}}-\frac{\mechanical{L}}{\mechanical{c}+\mechanical{v}}\approx\frac{2\mechanical{L}\mechanical{v}}{\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{11.27}
$$

This is first order in $\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$. A straight path cannot recombine both beams at an endpoint without reversing their paths, so the source next loops the apparatus.

Keep A and B and the complete path length $\mechanical{L}$ moving together. Bend the path around until B meets A, like the front of a train following a circular track. For a circle, $\mechanical{R}=\mechanical{L}/(2\pi)$ and angular velocity $\signal{\omega}=\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{R}=2\pi\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{L}$. The source calls this uniform rotation while claiming that bending the arrangement has not changed its physical propagation law.

The angular form of the same GT is $\mechanical{R}\phi'=\mechanical{R}\phi\mp\mechanical{R}\signal{\omega}t$ and $t'=t$. The relative light speed becomes $\mechanical{c}'=\mechanical{R}\,d\phi/dt=\mechanical{c}\mp\mechanical{R}\signal{\omega}=\mechanical{c}\mp\mechanical{v}$. The source writes the same Galilean line element as

$$
d\mechanical{s}'^2=\mechanical{R}^2d\phi^2-2\mechanical{R}^2\signal{\omega}\,d\phi\,dt-\mechanical{c}^2\left(1-\frac{\mechanical{R}^2\signal{\omega}^2}{\mechanical{c}^2}\right). \tag{11.28}
$$

> [!note]
> Equation (11.28) omits the final $dt^2$ factor. Without it, the last term has velocity-squared units rather than the length-squared units of the other terms. The printed equation is retained.

In the loop, two beams can enter at one location, traverse the same length in opposite directions, and leave together to form fringes. The source identifies this with Sagnac's method. It calls (11.28) the Langevin line element, and its implied metric the Langevin metric, also discussed in [[07.06 The GTR and Its Galilean Metrics]]. The author argues that its Galilean derivation explicitly permits relative light speeds $\mechanical{c}'=\mechanical{c}\pm\mechanical{v}$, despite its usual presentation as a GTR rotating-frame metric.

Langevin's 1921 paper interpreted the Sagnac result through gravitational potentials induced by transformation to a rotating frame [21]. The source argues that the frame's noninertial forces made that interpretation plausible, but disputes it because the author reports the same effect with an inertially moving observer and a loop assembled from straight sections (Fig. 11.5).

The author states that Langevin nevertheless accepted a common Galilean time and different directional light speeds. In a 1937 note [25], Langevin wrote:

> The presence of the ... term in $d\phi\,dt$ implies anisotropy in the light propagation, of which speed varies with the direction between $\mechanical{c}-\signal{\omega}\mechanical{r}$ and $\mechanical{c}+\signal{\omega}\mechanical{r}$ with the first order of approximation in $\signal{\omega}$ ... In this system, the courses are equal to the first order, but of unequal length because of the unequal speeds of propagation.

The source concludes that Langevin accepted noninvariant relative light speed in this situation, and questions how he reconciled it with STR.

## Interpretation and checks

The source's displayed line elements omit a light-speed square in one place and a time differential square in another, so repair the dimensional reading before deriving delays. A Galilean-looking angular coordinate change is allowed as a chart transformation within relativity. Computing the nonzero closed-loop Sagnac delay in that chart is not a contradiction. Check the zero-rotation limit and the full metric contraction along each ray.
