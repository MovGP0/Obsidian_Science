> [!TLDR]
> The proposed experiment tests whether a fixed one-way light path has a travel duration that changes linearly with apparatus velocity.

## Physical question and model

What exactly is inferred from the reported timing or frequency plots? Separate detector readout, path calibration, platform motion, and the model used to convert the signal to a propagation-speed change. The recreated curves are explanatory approximations rather than original data tables.

## Lecture account

Light-speed measurement is difficult because the speed is enormous. Early measurements therefore required large, accurately surveyed distances. Femtosecond lasers permit pulse timing to a hundredth of a femtosecond. Two-way propagation can determine an accurate average speed, but absolute accuracy better than $10^{-6}$ remains difficult because path-length accuracy is limited.

The author argues that the nature of propagation can be tested without precise absolute distance metrology. The required measurement is whether duration $\Delta t$ over fixed separation $\mechanical{L}$ depends on the velocity $\mechanical{v}$ of the moving reference system. A linear dependence indicates Galilean propagation; independence from the common inertial velocity of emission point A and reception point B indicates invariant relative light speed.

Propagation must remain strictly one-way between A and B. To avoid the separated-clock synchronisation problem, the source loops the path and brings the endpoints close together while preserving their inertial motion.

Direct timing at different inertial velocities is possible, but the author also proposes avoiding a clock entirely. A loop supports two propagation directions. Relative wave speeds are $\mechanical{c}_w-\mechanical{v}$ and $\mechanical{c}_w+\mechanical{v}$, giving slightly different durations $t_-$ and $t_+$. One duration can serve as a reference for the other. Combining the received waves also permits interferometry.

The experiments used direct timing for sound and interferometry for light [12]. For a $3\,\unit{m}$ light path, propagation takes $T=10\,\unit{ns}$. The experiment seeks the change as the emission–reception frame velocity varies. Moving that frame faster than a few metres per second without degrading measurement is difficult. For Galilean propagation, the source gives

$$
T'-T=\delta T=\frac{T\mechanical{v}}{\mechanical{c}}.
$$

At $\mechanical{v}=1\,\unit{m/s}$, it prints $\delta T\approx3.3\times10^{-7}\,\unit{ns}$. Interfering light that travels in opposite directions doubles the signal. Optical fibre could amplify the signal by about a thousand, but would require assumptions about propagation through silica. To avoid that added theory, the author chooses free-space light propagation.

> [!note]
> For the stated $T=10\,\unit{ns}$ and $\mechanical{v}=1\,\unit{m/s}$, the displayed formula gives approximately $3.3\times10^{-8}\,\unit{ns}$, a factor of ten below the printed value. Both the formula and source value are retained.

To validate the method, the author first tests sound, whose relative speed is known to depend linearly on receiver velocity. A rectangular path is made from $50\,\unit{mm}$ diameter PVC pipes with smooth corners. Its total length is $\mechanical{L}=8.6\,\unit{m}$, and sound takes about $26\,\unit{ms}$ for one circuit.

A $50\,\unit{cm}$ slot admits a rectangular PCB with a buzzer source and microphone on opposite sides. The same board carries the required minimal electronics, including a microphone preamplifier. A signal generator drives the buzzer at $4$–$5\,\unit{kHz}$ in $1\,\unit{ms}$ pulses, repeated below $10\,\unit{Hz}$. The assembly can move manually in either direction at uniform speeds below $1\,\unit{m/s}$.

The microphone records multiple passages, but only the first return after a circuit is used. The oscilloscope directly measures propagation duration, with precision better than $5\,\unit{ms}$ after averaging, as printed in the source.

> [!note]
> The text prints $5\,\unit{ms}$ precision, whereas the plotted changes and uncertainty bars are on a microsecond scale. The source unit is retained; the screenshot alone does not establish the intended correction.

Figure 11.4 shows measurements over velocities from $-0.2$ to $+0.7\,\unit{m/s}$. The source identifies the linear dependence on receiver velocity as Galilean propagation. A similar, less precise measurement on a straight $2\,\unit{m}$ section gave the same dependence. Sound permits this comparison because emitter and receiver can be synchronised using a much faster electromagnetic signal. The author therefore regards the loop method as validated for distinguishing Galilean wave speed $\mechanical{c}_w\pm\mechanical{v}$ from invariant relative velocity.

Points, vertical uncertainty bars, and trend line are approximate screenshot readings, not the original dataset or a new fit. The vertical signal is shown in millivolts using the source's printed calibration. The author interprets its linear velocity dependence as Galilean relative light speed. The following source paragraph refers to panel “c”, although the supplied plot is labelled “b”.

Two measurement series were made at different times. The first used a $1\,\unit{mW}$ He–Ne laser, beam splitters, mirrors, and a variable-aperture photodiode detector on a movable translation stage. The second used a $780\,\unit{nm}$ diode laser with output above $30\,\unit{mW}$. Light was fibre-coupled to a collimator on the moving stage.

Cube beam splitters and mirrors formed a loop with total path length $3.1\,\unit{m}$. Oppositely directed beams recombined into interference fringes. The detector aperture was placed at a fringe midpoint for linear phase sensitivity. The stage moved in either direction below $15\,\unit{cm/s}$; faster motion introduced vibration noise. Initial alignment checked stable photodetector output at different positions across the full $20\,\unit{cm}$ travel.

The source identifies the measured linear dependence on the velocity of frame O′, carrying both emitter and receiver, as the signature of Galilean light propagation.

The author states that the light result has the same character as the sound result and concludes that one-way relative light speed is Galilean, $\mechanical{c}'=\mechanical{c}\pm\mechanical{v}$. He presents this as both support for Cosmic Relativity and a complete experimental refutation of STR.

> [!note]
> The source's stated conclusion is that sound validates the apparatus method and the light measurement then establishes Galilean propagation in an inertially moving frame. It treats this as refuting the light hypothesis and STR directly.

The source argues that Galilean propagation is expected once cosmic gravity is recognised. It interprets the experiments as establishing an absolute spatial and temporal frame for dynamics and light propagation. Identifying that frame with the gravitational Universe is then supported, in the author's account, by agreement between calculated cosmic potentials and observed effects such as motional time dilation.

## Interpretation and checks

Test reversal of the apparatus, a stationary control, path-length changes, and independent timing calibration. Distinguish a repeatable modulation from its proposed cosmic cause. A claimed first-order effect on a closed translating loop must explain why the opposite segment contributions do not cancel. Without raw time series and a systematic-error budget, the lecture figures cannot independently establish the exclusion of standard relativity.
