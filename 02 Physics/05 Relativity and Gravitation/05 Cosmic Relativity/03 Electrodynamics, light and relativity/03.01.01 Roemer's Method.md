title: "Roemer’s Method"

> [!TLDR]
> Changes in the observed timing of Io's eclipses reveal the extra time needed for light to cross changing Earth–Jupiter distances.

## Physical question and model

Why do Io's eclipse timings vary as Earth moves around its orbit? Model the eclipse as a repeating source event with an approximately known orbital period and a changing light-path distance. The observation is a change in reception time, not a direct clock reading at Jupiter.

## Lecture account

O. Roemer estimated the speed of light by an astronomical method, by timing the sightings of the eclipse of Jupiter's nearest moon ‘Io’, as the distances between the Earth and Jupiter changed due to their orbital motion [2]. The diagram (Fig. 3.1) explains the method. Io takes about $42.48\,\unit{h}$ (42 h and 29 min) to orbit Jupiter. When the Earth is between the Sun and Jupiter (opposition) and closest to Jupiter, the distance between the planets changes slowly. But, when the Earth is near a quadrature relative to Jupiter (Earth-Sun-Jupiter at nearly $90^{\circ}$), the distance is changing fast, nearly at the rate of the orbital speed of the Earth. (Jupiter's orbital speed is about 2.3 times lower). Also, either an immersion (extinction) or an emergence of the eclipse is visible and can be timed well in this configuration. Consider the location E1 when an immersion of an eclipse is timed. The next eclipse would happen 42.48 h later. However, during that time, the Earth moves in its orbit a distance of $\mechanical{v}\signal{T}$ towards Jupiter, where $\mechanical{v}$ is about $30\,\unit{km/s}$. Then the view of the next immersion, which is an event carried and brought to us by light, has to travel a lesser distance between Jupiter and the Earth. Hence, *the immersion seems to occur earlier* than at the expected delay of 42.48 h. The precision of the measurement can be improved by repeated measurements. Similarly, the duration between the emergences, visible from the other side of the Earth's orbit, will have a delay that is larger than 42.48 h. It is clear that the average maximum delay is $42.48\times3600\times30/\mechanical{c}\,\unit{s}$, where $\mechanical{c}$ is the velocity of light.

![[cosmic-relativity-03-01-roemer-eclipse-timing.svg|**Fig. 3.1** The idea behind Roemer's estimate of the speed of light. Eclipse light variations have period T − vT/c while Earth approaches Jupiter and T + vT/c while Earth recedes, where v is Earth's velocity component along the Earth–Jupiter line. The difference between the two observed periods in the source figure is $\signal{\Delta T}=2\mechanical{v}\signal{T}/\mechanical{c}$.

Roemer tracked the eclipses for several years (all eclipses were not visible, with some happening when Jupiter was not visible from a particular location). The duration between the successive eclipses varies by about $30\,\unit{s}$ on the average, which gives the speed of light as $152900\,\unit{s}\times30\,\unit{km/s}/30\,\unit{s}\approx1.5\times10^8\,\unit{m/s}$. Though the statistical errors are large and many systematic effects are to be taken into account, the method had the strength of solid logic. Besides, since the difference between the time delays for immersion and emergence is determined from the accumulated delay for many events of the eclipse (30–40), the method had the additional advantage of not needing precise timing of the eclipse at the level of seconds. The sophistication of clocks had only just started to improve, after the advances made by C. Huygens in the mathematical design and technology of pendulum clocks.

> [!note] Units in the source estimate
> The numerical delay expression uses 30 for Earth's speed in km/s. Thus the numerical value of $\mechanical{c}$ must also be entered in km/s in that expression.

## Interpretation and checks

If the observer is moving away, the path grows and received eclipses are delayed. The leading delay is the distance change divided by light speed. The sign reverses when the path shrinks. An orbital ephemeris and perturbations of Io's motion are needed for quantitative inference. Predict whether doubling the change in path length doubles the timing residual under this approximation: it does.
