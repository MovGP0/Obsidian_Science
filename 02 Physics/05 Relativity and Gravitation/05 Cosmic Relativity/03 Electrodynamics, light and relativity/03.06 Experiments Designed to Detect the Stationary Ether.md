## Physical question and model

## Lecture account

The Galilean principle of relativity, that a state of uniform motion cannot be detected and distinguished from a state of rest, was at the basis of dynamics. This is related to the undetectability of pure ‘space’. The question naturally arose whether the hypothetical ether could be detected by measuring the relative velocity of light while the laboratory on the Earth was moving through the ether, just as one could detect the presence of the medium of air by measuring the velocity of sound relative to an observer moving and chasing the sound waves. However, the enormous velocity of light makes this task practically very difficult. The solution employing an optical interferometer was invented by Michelson [3].

It is no exaggeration to say that this class of crucial experiments is represented in the history of physics by the Michelson-Morley (M-M) experiment, which is perhaps the most famous experiment of all times in physics. It is perhaps also the most misunderstood experiment in the history of physics. We will soon see how and why.

The experiments of the Michelson-Morley type do not attempt to measure the numerical value of the speed of light. Instead, they compare the velocity of light in different directions relative to the moving laboratory frame, to test whether there is any dependence on the velocity of the frame in the propagation of light. However, there is a lot of confusion about the interpretation of such experiments, especially for those who are not aware of the theory of relativity by Lorentz and Poincaré. The M-M experiment was designed to detect the motion of the Earth through the ether, assuming a Galilean dependence of the relative velocity of light in a frame moving through the ether. It did not, and could not, test whether the relative velocity of light was an invariant constant, because such a conclusion depends on the theory used to interpret the experiment, as we will see soon. The original idea of the experiment was due to J. C. Maxwell. In a Letter to Mr. D. P. Todd, of the Nautical Almanac Office, Washington, USA [13], Maxwell wrote,

> In the terrestrial methods of determining the velocity of light, the light comes back along the same path again, so that the velocity of the earth with respect to the ether would alter the time of the double passage by a quantity depending on the square of the ratio of the earth's velocity to that of light, and this is quite too small to be observed.

The “time of the double passage” is the small quantity to be measured. Michelson worked out this second order effect ($\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2$) in the paper on his first experiment in 1881 [14]. After obtaining the results of the experiment, employing a novel configuration of optical interferometry, Michelson wrote clearly on the conflict with the prevalent view,

> The interpretation of these results is that there is no displacement of the interference bands. The result of the hypothesis of a stationary ether is thus shown to be incorrect, and the necessary conclusion follows that the hypothesis is erroneous. This conclusion directly contradicts the explanation of the phenomenon of aberration which has been hitherto generally accepted, and which presupposes that the earth moves through the ether, the latter remaining at rest.

However, he felt that the experiment did not have sufficient precision, when he found that he had made an error (factor of 2) in the calculation of the effect. This prompted him to prepare another experiment in 1887, with Morley, clarifying its aim thus,

> On the undulatory theory, according to Fresnel, first, the ether is supposed to be at rest except in the interior of transparent media, in which secondly, it is supposed to move with a velocity less than the velocity of the medium in the ratio $\frac{\signal{n}^2-1}{\signal{n}^2}$, where $\signal{n}$ is the index of refraction. These two hypotheses give a complete and satisfactory explanation of aberration. The second hypothesis, notwithstanding its seeming improbability, must be considered as fully proved, first, by the celebrated experiment of Fizeau, and secondly, by the ample confirmation of our own work. The experimental trial of the first hypothesis forms the subject of the present paper.

The reason for repeating the 1881 experiment much more carefully was also explained,

> In deducing the formula for the quantity to be measured, the effect of the motion of the earth through the ether on the path of the ray at right angles to this motion was overlooked. The discussion of this oversight and of the entire experiment forms the subject of a very searching analysis by H. A. Lorentz, who finds that this effect can by no means be disregarded. In consequence, the quantity to be measured had in fact but one-half the value supposed, and as it was already beyond the limits of errors of experiment, the conclusion drawn from the result of the experiment might well be questioned; since, however, the main portion of the theory remains unquestioned, it was decided to repeat the experiment with such modifications as would assure a theoretical result much too large to be masked by experimental errors.

The interferometer invented by Michelson is a simple device consisting of a source of collimated light that is nearly monochromatic, a beam-splitter (half silvered mirror), two mirrors, and a light detector, arranged such that light propagates in two orthogonal directions after the beam splitter and returns to the beam splitter after reflection from the mirrors to recombine at the detector. It is sensitive to the differential changes in the two paths.

Since the M-M experiment is considered as the solid foundation and the empirical pillar for the Principle of Relativity, it is important that we examine it carefully to understand the exact implications of its null result. Here we will do the analysis assuming that there is an absolute frame and the relative velocity of light is Galilean, as it was assumed while Michelson conceived the experiment. This is to prove with emphasis that a theory of relativity with assumptions that go against the prevalent belief in physics, nevertheless predicts an exact null result in the M-M experiment, as it was indeed observed. We will first do the entire analysis from a reference frame that moves along with the apparatus (‘comoving’), exactly as in the experiment in which the experimenter and the apparatus were moving together with the Earth. Since the speed of light does not depend on the speed of the source, as it is the case for other Galilean waves like sound, we know that the light emitted from a source moves a distance $\mechanical{c}t$ in the duration $t$. In vacuum, this isotropic propagation defines a ‘light sphere’ that expands at the speed $\mechanical{c}$ (Fig. 3.7). When an apparatus consisting of a source and a detector separated by the distance $\mechanical{L}$ is at rest, light takes the duration $\mechanical{L}/\mechanical{c}$ to cover the distance $\mechanical{L}$. But, the relative velocity of light does depend on the velocity of the observer. If the apparatus and the observer are moving together (say, along the x-axis) at the speed $\mechanical{v}$, the relative speed of light is reduced, to $\mechanical{c}_{\rightarrow}=\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{v}$. Then the time taken to reach the mirror depends on the velocity of the observer as

$$
\signal{\Delta t}_-=\mechanical{L}_x/(\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{v})\simeq(\mechanical{L}_x/\mechanical{c})(1+\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c})=(\mechanical{L}_x/\mechanical{c})+\mechanical{L}_x\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}^2. \tag{3.17}
$$

The extra velocity-dependent delay $\mechanical{L}_x\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}^2$ could then be used to determine the velocity relative to the absolute frame, in principle. But the enormous speed of light makes it nearly impossible to find a practical possibility of measurement in the laboratory. The additional delay, due to the motion of the Earth in its orbit at $30\,\unit{km/s}$, is only $0.3\,\unit{ps}$, for $\mechanical{L}=1\,\unit{m}$. The genius of Michelson was in finding a solution that looks like a paradox: allow the signal to be much smaller by reflecting and bringing back the light, propagating the light along and against the velocity $\mechanical{v}$, and make it relatively easily measurable!

The extra delay that is linear in the velocity $\mechanical{v}$ cancels out in the two-way propagation, because the relative velocity of light is $\mechanical{c}_{\leftarrow}=\mechanical{c}+\mechanical{v}$ when light propagates back after reflection. The duration taken in the return trip is $\signal{\Delta t}_+=\mechanical{L}_x/(\mechanical{c}+\mechanical{v})$. The total duration for the two-way travel of light is

$$
\signal{\Delta t}_x=\frac{\mechanical{L}_x}{\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{v}}+\frac{\mechanical{L}_x}{\mechanical{c}+\mechanical{v}}=\frac{2\mechanical{L}_x}{\mechanical{c}(1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2)}. \tag{3.18}
$$

The dependence of the duration of propagation on the speed of the apparatus is now second order in the velocity of motion, $(\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2)$. Therefore, experiments with the two-way propagation of light probe only the *average velocity of propagation*, $\mechanical{c}_{\rightarrow}+\mechanical{c}_{\leftarrow}=\mechanical{c}$, when the sensitivity is limited to first order in the quantity $\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$.

> [!note] Source average-speed expression
> The source prints the sum $\mechanical{c}_{\rightarrow}+\mechanical{c}_{\leftarrow}=\mechanical{c}$ as an average. For its preceding definitions the sum is $2\mechanical{c}$; an arithmetic average would require division by two. The printed expression is retained.

With $\mechanical{v}=30\,\unit{km/s}$, the extra delay $(\mechanical{L}_x/\mechanical{c})(\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2)$ is only $0.03\,\unit{fs}$, for $\mechanical{L}_x=1\,\unit{m}$. However, Michelson had a definite reason to devise the two-way propagation. Light propagates at its enormous velocity as a wave of microscopic wavelength. Light travels a distance equal to a wavelength ($10^{-6}\,\unit{m}$) in a duration of about $3\,\unit{fs}$. Therefore, if the periodic nature of the wave of light, quantified in its wavelength, could be used as a ruler to indicate the duration of propagation, that would be a technique to measure extremely small changes $\mechanical{\delta L}$ in the arm-length, or tiny durations corresponding to $\mechanical{\delta L}/\mechanical{c}$. Michelson had already worked with optical interferometry, in which one optical wave is compared with another to detect changes in the phase of the wave equal to a fraction of the wavelength.

Michelson devised an interferometer with two orthogonal arms, $\mechanical{L}_x$ and $\mechanical{L}_y$ (Fig. 3.6). The light from the bright spectral line is collimated and split into two equal beams at a 50:50 beam-splitter. After reflection and the two-way propagation along the two arms, the light beams are combined again at the beam-splitter (now the coated glass piece is a beam-combiner). At the output, one can compare the two superposed waves, crest to crest, and measure any relative delay as a fraction of the wavelength. Of course, one cannot ‘see’ the waves; what is measured is only the intensity of the light. When the crests coincide, we have the maximum intensity and when the crests coincide with the troughs of the waves, half a wavelength away, the intensity of light becomes zero. Thus, from the periodic bright ‘fringe’ to the dark fringe of the interference pattern, one can easily discern a variation equivalent to tens of nanometres, which translates to a duration less than $0.1\,\unit{fs}$. What is measured in such an interferometer is the difference in the duration of propagation in the two arms, *if it is ensured that the lengths of the arms do not change*. The instrument cannot detect similar and equal changes in the two arms, in the arm length or in the duration. The relative velocity of light along the perpendicular arm (relative to the frame moving at the velocity $\mechanical{v}$) is $\mechanical{c}'=\sqrt{\mechanical{c}^2-\mechanical{v}^2}=\mechanical{c}\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}$, both ways. Then the total duration of propagation is $2\mechanical{L}_y/\mechanical{c}'$.

Michelson increased the effective path lengths of the light and the magnitude of the expected signal with multiple reflections. The length of the arms were chosen to be equal, $\mechanical{L}_x=\mechanical{L}_y=\mechanical{L}$, to suit the interferometric method with spectral sources. (This turned out to be important in the interpretations and also in the history of the development of the theories of relativity.) Michelson's apparatus could sense a shift of the fringes (from bright to dark) corresponding to 1/100 of the wavelength. (A gravitational wave detector has the same configuration as the M-M interferometer. With the technology and laser power implemented there, one can measure changes in the arm-length a billion times smaller.).

In the perpendicular arm, $\mechanical{L}=\sqrt{\mechanical{c}^2t^2-\mechanical{v}^2t^2}=\mechanical{c}t\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}$. The source gives the corresponding travel time as $t=\mechanical{L}/\mechanical{c}\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}$; its denominator grouping is not explicit. The recreated geometry uses an arbitrary scale.

The difference in the duration of propagation along the two arms is

$$
\signal{\Delta t}_x-\signal{\Delta t}_y
=\frac{2\mechanical{L}_x}{\mechanical{c}(1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2)}
-\frac{2\mechanical{L}_y}{\mechanical{c}\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}}
\simeq\frac{2\mechanical{L}}{\mechanical{c}}\frac{\mechanical{v}^2}{\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{3.19}
$$

This expression was derived in the frame of reference of the observer, comoving with the apparatus. The same expression follows if we calculate in the stationary absolute frame, in which the apparatus is moving at the velocity $\mechanical{v}$ (Fig. 3.6b). Then the velocity of light has the same magnitude in all directions. The light from the beam-splitter is reflected back from the mirror after the duration $\signal{\delta t}_a$, during which the apparatus moves by the distance $\mechanical{v}\signal{\delta t}_a$. Therefore, the equation to determine the duration is

$$
\signal{\delta t}_a=\frac{\mechanical{L}}{\mechanical{c}}+\frac{\mechanical{v}\signal{\delta t}_a}{\mechanical{c}}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad\signal{\delta t}_a=\frac{\mechanical{L}}{\mechanical{c}(1-\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c})}. \tag{3.20}
$$

Similarly, the duration for the return trip from the mirror to the beam-splitter is $\signal{\delta t}_a=\mechanical{L}/\mechanical{c}(1+\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c})$. As shown in Fig. 3.7, light takes the duration $\mechanical{L}/\mechanical{c}\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}$ to travel along the perpendicular arm $\mechanical{L}_y$. The total two-way propagation duration along the y-axis is

$$
\signal{\Delta t}_y=\frac{2\mechanical{L}_y}{\mechanical{c}\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}}. \tag{3.21}
$$

The apparatus was floated in a tank of mercury to enable rotating the heavy apparatus with ease. The observations were done by actively rotating the whole apparatus, once every 6 minutes. Also, the experiment was repeated at various phases of the Earth's orbit around the Sun. If the interferometer is rotated by 90 degrees, the $\mechanical{L}_x$ arm that was along the motion of the Earth becomes perpendicular to the velocity $\mechanical{v}$, and the $\mechanical{L}_y$ arm becomes parallel to the motion (this also happens automatically with the Earth's rotation, albeit very slowly). This changes the sign of the signal in Eq. (3.19). The amplitude of the periodic shift in the fringes with rotation is then $(4\mechanical{L}/\mechanical{c})(\mechanical{v}^2/2\mechanical{c}^2)$.

In 1887, Michelson and Morley announced the failure to detect any shift of the fringes and the motion of the Earth through the ether, in spite of the sufficient sensitivity of the experiment [15]. The conclusion was

> It appears, from all that precedes, reasonably certain that if there be any relative motion between the earth and the luminiferous ether, it must be small; quite small enough entirely to refute Fresnel's explanation of aberration.

*In effect, the M-M experiment concluded that the ether was as undetectable as the empty space*. In fact, that was exactly the sense in which the null results were interpreted. The Principle of Relativity was verified to be a universal principle encompassing all phenomena in mechanics and electrodynamics. But what was the exact physical mechanism by which it was implemented in nature? After all, if the ether existed and light propagated in the stationary ether, a signal should have been detected in the M-M experiment. What is the physical phenomenon that makes it impossible to detect such a signal and the Earth's inertial motion through an absolute ether?

A seemingly bizarre answer came from G. F. Fitzgerald first [16], which was also the solution suggested independently by H. A. Lorentz later [17]. They proposed that the length of the arm ‘$\mechanical{l}$’ along the direction of motion *contracted* by the amount $\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}$, whereas the length perpendicular to the direction of the motion remained intact. If the length is considered at an angle $\mechanical{\theta}$ to the velocity, then the projection along the motion $\mechanical{L}\cos\mechanical{\theta}$ is contracted. This results in a null signal in the M-M experiment—no variation of the interference fringes with the rotation of the apparatus would be seen. This was in some sense a rational solution that fits well the nature of the apparatus that was sensitive only to the differential changes in its arm-lengths. With the ‘length contraction’, the difference in the durations of propagation along the equal arms becomes,

$$
\signal{T}_\parallel-\signal{T}_\perp=\signal{\Delta t}_x-\signal{\Delta t}_y
=\frac{2\mechanical{L}\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}}{\mechanical{c}(1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2)}
-\frac{2\mechanical{L}}{\mechanical{c}\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}}=0. \tag{3.22}
$$

Though the propagation of light is indeed Galilean, with the relative velocity $\mechanical{c}\pm\mechanical{v}$ in the absolute frame (of the ether), the physical phenomenon of length contraction makes the distance to propagate slightly smaller, compensating exactly for the Galilean nature of propagation. Then, there is no velocity-dependent signal and the PoR is preserved exactly. *I emphasise that the relative velocity of light is indeed Galilean, once we accept that the length contraction is a physical effect*. Only then the delay in the propagation of light is compensated exactly by the length contraction. In other words, if the physically real length contraction happens in a world in which the relative velocity of light is an invariant constant, then the M-M type of an experiment necessarily will show a non-zero velocity-dependent signal! The null result needs both the Galilean propagation and the length contraction to match together and mask each other.

It is somewhat surprising that the generally conservative physics community did not outright reject the speculation of the length contraction. Lorentz, who was deep into the theory of electrodynamics, and the dynamics of the electron, tried to formally implement the PoR in Maxwell's equations of electrodynamics, retaining the stationary ether. This happened in several steps, with contributions by H. A. Lorentz, J. Larmor, and H. Poincaré, from 1894 to 1905 [18–21]. The null result of the M-M experiment implied the invariance of Maxwell's equations of electrodynamics in uniformly moving frames, similar to the invariance or equivalence of Newton's laws in mechanics for all uniformly (inertially) moving observers. In other words, no experiment in electrodynamics could be used to identify the velocity of inertial motion and distinguish the state of motion from the state of rest. Then one needs to replace the usual Galilean transformations with a new set of equations, to describe the transformation of spatial coordinates and time in a frame moving relative to a stationary frame. For motion in the x-direction, the Galilean transformation (GT) for the x-coordinate is $\mechanical{x}'=\mechanical{x}-\mechanical{v}t$. The other coordinates do not change, $\mechanical{y}'=\mechanical{y}$ and $\mechanical{z}'=\mechanical{z}$. Time is considered as absolute and universal in Galilean relativity; so the same time is valid in all frames, $t'=t$.

Since the goal was well defined, as finding the correct transformations of the spatial coordinates and time to implement the PoR through the invariance of the electrodynamical phenomena, H. A. Lorentz eventually arrived at the ‘Lorentz transformations’ (LT), so named by Poincaré, who made completing refinements to the mathematical structure. Even before incorporating the length contraction, Lorentz had suggested a modification of the time in a moving frame by creating a ‘local time’, to achieve the consistency of certain results, including the aberration of light, with the PoR. This was a term that is linear in the velocity. The Maxwell equations of electrodynamics remain invariant to the first order in $\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$, under inertial motion, if the term $\mechanical{v}\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{c}^2$ is subtracted from the time marked by a clock at the position $\mechanical{x}$. Then the clock in a moving frame O′, at the spatial coordinate $\mechanical{x}$ and at the time $t$ (in the stationary frame O) has the formal local time $t'=t-\mechanical{v}\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{c}^2$. The length contraction brought the second order term $\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}\equiv1/\gamma$ to the transformation of the spatial coordinate. Full consistency required a similar term in the transformation of time as well. The final Lorentz transformations linking the space and time coordinates of a frame O′ moving relative to a stationary (in the ether) frame O, with velocity $\mechanical{v}$ along the x-axis are

$$
\mechanical{x}'=\gamma(\mechanical{x}-\mechanical{v}t) \tag{3.23}
$$

$$
\mechanical{y}'=\mechanical{y};\quad\mechanical{z}'=\mechanical{z} \tag{3.24}
$$

$$
t'=\gamma(t-\mechanical{v}\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{c}^2) \tag{3.25}
$$

These transformations defined the modern theory of relativity and the universal PoR. Both spatial and temporal intervals depend on the velocity of motion, with the characteristic second order dependence given by the ‘Lorentz factor’ $\gamma=1/\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}$. In the next chapter we will see that the same transformations follow from the hypothesis that the relative velocity of light is an invariant constant. One can already see the strangeness in the physical situation because here we discussed the LT *assuming explicitly that the relative velocity of light is not a constant* and that the relativistic invariance is the result of the real physical modifications of length and time during motion. How can two antipodal and contradictory physical situations consistently rely on the same coordinate transformations? We will see the answer to this instructive puzzle later.

The LT can also be motivated with a theoretical demand of the invariance of the wave solutions of Maxwell's equations. This way, the velocity of light is explicitly present in the equations, with its physical significance associated with all electromagnetic phenomena. This historical route, addressed by W. Voigt, Lorentz, Poincaré and Einstein, in different but related contexts, arrives at the Lorentz transformations (or a close variant, in the case of Voigt in 1887) by demanding that

$$
\frac{\partial^2\signal{\psi}}{\partial\mechanical{x}^2}-\frac{1}{\mechanical{c}^2}\frac{\partial^2\signal{\psi}}{\partial t^2}
=\frac{\partial^2\signal{\psi}}{\partial\mechanical{x}'^2}-\frac{1}{\mechanical{c}^2}\frac{\partial^2\signal{\psi}}{\partial t'^2} \tag{3.26}
$$

in two inertially related reference frames O and O′. We say ‘theoretical demand’ because whether or not such a demand is physically reasonable depends on the actual experimental result involving measurable quantities. In any case, this demand requires symmetric linear transformations on both space and time, to keep the same wave velocity $\mechanical{c}$ in these frames, and we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{x}'&=\gamma(\mechanical{x}-\mechanical{v}t)\\
t'&=\gamma(t-\mechanical{v}\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{c}^2)
\end{aligned} \tag{3.27}
$$

From the first expression for $\mechanical{x}'$ in the LT, the length of an object, $d\mechanical{x}'=\mechanical{x}'_2-\mechanical{x}'_1$, measured in the frame F′ (by an observer in F′), is transformed to $d\mechanical{x}=\mechanical{x}_2-\mechanical{x}_1=\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}\,d\mechanical{x}'$ as measured by an observer in the frame F, relative to which F′ is moving. Since $\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}$ is less than 1, all lengths contract in motion. This is what is needed to explain the null result of the M-M experiment.

The Lorentz transformation for time is more subtle. The transformation of time indicates that clocks at different physical positions in the frame O′ have different readings. This is the local time proposed by Lorentz, and interpreted by him as a position-dependent mathematical adjustment of the clocks to achieve the formal invariance. If a clock is carried at $\mechanical{x}'=0$ in the moving frame O′, it is at different locations $\mechanical{x}_1$ and $\mechanical{x}_2$ relative to another frame O at the two instants $t_1$ and $t_2$, due to motion. From the expression for the LT for time, we get for a small duration $dt'=t'_2-t'_1$, during which the clock moves by $d\mechanical{x}=\mechanical{v}dt$,

$$
dt'=\gamma(dt-\mechanical{v}\,d\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{c}^2)
=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}}dt(1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2)
=dt\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{3.28}
$$

The duration in the moving clock is shorter compared to the duration measured in a clock that is stationary. Time progresses slower in the moving clock. This is the relativistic time dilation. The importance and implications of the time dilation contained in the LT were realised only after Einstein published his Special Theory of Relativity, in which clocks and time assumed a primary role.

Maxwell's equations of electrodynamics are invariant under the LT—they have the same form in all inertial frames. The ‘Lorentz invariance’ of the full set of Maxwell's equations of electrodynamics ensured by the Lorentz transformations succeeded in comprehensively dealing with the null results of the whole class of experiments in electrodynamics, without exception. For Newton's law $\mechanical{F}=\mechanical{m}\,d^2\mechanical{x}/dt^2$, the application of the LT to the acceleration gives an expression that is not the same as $\mechanical{m}\,d^2\mechanical{x}/dt^2$; there is a velocity-dependent coefficient. Hence, Newton's law is not invariant under the LT. What really happens in this case is that the expression for the force also contains a similar velocity-dependent term, making the equation ‘covariant’, preserving the same simple form. Hence, the LT provided for the universal covariance of physical laws known till then. The essential point is that the PoR was confirmed as a universally valid principle—uniform inertial motion cannot be detected by any physical means because what is locally measurable does not depend on the velocity of uniform motion.

## Interpretation and checks

For equal rigid arms in the simple stationary-ether model, the unrotated parallel–transverse delay starts at order arm length times speed squared divided by light speed cubed. The change on a quarter turn is twice that difference; confusing these gives a factor-of-two error. A null result rejects that combined propagation-and-length model within sensitivity. Lorentz contraction and clock conventions must be included consistently when comparing another model.
