> [!TLDR]
> Stellar aberration is the yearly change in an observed stellar direction associated with Earth's orbital motion and finite light speed.

## Physical question and model

Why does a moving telescope see a star at a displaced angle? Aberration compares the photon direction with the detector's motion. Assume a distant source and a specified inertial frame over the observation interval; distinguish this velocity-dependent effect from position-dependent parallax.

## Lecture account

One of the important observational signatures for a heliocentric solar system was the ‘parallax’ of a nearby star in the background of very distant stars, as the Earth moves from one extreme of its orbit to the other. But, this program did not manage to get a conclusive proof. However, J. Picard reported in 1680 that the apparent angular position of the distant star (Polaris in this case) as observed through a telescope changed as much as 40 arcseconds during the year. The maximum deviation was not at the extremes of the orbit relative to the star (as parallax would have been), but at the midpoints of the extremes. No explanation could be found. The puzzle was solved in 1729 by J. Bradley, who observed the same angular aberration of $40\,\unit{arcsec}$ for more stars, over a year [7].

> [!note] Source caption notation
> The caption uses α both for an angle near 90° and for the small aberration angle. The subsequent general formula uses $\mechanical{\theta}$ for the true stellar direction.

Bradley's explanation involved the simple Galilean picture of the observation of corpuscular light arriving at its enormous speed $\mechanical{c}$, by an observer (telescope) moving with the Earth at its orbital velocity $\mechanical{v}$. For the simple case of light arriving from the zenith, the telescope axis moves through the distance $\mechanical{v}t$ by the time $t=\mechanical{L}/\mechanical{c}$ light takes to travel the length of the tube $\mechanical{L}$. Therefore, the tube needs to be tilted to compensate for the angle $\mechanical{\alpha}=\mechanical{v}t/\mechanical{L}=\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$ (Fig. 3.3). With the values of $\mechanical{v}=30\,\unit{km/s}$ and $\mechanical{c}=300000\,\unit{km/s}$, we see that the angle is $\mechanical{\alpha}=10^{-4}\,\unit{rad}$, which is nearly 20 arcseconds. Since the velocity in the orbit reverses in 6 months, the total aberration is $2\mechanical{\alpha}=2\times10^{-4}\simeq40\,\unit{arcsec}$.

In the more general case, for a star at the ‘true’ angular position $\mechanical{\theta}$, we get

$$
\tan\mechanical{\phi}=\frac{\sin\mechanical{\theta}}{\cos\mechanical{\theta}+\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}}. \tag{3.1}
$$

In 1729, a precise value for the speed of light was not known. In fact, Bradley estimated the most precise value then, from the measured aberration, by assuming the correctness of his theory of stellar aberration, $\mechanical{c}=\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{\alpha}\simeq295000\,\unit{km/s}$. Of course, the assumed correctness of Bradley's theory also implied the heliocentric view of the solar system, with a mobile Earth.

Nearly a century later, stellar aberration became a crucial test for the properties of the hypothetical ether. The questions that arose naturally in the context of the ether was how the ether permeated in transparent optical media like glass or water, and whether it was carried along with the movement of such bodies. Since the velocity of light in transparent media with the refractive index $\signal{n}$ is $\mechanical{c}'=\mechanical{c}/\signal{n}$, Bradley's aberration formula becomes

$$
\tan\mechanical{\phi}=\frac{\sin\mechanical{\theta}}{\cos\mechanical{\theta}+\signal{n}\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}}. \tag{3.2}
$$

For angles close to the zenith ($\mechanical{\theta}\simeq90^{\circ}$), the aberration angle should be $\tan(\mechanical{\theta}-\mechanical{\phi})\simeq\signal{n}\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$. The material dependent aberration angle would be the signature of a stationary ether, whereas if the ether was completely carried along with the material, there would be no aberration. It was not known in 1800 whether the speed of light increased or decreased when light propagates in a denser medium. Newton's corpuscular picture suggested an increase in the speed, to be consistent with Snell's law of refraction, $\sin\mechanical{i}/\sin\mechanical{r}=\signal{n}=\mechanical{c}'/\mechanical{c}$. The wave theory demanded less velocity in the denser medium. The test required the filling of a telescope tube with water, as F. Arago considered in 1810. Instead, Arago conducted some experiments on the refraction of light through prisms. It was concluded that the aberration angle remains invariant and independent of the medium through which light passes, from these indirect experiments. A real experiment with a water filled telescope was considered very difficult, and was done only by 1871, by Airy [8]. (Airy wrote about the reason to undertake the measurement: “Had the result of the observations been confined to the determination of an astronomical constant, or the variation of its value for different telescopes, I should not have thought it worthy of communication to the Royal Society. But it is really a result of great physical importance, not only affecting the computation of the velocity of light, but also influencing the whole treatment of the Undulatory Theory of Light.”).

These results suggested that *the aberration angle did not depend on the refractive index of material*. However, the theory needed to reconcile the invariance of the angle of aberration with the fact that the velocity of light in a transparent medium was lesser, $\mechanical{c}'=\mechanical{c}/\signal{n}$. Bradley's theory of aberration needed modification.

A. Fresnel had made the important theoretical discovery in 1815 that *the ether hypothesis required a partial drag of the ether* [9]; if $\mechanical{v}$ was the velocity of the moving transparent media, the ether drag was at the effective velocity $\mechanical{v}_d=\mechanical{v}(1-1/\signal{n}^2)$. This expression, which was verified in several experiments later, turned out to be very important in the development of theories of relativity. Einstein derived the same expression in his theory that did not have the ether medium, from the Lorentz transformations and a new law for the composition of velocities.

> [!note] Source speed ratio
> The source gives $\signal{n}=\mechanical{c}'/\mechanical{c}$ in the discussion of Newton's corpuscular model, while elsewhere it uses $\mechanical{c}'=\mechanical{c}/\signal{n}$ for the medium light speed. Both source expressions are retained.

## Interpretation and checks

At low speed the angular shift is proportional to the transverse observer speed divided by light speed. It vanishes for zero transverse motion. A water-filled telescope changes internal ray propagation and refraction as well as transit time, so one must trace the whole optical system. A formula that predicts an extra refractive-index factor from transit time alone is not a complete optical prediction. Compare the annual phase of aberration with that of parallax.
