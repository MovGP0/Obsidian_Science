> [!TLDR]
> The Hilbert–Einstein action yields the gravitational field equations through stationary variation, placing general relativity within the action formulation of dynamics.

## Physical question and model

Why use an action to obtain the gravitational equations? Specify the metric as the varied field, the matter action, and boundary conditions. The variational principle relates a common geometric description to both source dynamics and conservation laws.

## Lecture account

The ‘tense November’ of 1915 is discussed from several perspectives in physics history. Einstein reached the final mathematically correct field equation after four persistent steps that month, with presentations each week to the Prussian Academy of Sciences. D. Hilbert’s communications about applying Hamilton’s stationary-action principle to an appropriate action function also spurred the final effort [11]. This route to Einstein’s equation shows harmony with the rest of physics. Hamilton’s principle is universal, valid in the dynamics “of light as well as of planets”, as Hamilton stated [12]. It extends to physical fields, including gravity despite its geometric appearance.

The action principle obtains dynamical equations by requiring that the action $\mechanical{S}$ be extremal: its first-order variation $\delta\mechanical{S}$ vanishes under variations of its arguments. Applied to four-dimensional space-time, Einstein’s field equation follows from

$$
\mechanical{S}=\frac{\mechanical{c}^4}{16\pi\mechanical{G}}\int\mechanical{R}(\mechanical{g}_{ik})\sqrt{-\mechanical{g}}\,d^4\mechanical{x}+\int\energy{L}_m\sqrt{-\mechanical{g}}\,d^4\mechanical{x}, \tag{7.12}
$$

by taking variations with respect to the metric [13]. Here $\mechanical{g}$ is the determinant of the metric matrix $\mechanical{g}_{ik}$, and $\mechanical{R}=\mechanical{g}^{ij}\mechanical{R}_{ij}$ is the Ricci scalar, a function of the metric and its derivatives. The second term was already known as the matter-field Lagrangian; its variation gives the energy–momentum tensor. The Hilbert–Einstein action (7.12) is analogous to the relativistic action of a particle, $\mechanical{S}=-\mechanical{c}\int\mechanical{m}\,d\mechanical{\tau}$, remembering that curvature resembles the tidal term, proportional to $\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{r}^3$. Multiplying $\mechanical{R}/\mechanical{G}$ by the four-volume element $d^3\mechanical{x}\,dt$ is analogous to $\mechanical{M}\,d\mechanical{\tau}$.

> [!note]
> The source writes the particle action as $\mechanical{S}=-\mechanical{c}\int\mechanical{m}\,d\mechanical{\tau}$. If $\mechanical{\tau}$ denotes proper time in seconds, the coefficient requires $\mechanical{c}^2$ for action units. The printed expression instead has action units if $d\mechanical{\tau}$ is a proper-length interval. The source’s expression is retained.

## Interpretation and checks

Varying the curvature action produces bulk and boundary terms; a well-posed fixed-boundary problem must handle both. Matching the coupling to the weak-field limit fixes its physical normalisation. Ask which assumptions are retained when a new cosmic term is added to the action. A background inserted by hand must also have a consistent variation or an explicitly stated external-source status.
