> [!TLDR]
> Gravity and electromagnetism share source charges and currents, while gravity’s universal coupling and self-interaction lead to different dynamics and nonlinearity.

## Physical question and model

When is an analogy between gravitation and electromagnetism useful? In a weak field and for slow source motion, metric perturbations can be arranged into electric-like and magnetic-like terms. The approximation and field normalisation must be stated.

## Lecture account

Relativistic gravity developed along two routes in 1908–1915: scalar-field theories pursued by M. Abraham, G. Nordström, G. Mie, and others; and Einstein’s route, based firmly on the equivalence principle and the rank-two tensor $\mechanical{g}_{\mu\nu}$ as the gravitational-field basis. Surprisingly, a route using the similarities between gravity and electrodynamics, with a rank-two tensor (then called a six-vector) related to first derivatives of potentials, was not pursued. Reasons may vary. For Einstein, the equivalence principle was decisive: gravity vanishes in a freely falling frame, so a theory could not rest on the force field involving only first metric derivatives.

Even then, Einstein recognised effects similar to electrodynamics. In 1912, he derived an increase of a body’s inertia by $\mechanical{G}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{M}/(\mechanical{R}\mechanical{c}^2)$ inside a spherical shell of mass $\mechanical{M}$ [22]. In the same paper, ‘Is there a gravitational effect which is analogous to electrodynamic induction?’, he showed that accelerating the shell at $\mechanical{a}$ induces an acceleration of the small mass *in the same direction*, $\mechanical{k}=3\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}\mechanical{a}/(2\mechanical{R}\mechanical{c}^2)$.

Although both are inverse-square, long-range interactions, electromagnetism and gravity differ significantly. Electromagnetism has two charge types, quantised in units of electron charge $\electric{e}$ (or $\electric{e}/3$). More importantly, acceleration in gravity is independent of the body’s mass, while acceleration in an electric field depends on charge-to-mass ratio. Thus, electromagnetism has no UFF or equivalence principle. This is striking if mass is viewed as gravitational charge or source.

Electromagnetism is also linear: the electromagnetic field has no electric charge and generates no further field; the photon is electrically neutral. In contrast, mass–energy equivalence makes every energy a gravity source. Thus, gas pressure associated with random motion and kinetic energy becomes a gravity source. Gravitational-field energy also sources its own field, a ‘positive feedback’ making gravity nonlinear. *Gravity generates its own gravity.* This is related to the strong equivalence principle.

Despite these differences, *there are only two source types for the gravitational field, exactly as for the electromagnetic field: charges and their currents*. The remaining complexity comes from nonlinearity, and simplicity from the WEP. At their foundations, the interactions have more similarities than differences. This similarity is often missed because gravity is formulated through matter-induced changes in space-time intervals, relying entirely on equivalence of mass and inertia.

We will see these similarities and differences when reviewing results of the cosmic gravitational paradigm. As mentioned in Chapter 1, Newton’s relativistic law of motion is analogous to Lenz’s induction law ($\mechanical{F}\propto-d\magnetic{A}/dt$), and the Coriolis force is analogous to the familiar $\mechanical{v}\times\magnetic{B}$ Lorentz force. The same physical mechanism and similar sources underlie these effects. Inertial effects without identifiable interaction sources in the GTR will be accounted for in Cosmic Relativity as gravitational effects of cosmic matter in relative motion.

## Interpretation and checks

Gravity couples to stress–energy, not only to a scalar charge current. Tensor constraints and nonlinear self-coupling therefore prevent a universal replacement of electric charge by mass in Maxwell's equations. Test the static weak-field limit first, then the leading current-dependent correction. Different conventions place factors of two or four in different fields; compare forces or precession rates, not unnormalised field names.
