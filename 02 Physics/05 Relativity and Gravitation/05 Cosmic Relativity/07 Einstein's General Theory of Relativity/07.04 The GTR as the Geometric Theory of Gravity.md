> [!TLDR]
> Tidal effects and geodesic deviation distinguish spacetime curvature from a coordinate effect of acceleration. Curvature combines metric derivatives in a way that is independent of the chosen coordinates.

## Physical question and model

What observation distinguishes curvature from an apparent gravitational force? Compare neighbouring free-fall paths or parallel-transport a vector around a small loop. A nonzero connection in a chosen chart is not itself an invariant field strength.

## Lecture account

We review standard aspects of Einstein’s equation, focusing on the geometric interpretation that connects space-time curvature to matter–energy distribution. The preceding section discussed the geodesic equation for a test particle in arbitrary coordinates, equivalent to a reference frame in arbitrary motion with a gravitational field. The quantities $\mechanical{\Gamma}^i_{jk}$ ([[07.02 General Theory of Relativity as a Theory of Gravity|Section 7.2]]) represent space-time components of the gravitational field and accelerations. They vanish in inertial motion in gravity-free space. For example, the familiar vertical (‘z’) field component is $\mechanical{g}=-\nabla\mechanical{\phi}=-\mechanical{c}^2\mechanical{\Gamma}^z_{tt}$. Yet free fall can make $\mechanical{\Gamma}^i_{jk}=0$. A nonuniform reference grid can also generate nonzero connection components, falsely describing inertial motion as accelerated motion in gravity. Thus, these quantities cannot uniquely and faithfully represent a true gravitational field.

The measurable difference between gravity and an accelerated frame was depicted in Fig. 6.2 (see [[06.04 Einstein's Equivalence Principle]]). Figure 7.1 exaggerates it. In a gravitational field, tidal forces make freely falling trajectories converge or diverge, unlike in a uniformly accelerated frame. Gravity therefore requires the deviations of two or more geodesics, rather than the behaviour of one. This ‘geodesic deviation’ is their relative acceleration. It is related to tidal forces, the space-time variations of gravitational fields, or second derivatives of gravitational potentials. These are interpreted as components of space-time curvature, because *geometric curvature is related to second derivatives of the metric*. Tidal fields occur both inside matter and in gravitational fields with no significant local matter, such as the space around Earth. Differences between these descriptions are not discussed here.

![[cosmic-relativity-07-01-tidal-fields.svg|**Fig. 7.1** A true gravitational field differs from an accelerated frame through tidal effects. (a1) Gravity points towards the source’s centre, bringing two freely falling bodies closer together. (a2) Because field strength varies inversely with squared distance, g = GM/r², bodies falling from unequal heights separate. (b) In an upward-accelerating frame, the apparent free fall of floating bodies is kinematic; their separation stays constant in every direction.]]

To summarise the correspondence between the physical gravitational field, conventionally understood, and a geometric theory, start with the constant Minkowski metric of empty flat space-time, $\eta_{ik}=\operatorname{diag}\{-1,1,1,1\}$, and generalise it to $\mechanical{g}_{ik}$, a function of all space-time coordinates. The symmetric ten-component metric represents all possible gravitational ‘potentials’. Of special importance are $\mechanical{g}_{00}$ and the three-component $\mechanical{g}_{0i}$. The former is related to the familiar potential $\mechanical{\phi}$ of static masses and determines clock rates and quantum phases. The latter is generated by moving masses (currents), resembles the electromagnetic vector potential $\magnetic{A}_i$, and determines many gravitomagnetic post-Newtonian phenomena. Its components are typically $\mechanical{v}\mechanical{\phi}/\mechanical{c}$, with $\mechanical{v}$ the moving matter’s velocity. Thus, $\mechanical{g}_{0i}$ involves a velocity transformation mixing time and space components. The remaining six components ($\mechanical{g}_{11},\mechanical{g}_{12}$, etc.) pertain to arbitrary spatial reference-grid transformations, like the length-factor $\mechanical{g}_{\theta\theta}=\mechanical{r}^2$ in spherical-polar coordinates or the direction cosines in rotated coordinates relative to flat Cartesian coordinates. They are not truly gravitational in a strict physical sense. This repeats the physical point that true gravitational terms are linked to mass–energy and its currents, as in electromagnetism. The main gravitational effects relate to these two source types and the metric representatives $\mechanical{g}_{00}$ and $\mechanical{g}_{0i}$ of their potentials.

Space-time derivatives of $\mechanical{g}_{ij}$ are generalised gravitational fields, including the Newtonian field $\mechanical{\mathbf g}=-\hat{\mathbf r}\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{r}^2$. In the mathematical theory, these appear as Christoffel symbols $\mechanical{\Gamma}^i_{jk}$, with an extra index for the coordinate derivative. However, free fall can make them disappear, so they do not uniquely represent the true field. Electrodynamics has no equivalent principle. If all particles had identical charge-to-mass ratios, it would have a similar situation. Second metric derivatives describe differences in particles’ gravitational accelerations, or tidal relative accelerations. Clock-rate differences at different space-time points also matter. All these effects are components of ‘curvature’, represented generally by the Riemann tensor $\mechanical{R}_{ijkl}$. Space-time symmetries and physical constraints reduce the geometric object to the symmetric ten-component Ricci tensor $\mechanical{R}_{ik}$ in Einstein’s equations:

$$
\mechanical{G}_{ik}=\mechanical{R}_{ik}-\frac12\mechanical{g}_{ik}\mechanical{R}=\mechanical{\kappa}\energy{T}_{ik}. \tag{7.13}
$$

Here $\mechanical{G}_{ik}$ is the Einstein tensor. In Einstein’s GTR papers, he used this symbol for the Ricci tensor. The Ricci tensor represents tidal effects in matter-containing regions. In empty space, $\energy{T}_{ik}=0$, so Einstein’s equation reduces to its vacuum form, $\mechanical{R}_{ik}=0$. This equation gives the gravitational effects outside isolated masses such as the Sun or Earth, and outside compact objects such as neutron stars or black holes.

The other side of the theory is the geodesic equation for particle dynamics, equation (7.7) in [[07.02 General Theory of Relativity as a Theory of Gravity]]. It generalises a free particle’s inertial motion to arbitrary coordinate systems with accelerations and gravity. Curvature does not appear explicitly there, as expected for a single test particle.

The prescription is straightforward: find a metric consistent with the matter distribution and its symmetries such that *the metric solves Einstein’s field equation*. Find the appropriate metric-derivative combinations (curvature components) and substitute them in the equations. The matter distribution is represented by $\energy{T}_{ik}$. Once the metric solves the equation, relations between factual matter properties (density, pressure, etc.) and metric components are established for that situation. These relations contain all observable gravitational effects.

The same curvature tensor describes test-particle dynamics in a gravitational field without other interactions. In flat space, physical quantities such as momentum vectors can be compared directly at two points. In curved space, transporting a vector parallel to itself from A to B by different paths gives different final vectors (Fig. 7.2). Their difference is proportional to curvature. Parallel transport of $\mechanical{p}$ along the two sides $\alpha u$ and $\alpha v$ of an elementary parallelogram, defined by unit vectors $u,v$, gives final vectors $\mechanical{p}_1$ and $\mechanical{p}_2$. Their difference per unit area is proportional to curvature $\mechanical{R}(u,v)$:

$$
\lim_{\alpha\to0}\frac{\mechanical{p}_1-\mechanical{p}_2}{\alpha^2}=\mechanical{R}(u,v)\mechanical{p}. \tag{7.14}
$$

![[cosmic-relativity-07-02-parallel-transport.svg|**Fig. 7.2** Curvature and geodesic deviation. (a) The difference p₁ − p₂ after transporting a vector parallel to itself along two paths measures local curvature. (b) Comparing nearby trajectory velocities after parallel transport gives geodesic deviation due to relative acceleration.]]

In a chosen coordinate system, the components of $\mechanical{R}(u,v)\mechanical{p}$ are $\mechanical{R}^i_{jkl}u^jv^k\mechanical{p}^l$. This can quantify geodesic deviation, the relative deviation of two space-time trajectories. For particle dynamics, take vector $\mechanical{v}$ as the velocity of a freely falling particle and $\mechanical{u}$ as the separation vector to a nearby particle. If all particles start from relative rest, the second particle’s velocity $\mechanical{v}'$ is obtained by parallel transport of $\mechanical{v}$ along $\alpha\mechanical{u}$. After a short duration $\alpha$, the particles are at new positions $\mechanical{v}\alpha$ and $\mechanical{v}'\alpha$. Their velocities can be compared after parallel transport to the same location, yielding vectors $\mechanical{v}_1,\mechanical{v}_2$ at B. Their difference gives relative acceleration per unit separation:

$$
\lim_{\alpha\to0}\frac{\mechanical{v}_1-\mechanical{v}_2}{\alpha^2}=\lim_{\alpha\to0}\frac{\mechanical{a}_{\mathrm{rel}}}{\alpha}=\mechanical{R}(u,v)\mechanical{v}. \tag{7.15}
$$

> [!note]
> The source uses $\alpha$ first as a parallelogram side scale and then as a short duration. It also changes $u$ from a unit direction to a separation vector. Equation (7.15) retains this notation; a dimensional interpretation requires consistent parametrisation of the two directions.

Thus, abstract space-time curvature is connected to observable trajectories’ relative acceleration, which is related to matter’s energy and momentum densities through Einstein’s field equation. In a sense, we return to a description resembling Poisson’s equation. This reduces the usual exaggerated geometric aura of the equation’s left side. Written as ‘tidal deformation of an ensemble of particle trajectories ∝ energy–momentum tensor of matter’, the equation contains little geometry. It can be presented as a relation between the fractional volume change of a spherical particle cloud due to tidal fields and the sum of the energy–momentum tensor’s diagonal elements [14]:

$$
\frac{\ddot{\mechanical{V}}}{\mechanical{V}}=-\mechanical{\kappa}\sum\energy{T}_{ii}. \tag{7.16}
$$

This is physically reasonable because the only physical manifestation of that field-theory structure is the *dynamics of test particles* in unobservable fields occupying still more unobservable space (Section 2.1). The GTR is commonly studied as a geometric theory of space and time. Yet space and time are unobservable without matter. The theory pictures them as real entities extending smoothly from one infinity to another, with abilities to stretch, contract, and expand infinitely. In cosmology, space is even endowed with ‘inflation’ faster than light: nearby points initially separated by $d\mechanical{x}$ can become separated by $d\mechanical{X}$ in time $dt$, with $d\mechanical{X}/dt$ exceeding $\mechanical{c}$. It is conjectured that the Universe’s space expands with everything it ‘holds’, while nothing is really ‘moving’ during cosmic expansion.

We have seen Einstein’s view on geometrising a physical theory in his letter to L. Barnett (Section 2.4):

> I do not agree with the idea that the general theory of relativity is geometrising Physics or the gravitational field. The concepts of Physics have always been geometrical concepts... I am convinced, however, that the distinction between geometrical and other kinds of fields is not logically founded.

In a review of E. Meyerson’s *La déduction relativiste*, Einstein expressed his view of identifying the GTR as a geometric theory:

> This occurred only because it was usually forgotten that geometry is the study of the possible positions (and displacements) of rigid bodies. According to the general theory of relativity the metric tensor determines the behavior of the measuring rods and clocks as well as the motion of free bodies in the absence of electrical effects. The fact that the metric tensor is denoted as ‘geometrical’ is simply connected to the fact that this formal structure first appeared in the area of study denoted as ‘geometry’. However, this is by no means a justification for denoting as ‘geometry’ every area of study in which this formal structure plays a role, not even if for the sake of illustration one makes use of notions which one knows from geometry. Using a similar reasoning Maxwell and Hertz could have denoted the electromagnetic equations of the vacuum as ‘geometrical’ because the geometrical concept of a vector occurs in these equations.

The physical reality of elastic space is neither a problem nor an advantage in usual relativity applications, except in cosmology. Viewing cosmic expansion as expansion of space rather than receding galaxies is central to modern relativistic cosmology, particularly inflation. We postpone that discussion to the next chapter on *Our Universe*. We leave whether gravity is geometry and examine deeper conceptual aspects of the GTR.

Einstein began the GTR in 1907 and reached a nearly final form in 1915. During this difficult theoretical route, a significant, lengthy hesitation occurred, relevant to the theory’s physical content. The accepted equation is based on general coordinate invariance: preservation of its form under arbitrary coordinate transformations, including accelerations. Before reaching this, Einstein rejected a nearly complete formulation because he had a convincing argument against general coordinate invariance. He repeatedly argued that equations preserving their form under every coordinate transformation could not be physically correct. This much-debated ‘hole argument’ appears in his writings of 1913–1915.

## Interpretation and checks

A flat plane in polar coordinates has connection terms but zero curvature. Conversely, a freely falling observer can cancel connection terms at an event while curvature remains. The loop-induced vector change scales with enclosed area in the small-loop limit. Use that scaling as a check on the source schematic. For larger loops, path ordering and variation of curvature prevent replacing the whole result by one constant local tensor.
