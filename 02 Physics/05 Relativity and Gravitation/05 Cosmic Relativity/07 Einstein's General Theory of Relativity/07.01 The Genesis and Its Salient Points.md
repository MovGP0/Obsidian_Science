> [!TLDR]
> Einstein’s local equivalence of free fall and a gravity-free frame provided the starting point for general relativity.

## Physical question and model

What does the freely falling laboratory contribute to a gravity theory? It identifies a local frame in which nongravitational experiments approximately recover special relativity. This motivates a metric description but does not by itself determine its global field equations.

## Lecture account

The conceptual breakthrough that seeded the GTR was Einstein’s realisation that a gravitational field vanishes locally in a freely falling frame. We discussed this in the preceding chapter on equivalence principles. He called this revelation the “happiest thought” of his life [6].

> In an example worth considering, the gravitational field has a relative existence only in a manner similar to the electric field generated by magneto-electric induction [Faraday’s experiment]. Because for an observer in free-fall from the roof of a house there is during the fall—at least in his immediate vicinity—no gravitational field. Namely, if the observer lets go of any bodies, they remain relative to him, in a state of rest or uniform motion, independent of their special chemical or physical nature. (Of course, this consideration ignores the air resistance.) The observer, therefore, is justified in interpreting his state as being ‘at rest’.

This opened a route to apply the STR in any frame, inertial or noninertial. By transferring to a special ‘freely falling’ coordinate system, one reaches a ‘state equivalent to rest’, without the field and its forces inside the frame, where the STR applies. Einstein continued:

> ...then there is no objective reason for the observer to consider himself as falling in a gravitational field. To the contrary, he has every right to consider himself in a state of rest and his vicinity as free of fields as far as gravitation is concerned. The experimental fact that the acceleration in free-fall is independent of the material, therefore, is a powerful argument in favour of expanding the postulate of relativity to coordinate systems moving nonuniformly relative to each other.

The remaining task was to adapt the mathematical formulation to this insight. It took another eight years to obtain a mathematically and physically cogent theory, but the final theory is not complicated to understand or interpret.

The fundamental hypothesis remained unchanged, repeated in every formal exposition by Einstein during the theory’s formulation and after its completion. In the 1914 ‘Entwurf’ (Outline) paper with M. Grossmann on relativistic gravity and dynamics, Einstein wrote [7]:

> The theory expounded in what follows derives from the conviction that the proportionality between the inertial and the gravitational mass of bodies is an exactly valid law of nature that must already find expression in the very foundation of theoretical physics. I already sought to give expression to this conviction in several earlier papers by seeking to reduce the gravitational mass to the inertial mass; this endeavour led me to the hypothesis that, from a physical point of view, an (infinitesimally extended, homogeneous) gravitational field can be completely replaced by a state of acceleration of the reference system. This hypothesis can be expressed pictorially in the following way; An observer enclosed in a box can in no way decide whether the box is at rest in a gravitational field, or whether it is in accelerated motion, maintained by forces acting on the box, in space that is free of gravitational field (equivalence hypothesis).

This graphic description leaves no doubt that the theory’s underlying space and time are the empty space of the STR, remaining homogeneous and isotropic during arbitrary frame motion. I emphasise again that the true physical situation—such space is nonexistent even approximately because matter and its gravity are distributed everywhere in our Universe—was not expected to be incorporated into the foundations, since this fact was unknown when Einstein proposed the theory.

## Interpretation and checks

A theory also needs a source law and a Newtonian limit. Compare the roles of local equivalence, stress–energy conservation, and boundary conditions rather than treating covariance as a substitute for them. Check two separated free test bodies: their relative acceleration can reveal curvature that one accelerometer cannot detect. This is the bridge from a local thought experiment to geometry.
