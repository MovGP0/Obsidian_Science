## Lecture account

## Abstract

Einstein’s General Theory of Relativity (GTR) is also the modern theory of gravitation. It generalises a ‘flat’, empty four-dimensional space-time, in which intrinsic measures of space and time (the metric) remain invariant from point to point, to a general space-time that can be ‘curved’ by matter. I review the postulated conceptual foundations of the mature GTR, following Einstein’s convictions. The strengths and ambiguities of the theory are examined carefully, particularly Einstein’s difficult choice between Mach’s principle and general coordinate invariance. Finally, I discuss the GTR’s inherent incompleteness: it is a gravity theory applicable in this Universe, but does not incorporate the gravity of matter in the Universe. The remedy is a modification to complete Einstein’s theory.

## Introduction

Einstein completed his relativistic gravitation theory in November 1915, a decade after publishing the STR. His motivation was to modify Galilean-invariant Newtonian gravitation to make it consistent with the Principle of Relativity, expressed in the STR as Lorentz invariance. Two fundamental postulates made this feasible: (1) extension of the STR to accelerated frames through a set of instantaneous Lorentz frames whose instantaneous velocities coincide with the accelerated frame’s velocity; (2) the observed equivalence of inertial mass (inertia) and gravitational mass, with universal free fall as its consequence. Einstein transformed the latter into a principle of complete equivalence between a uniform gravitational field $\mechanical{g}$ and an accelerated gravity-free frame with acceleration $\mechanical{a}=-\mechanical{g}$. He could then construct gravitational dynamics from a theory in an accelerated frame.

The GTR is widely studied, with numerous excellent textbooks [1–5]. This chapter does not review its mathematical formulation, tests, or applications in detail. The essential mathematical features and physical interpretation of Einstein’s field equation are presented in [[07.02 General Theory of Relativity as a Theory of Gravity|Sections 7.2]]–[[07.04 The GTR as the Geometric Theory of Gravity|7.4]]. Specific applications found in textbooks are not discussed here. The reasons for this chapter are:

- To emphasise the inseparable basis of the GTR *in the STR and the Minkowski formulation* of four-dimensional pseudo-Euclidean flat space-time *without distributed matter*, generalised to a pseudo-Riemannian world with curvature and possible time evolution representing gravity.
- To explain the physical meaning of the terms in Einstein’s final equations and their relation to gravitational charge (mass) and its currents, as in electrodynamics.
- To discuss why Einstein finally left out the *Machian program*, a strong motivation for several years.
- To discuss why the GTR could not explain inertial forces beyond grouping gravity and conventional pseudo-forces into a general class arising in general coordinate transformations.
- To discuss *a significant modification as a necessary completion of the GTR that changes Einstein’s theory conceptually without affecting its tested results and predictions*.

Since only the general nature of the theory is discussed, with essential physical and mathematical aspects, I follow Einstein’s exposition as closely as possible. I stress that *manifestations of the gravitational field $\mechanical{g}_{\mu\nu}$ are position- and time-dependent modifications of physical intervals of distance and duration, as measured by scales and clocks made of real matter*.

## Interpretation and checks

This chapter mixes standard general-relativity results with the author's proposed revision. Standard cosmological solutions already include cosmic matter. Check whether a claimed missing term is a new coupling or the same stress–energy written separately. An invariant observable, such as a clock comparison or tidal acceleration, is a better test than the appearance of one metric component.
