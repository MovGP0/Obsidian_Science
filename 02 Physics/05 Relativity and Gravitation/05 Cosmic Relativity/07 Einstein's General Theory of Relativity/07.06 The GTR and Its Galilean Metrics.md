## Physical question and model

Do Galilean-looking coordinate formulas imply Galilean local light physics? A coordinate change can create mixed time–space metric components even in flat spacetime. Schwarzschild curvature, however, cannot be generated from a flat metric by a coordinate relabelling alone.

## Lecture account

The GTR’s *general covariance* is an automatic mathematical feature: tensor equations preserve their form under general coordinate transformations. Any physical relation between tensors is generally covariant. But invariance is not implied: quantitative phenomena, such as time dilation, vary under transformation. Thus, the ideal motivating generally covariant equations through equivalence of inertia and mass is not explicit. To illustrate, we examine important GTR problems through metrics obeying Einstein’s equation. One might expect these metrics to arise from Lorentz transformations for linear inertial motion and equivalent nonlinear transformations for acceleration. Instead, we will see metrics obtained purely from Galilean transformations: a rotating frame and the Schwarzschild metric outside a spherical mass.

P. Langevin derived the rotating-frame metric and used it to analyse the Sagnac effect [20]. It involves a simple Galilean transformation in metric form. From $(\mechanical{x},t)$ to $(\mechanical{x}',t')$,

$$
t'=t,\quad\mechanical{x}'=\mechanical{x}-\mechanical{v}t. \tag{7.20}
$$

The transformed metric is

$$
d\mechanical{s}^2=-\mechanical{c}^2dt^2+d\mechanical{x}^2\Rightarrow-\mechanical{c}^2dt^2(1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2)-2\mechanical{v}\,d\mechanical{x}\,dt+d\mechanical{x}^2. \tag{7.21}
$$

If the straight physical path is looped around, $\mechanical{x}=\mechanical{R}\mechanical{\phi}$, where $\mechanical{\phi}$ is angle from one endpoint (Fig. 7.4). This mathematical coordinate transformation preserves all kinematic coordinate relations. Velocity becomes $\mechanical{v}=\mechanical{R}\dot{\mechanical{\phi}}=\mechanical{R}\mechanical{\Omega}$, and $\mechanical{x}'=\mechanical{R}\mechanical{\phi}-\mechanical{R}\mechanical{\Omega}t$ is the Galilean transformation. Thus,

$$
d\mechanical{s}'^2=-\mechanical{c}^2(dt)^2(1-\mechanical{R}^2\mechanical{\Omega}^2/\mechanical{c}^2)-2\mechanical{R}^2\mechanical{\Omega}\,d\mechanical{\phi}\,dt+d\mechanical{R}^2+\mechanical{R}^2d\mechanical{\phi}^2. \tag{7.22}
$$

This is the same linear Galilean transformation looped through mathematical bending. It is the *Langevin metric, identical to the Galilean metric*. It is an exact Einstein-equation solution. Yet it is evidently not special or general relativity (the gravity theory). A transformation consistent with the STR would be the one L. Thomas found in 1926 for atomic electrons in orbit: successive Lorentz transformations in progressively changing directions equal a Lorentz transformation and pure rotation, rather than a Galilean transformation.

As another example, consider the Schwarzschild metric for a spherical mass at $\mechanical{r}=0$. Start with rest in an absolutely homogeneous space and transform to an accelerating frame freely falling towards the mass from infinity. Without a point mass at $\mechanical{r}=0$, the flat metric is $d\mechanical{s}^2\simeq-\mechanical{c}^2dt^2+d\mechanical{r}^2+\mechanical{r}^2(d\mechanical{\theta}^2+\sin^2\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\phi}^2)$, the same as $d\mechanical{s}^2=d\mechanical{x}^2+d\mechanical{y}^2+d\mechanical{z}^2-\mechanical{c}^2dt^2$. With mass $\mechanical{M}$ present, an observer at $\mechanical{r}$ falls towards it at radial velocity $\mechanical{\mathbf v}=-\hat{\mathbf r}(2\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{r})^{1/2}$. The Galilean transformation to a uniformly accelerated frame from the homogeneous isotropic metric is $t'=t$, $\mechanical{r}'=\mechanical{r}+\mechanical{v}t$, with $\mechanical{v}=(2\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{r})^{1/2}$. It gives

$$
d\mechanical{s}'^2=-\mechanical{c}^2dt^2\left(1-\frac{2\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}}{\mechanical{c}^2\mechanical{r}}\right)+d\mechanical{r}^2+2\left(\frac{2\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}}{\mechanical{r}}\right)^{1/2}d\mechanical{r}\,dt+\mechanical{r}^2(d\mechanical{\theta}^2+\sin^2\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\phi}^2). \tag{7.23}
$$

This is the Painlevé metric (line element) [21]. From this derivation, it is a Galilean metric satisfying the GTR equations. Less evidently, it is the Schwarzschild metric in nondiagonal form, without a divergence at $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{c}^2$. To obtain the familiar Schwarzschild coordinates, eliminate the mixed $d\mechanical{r}\,dt$ term with a new time coordinate,

$$
dT=dt-d\mechanical{r}\frac{(2\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{r})^{1/2}}{\mechanical{c}^2(1-2\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{c}^2\mechanical{r})}. \tag{7.24}
$$

Then, in the source’s split expansion,

$$
\begin{aligned}
d\mechanical{s}'^2={}&-\mechanical{c}^2dT^2(1-2\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{c}^2\mechanical{r})\\
&+\frac{d\mechanical{r}^2(2\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{c}^2\mechanical{r})}{1-2\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{c}^2\mechanical{r}}-2(2\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{r})^{1/2}d\mechanical{r}\,dt
\end{aligned} \tag{7.25}
$$

$$
{}+2(2\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{r})^{1/2}d\mechanical{r}\,dt+d\mechanical{r}^2+\mechanical{r}^2(d\mechanical{\theta}^2+\sin^2\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\phi}^2) \tag{7.26}
$$

$$
{}=-\mechanical{c}^2dT^2(1-2\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{c}^2\mechanical{r})+\frac{d\mechanical{r}^2}{1-2\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{c}^2\mechanical{r}}+\mechanical{r}^2(d\mechanical{\theta}^2+\sin^2\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\phi}^2). \tag{7.27}
$$

I have derived the standard Schwarzschild line element from the obvious Galilean velocity transformation with free-fall velocity $\mechanical{v}(t)=(2\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{r})^{1/2}$.

In these widely used laboratory and astrophysical examples, a Galilean transformation to a moving frame relative to an absolute frame gives the transformed metric satisfying Einstein’s equation. This fact, involving the Langevin metric, becomes crucial in Chapter 11 when we discuss the true relative velocity of light.

## Interpretation and checks

The rotating flat metric and the Painlevé–Gullstrand form of Schwarzschild spacetime are different examples. The latter is a chart on an already curved solution, not a Galilean boost that creates gravity. Compute a curvature invariant or a local orthonormal measurement to distinguish them. Coordinate light speeds can vary while the locally measured vacuum speed remains fixed. The rotating chart requires staying within the region where its coordinate observers remain timelike.
