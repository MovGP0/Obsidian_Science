> [!TLDR]
> The author interprets spin-dependent geometric phases as physical interactions with the cosmic gravitomagnetic field induced by rotation.

## Physical question and model

Which phase remains after a quantum state is transported around a closed cycle? Separate the dynamical phase from the geometric phase and specify the path in the space of state directions. For adiabatic spin transport, the state must remain in its selected instantaneous eigenstate.

## Lecture account

A physical quantity changes through an interaction, even when its cause is initially hidden. A heuristic description can help, but the source requires a physical interaction and an interaction Hamiltonian for a full account of evolution.

Quantum phase depends on mass, charge, and spin. Apart from free evolution, the author therefore requires an interaction to change it. Some phase contributions admit a geometric or topological description: examples include the Pancharatnam optical phase, the Aharonov–Bohm (A–B) phase, and Berry's phase [13, 14].

The A–B phase is attributed here to interaction between the magnetic-source current and the moving particle's current [15]. Although the particle can pass through a region with no magnetic field, a potential exists due to the real solenoid current. The source stresses that interaction energy is more fundamental than its spatial gradient or circulation; a gradient or curl requires the underlying physical quantity.

Many measured geometric phases involve particles with spin whose momentum or spin direction turns along a spatial path. Cosmic Relativity treats momentum and spin as currents of gravitational charge, and thus predicts interaction between spin and cosmic matter currents. The author claims that all observable spin phases during physical rotation and transport arise from the induced cosmic gravitomagnetic field.

With $\mechanical{\mathbf B}_g=-\nabla\times\mechanical{\mathbf v}\simeq-2\signal{\boldsymbol\Omega}$ and spin energy $\mechanical{\mathbf s}\cdot\signal{\boldsymbol\Omega}$, the printed closed-path phase is

$$
\Delta\signal{\varphi}=-\oint\mechanical{\mathbf s}\cdot\signal{\boldsymbol\Omega}\,dt
=-2\pi\mechanical{s}\cos\theta. \tag{13.18}
$$

Here $\theta$ is the angle between the spin component and the rotation axis. The source next considers light in a helical optical fibre and a neutron in a magnetic field whose direction changes cyclically and adiabatically. It attributes the measured geometric phases to spin coupling to the cosmic field induced by rotation in the matter-filled Universe.

> [!note]
> Equation (13.18) has no explicit division by ℏ. It is dimensionless only when the spin is expressed in units of ℏ, rather than as dimensional angular momentum.

## Light in a helical fibre

Following Berry's paper [16], A. Tomita and R. Chiao measured the rotation of linear polarisation after propagation through a helical fibre [17]. The fibre tangent defines photon momentum. The geometry returns its direction to the initial direction. In a spherical space of directions this traces a closed circle; the helix pitch sets its solid angle. The measured phase agreed with the geometric prediction [13].

A photon has spin $\energy{\hbar}$. Linear polarisation is transverse, $\hat e\cdot\hat k=0$, while helicity spin is parallel or antiparallel to propagation. The author assigns the circulating photon an induced cosmic field of magnitude $2\signal{\Omega}$, with $\signal{\Omega}=\mechanical{c}/\mechanical{r}$.

Linear polarisation is the superposition $|x\rangle=(|R\rangle+|L\rangle)/\sqrt2$ of right and left circular polarisation. Both are spin-1 photon states, with opposite helicities. The source assigns them opposite phases $\signal{\varphi}=\oint\mechanical{\mathbf s}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf B}_g\,dt=\mp2\pi\cos\theta$. It then gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
|x'\rangle&=\frac1{\sqrt2}\left(e^{-i\signal{\varphi}}|R\rangle+e^{i\signal{\varphi}}|L\rangle\right)\\
&=\frac{e^{-i\signal{\varphi}}}{2}(|x\rangle+i|y\rangle)
+\frac{e^{i\signal{\varphi}}}{2}(|x\rangle-i|y\rangle)\\
&=\cos\signal{\varphi}|x\rangle+\sin\signal{\varphi}|y\rangle.
\end{aligned}\tag{13.19}
$$

The polarisation rotates through $\signal{\varphi}=-2\pi\cos\theta$. Adding the fibre's mechanical winding of $2\pi$ gives $\signal{\zeta}=2\pi(1-\cos\theta)$. This is the measured solid-angle phase. The author regards the sphere construction as a representation and cosmic spin coupling as its physical cause.

## Neutron spin transport

T. Bitter and D. Dubbers sent slow neutrons axially through a tube while a non-axial DC magnetic field slowly turned through $2\pi$ [18]. Neutron spin followed the field, maintaining a constant angle $\theta$ to propagation. Richardson and colleagues created a related geometry with ultracold neutrons in a magnetic bottle and a rotating external field [19]. In each case the total field sweeps a cone.

Magnetic coupling produces the ordinary dynamical factor $\exp(i\int\magnetic{\boldsymbol\mu}\cdot\magnetic{\mathbf B}\,dt/\energy{\hbar})$. The author adds the cosmic interaction $-\mechanical{\mathbf s}\cdot\signal{\boldsymbol\Omega}$ and writes, for spin ½,

$$
\Delta\signal{\varphi}=-\frac{\mechanical{\mathbf s}\cdot\signal{\boldsymbol\Omega}\,T}{\energy{\hbar}}=-\pi\cos\theta.
$$

He identifies this with the extra measured geometric phase. He also notes that experimental results are often quoted without the spin-projection factor $1/2$, which makes the reported value twice as large. The conclusion is that a geometric phase depending on a fundamental attribute such as spin should have a physical interaction as its cause; the proposed cause here is interaction with relative cosmic matter currents.

> The printed photon-phase integral omits both the explicit ℏ normalisation and the factor 1/2 used earlier in the spin energy. The numerical phase is transcribed as stated, without silently resolving these convention changes.

## Interpretation and checks

Check a cone whose opening angle tends to zero: the enclosed solid angle and geometric phase tend to zero, modulo a full phase cycle. Spin-one-half and photon helicity give different coefficients. A raw time integral with a cosine can differ from the geometric result by the phase convention for the state basis. The source also changes normalisation, as recorded below. Agreement with the solid-angle result alone does not select a cosmic interaction over the standard transport calculation.
