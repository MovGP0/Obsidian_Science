## Physical question and model

Can the measured response of spin to rotation establish a cosmic origin for spin effects? First specify the rotating frame, its angular velocity, and a spin Hamiltonian. The lecture treats the resulting coupling as an interaction with cosmic matter; that interpretation is an additional claim.

## Lecture account

## Abstract

Mass and spin angular momentum are fundamental to dynamics and relativity. The source interprets spin as a circulating current of mass and energy. As mass sources a gravitational potential, spin sources a multicomponent gravitational potential. Mass acts as linear inertia, while spin acts as angular inertia. Spin is compared with gravity's counterpart of an electromagnetic magnetic moment.

The chapter develops consequences in the presence of cosmic gravity. The author claims that cosmic gravity underlies phenomena depending exclusively on particle spin, with a proposed physical proof of the spin–statistics connection and the boson–fermion division as the main result.

## Introduction

Chapters 10 and 11 examined rotating-frame effects without a local gravitational source. The author attributed them to cosmic gravity and relative cosmic matter currents. Their potentials were represented by Galilean metric components

$$
\mechanical{g}_{00}=-\left(1-\frac{\mechanical{v}^2}{\mechanical{c}^2}\right)=-\left(1-\frac{\mechanical{r}^2\signal{\omega}^2}{\mechanical{c}^2}\right),\qquad\mechanical{g}_{i0}=\mechanical{g}_{0i}=-\frac{\mechanical{r}\signal{\omega}}{\mechanical{c}}.
$$

Examples included rotational inertial forces, induction in uniformly moving systems, and the Sagnac and Michelson–Gale interferometers. These were described through Ampère-like interactions between matter currents in systems without intrinsic spin. This chapter instead examines intrinsic particle spin, aggregates of spins, and their interaction with relative cosmic currents, especially in rotating frames.

Mass is gravitational charge and, through cosmic interaction, takes on its inertial role. Its meaning is similar in classical and quantum equations. Spin is often regarded differently: quantum spin has discrete observable projections and requires a special mathematical representation. The author stresses that its expectation value nevertheless has the same physical content as classical angular momentum and angular inertia.

A collection of aligned particle spins can transfer angular momentum to a bulk body, producing ordinary rotation. Einstein and de Haas used the reverse relation in their 1915 experiment on the gyromagnetic ratio of electron spin [1].

The spin angular momentum of a physical system is

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf s}=\int(\mechanical{\mathbf r}\times\mechanical{\mathbf p})\,dV.
$$

Here $\mechanical{\mathbf p}$ is the momentum density of an infinitesimal matter element. The integral extends over the volume containing matter or energy. Intrinsic quantum spin has no adequate mechanical picture, but its empirical angular-momentum effects remain. Experiments on photons, electrons, and neutrons show these effects.

The author's central proposal is that classical spin, quantum spin, and all angular momentum are closed currents of gravitational charge. Conservation of average angular momentum in an ensemble is used to support this identification [2]. Spin is then a gravitomagnetic moment, in correspondence with the electromagnetic magnetic moment

$$
\magnetic{\boldsymbol\mu}=\frac12\int(\mechanical{\mathbf r}\times\electric{\mathbf j})\,dV,
$$

where $\electric{\mathbf j}$ is electric current density. The author argues that an effect depending only on particle spin should therefore have a gravitational origin, just as an effect depending on mass can be traced to gravity.

Spin-dependent gravitational effects are central to the proposed account. Effects commonly assigned to pseudo-forces are instead attributed to cosmic gravity. Nonuniform relative mass currents, such as those in a rotating frame, interact with spin. General relativity also predicts small effects on a gyroscope near a rotating massive body, as discussed in [[07.09 The GTR and Gravitomagnetism]] [3].

The source quotes Einstein's Nobel lecture [4]. In paraphrase, Newton's inertia law does not explain why inertial states have a special physical position. Mach proposed resistance to acceleration relative to other bodies rather than to space. General relativity connects inertial and gravitational effects; its metric field should, apart from coordinate freedom, be determined by matter. Einstein noted that the field equations allow acceleration induction, but at a scale then too small for direct mechanical detection.

Detecting the small Lense–Thirring and geodetic effects required decades of technical work [3, 5]. The author contrasts these local-source effects with his proposed much larger interaction with all matter in the Universe. He states that these cosmic effects are outside the standard Einsteinian treatment.

The proposed universal spin coupling is $\energy{E}=\mechanical{\mathbf s}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf B}_g/2$. For comparison, the source writes the electromagnetic coupling as $\energy{E}=\magnetic{\boldsymbol\mu}\cdot\magnetic{\mathbf B}$. The factor $1/2$ in the gravitational expression follows from the different definitions of spin and magnetic moment in terms of currents. With $\mechanical{g}_{i0}=-\mechanical{v}_i/\mechanical{c}$, the source obtains

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf B}_g=\nabla\times\mechanical{\mathbf A}_g=-\nabla\times\mechanical{\mathbf v}=-2\signal{\boldsymbol\Omega}. \tag{13.1}
$$

In a rotating frame, the resulting interaction energy and classical torque are

$$
\energy{E}_g=\frac12\mechanical{\mathbf s}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf B}_g=-\mechanical{\mathbf s}\cdot\signal{\boldsymbol\Omega}, \tag{13.2}
$$

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf T}=\mechanical{\mathbf s}\times\mechanical{\mathbf B}_g/2. \tag{13.3}
$$

For intrinsic quantum spin, whose magnitude is fixed and whose directional projections are quantised, this interaction modifies the dynamical phase:

$$
\signal{\varphi}=\int\frac{\energy{E}_g}{\energy{\hbar}}\,dt
=\int\frac{\mechanical{\mathbf s}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf B}_g}{2\energy{\hbar}}\,dt. \tag{13.4}
$$

The integral follows the classical spin path. The source states that this result is exact for Lagrangians at most quadratic in the variables. In the preceding chapter's terminology, it changes the action-wave phase.

The author argues that the existence of cosmic matter requires its relative currents to interact with angular momentum. In his view, any relativistic gravity theory must include these effects. He states that measured cosmic density then fixes their magnitude.

## Interpretation and checks

An energy proportional to angular momentum times angular frequency has the correct units. Its sign and factor depend on the Hamiltonian and frame convention. A successful rotation-coupling calculation does not by itself identify a distant source. Compare an independently specified cosmic-field model with ordinary rotating-frame quantum dynamics before assigning the same measured phase to a new cause.
