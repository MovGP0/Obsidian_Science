> [!TLDR]
> Observed physical facts guide the choice and assessment of first principles in physics.

## Physical question and model

What can an observation establish about a physical theory? Begin with reproducible measurements and distinguish them from the interpretation placed on them. Cosmic matter is observed through radiation, motion, and gravitational effects. A universal law for how that matter determines every local experiment does not follow from its existence alone.

## Lecture account

Both physics and philosophy are most coherent and accessible when they are grounded on facts. This is because facts are close to our senses, and our mind that makes the theories to understand facts is conditioned by a long evolutionary history of sensory experiences. In any case, it is always appropriate to take our experiences in the factual physical world as the basis of the first principles in physics. Since we are unable to observe and experience everything of the physical world, the experienced world needs to be supplemented with other reasonable principles to proceed with our theories. These postulated principles are provisional, because what is experienced is not the complete physical world. In any case, the observed physical facts are to be considered as the reliable guide and judge.

Einstein wrote (in a comment on the theory of superconductivity in 1922 [4]),

> The theoretical scientific researcher is not to be envied, because Nature – or more precisely put, experiment – is a merciless and not very kindly judge of his efforts. She never says “yes” to a theory; in the best case merely “perhaps”, but in most cases simply “no”. If an experiment agrees with the theory it means “perhaps”; if it does not agree, then it means “no”. Every theory is sure to experience its “no” someday, most theories already do so soon after their formulation.

The first principles that have been introduced from the early days of natural sciences have changed and evolved progressively, as empirical experiences accumulated. Among them, the most ancient are perhaps the statements about physical space and time. Then there is matter, which is the direct object of our sensory experience. Most postulates in physics are about the nature and behaviour of matter. The experience of space and time is indirect, through matter and material physical processes; hence it is very difficult to define and establish postulates about space and time.

A physical theory is formulated based on available empirical facts, principles distilled from experiences, and reasonable hypotheses. But, one can be in a peculiar situation when this is done while being oblivious of some decisive factual physical element, merely because that particular physical element is yet to be observed as a fact. Then the theory can be functional, and possibly very successful in many situations one encounters, but without the correct basis. Inconsistencies and paradoxes arise now and then, as warning signs, but they tend to be ignored if the theory is found very effective in its initial applications. Over time, a paradigmatic status is bestowed on an encompassing theory. Finally, when an all-important and decisive physical fact that is discordant with the theory is observed and confirmed, what is the option for the researchers? In spite of the factual existence and the obvious decisive role of the new fact, the general impulsive reaction might be to ignore and deny it, because acknowledging it is possible only by replacing the entire theory that served well for so long. The longer such a situation persists, the more difficult it becomes to accept the replacement of a paradigm, especially when it is formally possible to add supplementary ‘explanations’, by stretching the existing interpretations. Such ‘gods of the gaps’ can often survive for quite some duration. A paradigm change in physics is often resisted, and unreasonably delayed, even at the face of compelling empirical evidence. The history of our planetary models is an example. This does not happen often, but the work presented in this book concerns such a situation, unprecedented in its contents and implications.

We have very successful theories of dynamics and relativity. These are the Special Theory of Relativity (STR), the General Theory of Relativity (GTR) and Quantum Mechanics (QM). These theories were developed in the early decades of the 20th century, and they were in mature use by 1930, with decisive empirical support. The GTR is also the theory of the gravitational interaction. The other fundamental theories of interactions (electromagnetic and nuclear) are built on the basis of the STR. The GTR provided a seemingly complete basis for understanding the gravitational dynamics and even a theory of cosmology. The greatest lesson we have learned from the GTR is that matter and its currents generate many gravitational potentials and new physical effects, in contrast to just the attractive long range force of gravity that is familiar from the Newtonian theory of gravity [1]. The realization that physical processes and time itself are affected by gravitational potentials was the post-Newtonian knowledge that shaped the gravitational physics and astronomy of the 20th century.

However, now comes the real factual physical situation that renders all this success in a genuine shadow of inconsistency. The theories of relativity and dynamics were formulated assuming an overarching physical environment of space and time—the arena in which all dynamics and physical processes take place. Einstein's relativity assumed a ‘clean canvas’ of *empty space, devoid of matter or any other distributed physical entity*, as its background. Famously, *Einstein's theories eliminated the physical notions of an absolute space and universal absolute time*. The observational knowledge that our Universe is very vast and dynamic, and that it contains an enormous amount of matter and energy, emerged well after our fundamental physical theories were in use in their mature complete form. But, vast amounts of matter and its motion imply enormous gravity in which the entire physical world and its processes evolve. Since gravity affects every physical process, the large gravitational potentials of the cosmic matter must necessarily be playing a decisive role in dynamics. Yet, this obvious and overwhelming *gravitational effects of the cosmic matter are not part of the fundamental theories, because these theories were completed well before we knew anything significant about the matter and its gravity in the Universe*. Since the matter in the Universe is observed, and measured, its gravity and its effects on all physical processes cannot be denied or ignored. But, these effects could not be contained in the theories that preceded this decisive knowledge. Further, a matter-filled evolving Universe provides a real absolute spatial reference for motion and also a universal time, which are explicitly denied by the current theories. We are in this peculiar and somewhat uncomfortable situation of inconsistency, as serious as those few situations faced during the entire history of physics.

> [!note]
> A drastic paradigm change is the only remedy, and it is this cosmic gravitational paradigm for fundamental physics that is described and explained in this book. *The paradigm and the theory of Cosmic Relativity have the factual Universe as their first and cardinal principle*. That is in fact the only principle, along with the universal physical fact that matter and its motion, or current, dictate all observable relativistic physical effects.

## Interpretation and checks

Try classifying a statement before using it: is it a measured relation, a coordinate definition, or a proposed mechanism? Two models may agree with one observation while making different predictions elsewhere. A useful first principle restricts those predictions enough to permit a test. As a transfer exercise, explain why observing a magnetic field does not yet determine the force on an arbitrary material.
