> Galaxy recession and standard-candle distances established cosmic expansion, linking observations with Friedmann’s and Lemaître’s models.

## Physical question and model

What does a redshift–distance trend establish about expansion? In an FLRW model, comoving separations scale with the scale factor. Its rate and acceleration require matter content, pressure, curvature, and initial data, not the field equations alone.

## Lecture account

The most important physical feature of universal matter is galaxy recession, with apparent relative velocity proportional to relative distance. E. Hubble’s discovery launched modern cosmology and matched the uniform expansion in A. Friedmann’s mathematical model. The GTR does not demand expansion rather than contraction: models can contract and then bounce into expansion. Given present expansion, $d\mechanical{a}(t)/dt>0$, one can follow cosmic evolution if matter and radiation energy–momentum densities are known. Friedmann discussed monotonic evolution from a zero-size Universe and periodic evolution with a period of about 10 billion years, assuming a total mass of $5\times10^{21}$ solar masses.

After Friedmann’s expanding solution, G. Lemaître’s 1927 paper, *A homogeneous Universe of constant mass and increasing radius accounting for the radial speeds of the extragalactic nebulae*, independently found a solution in which nebulae always recede, from minus to plus infinity [8]. He sought a situation between Einstein’s static solution and de Sitter’s matter-free expanding solution with a cosmological constant. Exceptionally, he analysed the limited observations by Hubble and Strömberg of newly identified galaxies’ distances and velocities. V. M. Slipher pioneered careful spectral-shift measurements.

Distances were estimated using bright stars as ‘standard candles’ of known intrinsic luminosity $\energy{L}_0$. At distance $\mechanical{d}$, a source has detected ‘apparent luminosity’ $\energy{L}=\energy{L}_0/(4\pi\mechanical{d}^2)$. An accurate received-light measurement and a standard candle determine galaxy distance. One method assumed a class of bright stars had a standard maximum intrinsic luminosity. A more reliable method used Cepheid variables, whose luminosity varies periodically. Henrietta Swan Leavitt discovered this relation in 1912. The period and light output were tightly related because the period was inversely related to the ‘breathing’ star’s size. Leavitt studied Cepheids in the galactic neighbourhood; Hubble used Cepheids found in galaxies to estimate distances.

Extragalactic-nebula velocities were determined from Doppler shifts of identifiable atomic spectral lines. The source relates the light-frequency shift from its laboratory value to radial velocity by $\delta\signal{\nu}=-\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$. Lemaître showed that the ratio of the Universe’s ‘radii’ at observation and emission, $\mechanical{a}_o/\mechanical{a}_e$, is the observable $1+\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$. He used Strömberg’s published spectral-shift compilation from Mt. Wilson, with its 60- and 100-inch telescopes. Spectral shifts can be measured accurately but do not directly give systematic expansion velocities: stars, galaxies, and cluster members have random bound-orbit speeds up to $300\,\unit{km/s}$. With limited data, recession velocities are hard to determine unless sufficiently distant galaxies are selected so random speeds are relatively negligible. But distant galaxies are faint, approaching a telescope’s visibility limit.

> [!note]
> The printed Doppler relation uses a frequency shift $\delta\signal{\nu}$ on the left and a dimensionless speed ratio on the right. A fractional shift, $\delta\signal{\nu}/\signal{\nu}=-\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$, is dimensionally consistent. The source’s notation is retained above.

Lemaître connected observations to theory and estimated the velocity–distance constant at about $625\,\unit{km/(s\,Mpc)}$, now called the Hubble constant. In theory it is $\dot{\mechanical{a}}/\mechanical{a}$. His 1927 data had large errors and could not empirically establish the theoretical expansion. Yet he clearly set observational cosmology’s future direction. The paper remained unnoticed despite his seeking Eddington’s and de Sitter’s opinions. Two years later Hubble’s breakthrough drew British astronomers’ interest. Eddington was reminded of Lemaître’s work and highlighted it in a 1930 consolidation paper [9].

> [!note]
> The source gives Lemaître’s estimate as “625 km/million parsecs”, without a time denominator. The unit above states the velocity-per-distance meaning required for a Hubble constant.

H. P. Robertson considered de Sitter’s expanding solution with cosmological constant and zero matter density mathematically in 1928 [10]. Discussing astronomical consequences previously considered by de Sitter, Eddington, and Weyl, he wrote: “Important among these is the prediction that the spectral lines in light from distant sources should be displaced toward the red; this would be interpreted as Doppler effect due to a larger motion of recession of the source, and *seems to be in accord with the known facts concerning the radial velocities of spiral nebulae*.”

Hubble’s 1929 paper was decisive, coming from an expert astronomer with a large telescope. He considered 24 nebulae with measured shifts and reasonably confident distances, all estimated within about $2\,\unit{Mpc}$, only a thousandth of today’s known Universe. He stated that his “results establish a roughly linear relation between velocities and distances among nebulae for which velocities have been previously published, and the relation appears to dominate the distribution of velocities.” The estimated constant was $530\,\unit{km/(s\,Mpc)}$ [1]. He anticipated the de Sitter interpretation already considered by Lemaître: “The outstanding feature, however, is the possibility that the velocity-distance relation may represent the de Sitter effect, and hence that numerical data may be introduced into discussions of the general curvature of space.”

Astronomers measure spectral shifts, not radial velocities. Relating shift to velocity requires physical interpretation. Section 8.3.1 examines interpretations of the simple cosmic-evolution equations.

## Interpretation and checks

At small redshift the recession–distance relation is approximately linear. At large redshift, a quoted recession velocity depends on the chosen distance and time convention and is not simply a local Doppler speed. Expansion and contraction are both mathematically possible, but a bounce requires conditions that a particular matter model may not satisfy. Ask which independent observations select the expanding solution.
