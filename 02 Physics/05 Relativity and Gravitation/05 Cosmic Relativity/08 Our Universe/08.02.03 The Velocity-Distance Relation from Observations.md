> [!TLDR]
> Improved distance calibration and deeper galaxy samples refined the early measured velocity–distance relation.

## Physical question and model

Why did early Hubble-constant estimates differ from later ones? A measured spectral redshift is only one part of the slope; the distance calibration matters equally. State the standard-candle assumptions, sample selection, and treatment of peculiar velocities.

## Lecture account

Within two years of Hubble’s first convincing linear recession–distance relation, Hubble and M. L. Humason published a refined result from 40 new galaxies reaching about $32\,\unit{Mpc}$ [11]. They used an improved luminosity calibration. The Hubble constant was $560\,\unit{km/(s\,Mpc)}$, with quoted uncertainty “definitely less than 20%” (Fig. 8.2).

The Hubble value was troublesome because it implied a cosmic age. In reasonable matter-filled models, age is below $1/\mechanical{H}$ in consistent units. Since $1\,\unit{Mpc}=3.1\times10^{19}\,\unit{km}$, the value becomes $\mechanical{H}=(560\,\unit{km/s})/(3.1\times10^{19}\,\unit{km})$. Then $1/\mechanical{H}\simeq5.5\times10^{16}\,\unit{s}$, less than two billion years and younger than Earth—an embarrassing conclusion.

Larger telescopes and correction of standard-candle confusion changed this drastically, bringing $\mechanical{H}$ below $100\,\unit{km/(s\,Mpc)}$. Allan Sandage pioneered this with a new 200-inch telescope, described in the source as at Mt. Wilson of the Palomar observatory [12]. In 1958 he published $\mechanical{H}\approx75\,\unit{km/(s\,Mpc)}$. As he wrote, “the velocity-distance relation is perhaps the most important single instance where cosmological theory meets the test of observational astronomy.” His revision allowed an age up to 13 billion years. Hubble’s overestimate arose mainly from distance calibration problems and bright ionised-hydrogen regions (HII) being mistaken for stars. Sandage’s work in a sense saved big-bang models from discredit, with some earlier observations also suggesting lower values.

More recent measurements resolved some discrepancies, essentially a factor of two, that persisted for about 50 years [13]. The parameter is now measured through direct redshift–distance relations, indirect CMBR observations, and ‘standard flux’ gravitational waves from binary neutron-star mergers. Conventional astrophysical methods give about $73\pm4\,\unit{km/(s\,Mpc)}$, while CMBR observations give the lower estimate $67\pm3\,\unit{km/(s\,Mpc)}$.

## Interpretation and checks

A linear scatter plot can support an expansion trend while giving a biased slope if the luminosity scale is wrong. The historical uncertainty quoted in the lecture does not include every subsequently discovered systematic. Test this by uniformly rescaling all distances: the inferred slope changes inversely. Do not treat a schematic recreation of the old points as an independent fit or a modern parameter estimate.
