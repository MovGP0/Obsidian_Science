> [!TLDR]
> A homogeneous and isotropic expanding Universe has no preferred centre and produces a linear velocity–distance relation for every observer.

## Physical question and model

How do homogeneity and energy conservation constrain expansion? Use an FLRW perfect fluid with a specified equation of state. The continuity equation describes the energy change of a comoving volume through expansion work; it is not the conservation of a globally defined Newtonian energy for the entire Universe.

## Lecture account

A finite isotropic expanding matter cloud has a centre and boundary. Gravity can collapse it to a point, making such a Universe conceptually unsatisfactory. An infinite Universe has no particular centre. Uniform density gives the same potential everywhere and no gradient, but an infinite potential is intractable. Einstein therefore considered a finite curved, matter-filled Universe without a boundary. It is unstable because finite, but has no preferred centre.

Denial of a privileged cosmic centre characterises model Universes. This agreed with the limited Earth-based view of observational cosmology. Centuries of wrongly insisting that Earth occupied the centre may also have encouraged rapid acceptance of a centreless Universe, often called the Copernican Principle. ‘Cosmological Principle’ is more appropriate for the statement that the Universe looks the same from any position when local density and random-velocity variations are averaged out. Large-scale homogeneity and isotropy define this appearance. They imply that isotropic galaxy recession, with velocity proportional to distance, is seen from any point. *At any epoch, $\mechanical{v}\propto\mechanical{d}$ is an exact feature of an evolving homogeneous, isotropic Universe* [17] (Fig. 8.3).

![[cosmic-relativity-08-03-uniform-expansion.svg|**Fig. 8.3** A linear velocity–distance relation is an exact geometric feature of homogeneous, isotropic expansion. Two epochs in a small part of a large Universe are shown. The factor H varies with time and generally decreases. Expansion preserves uniform grids and geometric relations defined by matter and light. For zero spatial curvature, k = 0, a sphere’s area and radius retain the Euclidean relation A = 4πr².]]

H. P. Robertson and A. G. Walker showed that these symmetries give the general simple line element [10],

$$
d\mechanical{s}^2=\mechanical{g}_{ik}d\mechanical{x}^i d\mechanical{x}^k=\mechanical{c}^2dt^2-\mechanical{a}^2(t)d\mechanical{l}^2, \tag{8.7}
$$

with universal cosmic time $t$ and $\mechanical{l}$ a function of comoving spatial coordinates:

$$
d\mechanical{l}^2=d\mechanical{r}^2+f^2(t)d\Sigma^2, \tag{8.8}
$$

where $d\Sigma^2=d\mechanical{\theta}^2+\sin^2\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\phi}^2$. The function $f(\mechanical{r})$ depends on spatial curvature: $f(\mechanical{r})=\mechanical{r}$ for flat $k=0$, and $f(\mechanical{r})=(\sin\mechanical{r},\sinh\mechanical{r})$ for $k=(+1,-1)$.

> [!note]
> Equation (8.8) prints $f^2(t)$, while the following text defines $f(\mechanical{r})$. The printed equation is retained. Equations (8.7) and (8.2) also use opposite overall metric-sign conventions.

The remainder is simple: the left side of Einstein’s equation gives the fractional change of an elementary volume of matter, $\ddot{\mechanical{V}}/\mechanical{V}$, due to tidal forces. This is equated to matter and energy density:

$$
\frac{\ddot{\mechanical{V}}}{\mechanical{V}}=\frac{3}{\mechanical{a}}\frac{d^2\mechanical{a}}{dt^2}=-4\pi\mechanical{G}(\mechanical{\rho}+3\mechanical{p}/\mechanical{c}^2)+\mechanical{\Lambda}\mechanical{c}^2. \tag{8.9}
$$

The factor 3 reflects conversion from three-dimensional volume to linear dimensions. Pressure’s equivalent energy density contributes to gravity along with matter density. A $\mechanical{\Lambda}$ term must also be added if such an entity exists in this Universe.

The total amount of matter in comoving volume is $\energy{E}=\mechanical{\rho}\mechanical{V}$. Matter–energy conservation is

$$
\frac{d\energy{E}}{dt}+\mechanical{p}\frac{d\mechanical{V}}{dt}=\mechanical{V}\mechanical{c}^2\frac{d\mechanical{\rho}}{dt}+\mechanical{\rho}\mechanical{c}^2\frac{d\mechanical{V}}{dt}+\mechanical{p}\frac{d\mechanical{V}}{dt}=0. \tag{8.10}
$$

> [!note]
> The preceding source text writes $\energy{E}=\mechanical{\rho}\mechanical{V}$, but equation (8.10) uses energy $\energy{E}=\mechanical{\rho}\mechanical{V}\mechanical{c}^2$ for mass density $\mechanical{\rho}$. Both source forms are preserved.

Since $\dot{\mechanical{V}}/\mechanical{V}=3\dot{\mechanical{a}}/\mechanical{a}$, we get

$$
\dot{\mechanical{\rho}}=-3\frac{\dot{\mechanical{a}}}{\mechanical{a}}(\mechanical{\rho}+\mechanical{p}/\mechanical{c}^2). \tag{8.11}
$$

Equation (8.9) can be integrated with integrating factor $2\dot{\mechanical{a}}$. Conservation equation (8.11) eliminates pressure:

$$
\ddot{\mechanical{a}}=-\frac{4\pi\mechanical{G}}3(\mechanical{\rho}+3\mechanical{p}/\mechanical{c}^2)\mechanical{a}+\mechanical{c}^2\mechanical{\Lambda}\mechanical{a}/3. \tag{8.12}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
2\dot{\mechanical{a}}\ddot{\mechanical{a}}&=-\frac{8\pi\mechanical{G}}3(3\mechanical{\rho}+3\mechanical{p}/\mechanical{c}^2-2\mechanical{\rho})\mechanical{a}\dot{\mechanical{a}}+\frac{2\mechanical{c}^2}3\mechanical{\Lambda}\mechanical{a}\dot{\mechanical{a}}\\
&=\frac{8\pi\mechanical{G}}3\left(\dot{\mechanical{\rho}}\frac{\mechanical{a}}{\dot{\mechanical{a}}}+2\mechanical{\rho}\right)\mechanical{a}\dot{\mechanical{a}}+\frac{2\mechanical{c}^2}3\mechanical{\Lambda}\mechanical{a}\dot{\mechanical{a}}\\
&=\frac{8\pi\mechanical{G}}3(\dot{\mechanical{\rho}}\mechanical{a}^2+2\mechanical{\rho}\mechanical{a}\dot{\mechanical{a}})+\frac{2\mechanical{c}^2}3\mechanical{\Lambda}\mechanical{a}\dot{\mechanical{a}}\\
&=\frac{8\pi\mechanical{G}}3\frac{d}{dt}(\mechanical{\rho}\mechanical{a}^2)+\frac{2\mechanical{c}^2}3\mechanical{\Lambda}\mechanical{a}\dot{\mechanical{a}}.
\end{aligned} \tag{8.13}
$$

Integration gives

$$
\frac{\dot{\mechanical{a}}^2}{\mechanical{a}^2}=\frac{8\pi\mechanical{G}}3\mechanical{\rho}+\frac{\mechanical{\Lambda}\mechanical{c}^2}3+\frac{A_0}{\mechanical{a}^2}. \tag{8.14}
$$

Here $A_0\equiv-k\mechanical{c}^2$ is an integration constant. Without explicit geometric interpretation, a matter-free Universe with $\mechanical{\Lambda}=0$ but nonzero $A_0$ makes no physical sense: it expands without matter or cosmological constant.

In the GTR interpretation, $k/\mechanical{a}^2$ denotes cosmic curvature. At $k=0$, the Universe is Euclidean and density relates to the Hubble parameter by $\mechanical{\rho}_c=3\mechanical{H}^2/(8\pi\mechanical{G})$, the critical density. For a given observed Hubble value, density is below $\mechanical{\rho}_c$ for negative curvature and above it for positive curvature.

This derivation used no Newtonian input. It used Einstein’s equation as a description of physical tidal forces on an elementary matter volume. With homogeneity and isotropy, that description corresponds exactly to $\nabla^2\mechanical{\phi}$. The only ‘relativistic’ aspect is pressure as a gravity source through $\energy{E}=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2$. Thus, Poisson’s or Newton’s equation gives exactly the same result. Perhaps this reduces the identification of geometry with gravity and excessive geometrical enthusiasm.

Solving Einstein’s equation formally with the FLRW metric $\mechanical{g}_{ik}$ gives the same two evolution equations [17]:

$$
\frac1{\mechanical{a}^2}\left(\frac{d\mechanical{a}}{dt}\right)^2+\frac{k\mechanical{c}^2}{\mechanical{a}^2}\equiv\left(\frac{\dot{\mechanical{a}}}{\mechanical{a}}\right)^2+\frac{k\mechanical{c}^2}{\mechanical{a}^2}=\frac{8\pi\mechanical{G}\mechanical{\rho}}3, \tag{8.15}
$$

$$
\frac1{\mechanical{a}}\frac{d^2\mechanical{a}}{dt^2}\equiv\frac{\ddot{\mechanical{a}}}{\mechanical{a}}=-\frac{4\pi\mechanical{G}}3\left(\mechanical{\rho}+\frac{3\mechanical{p}}{\mechanical{c}^2}\right). \tag{8.16}
$$

Here $\mechanical{\rho}$ is average density of matter and energy in all forms, while $\mechanical{p}$ is pressure in the usual thermodynamic sense. Ordinary matter at low random velocities has $\mechanical{p}\approx0$; isotropic light has $\mechanical{p}=\energy{\epsilon}/3$, with energy density $\energy{\epsilon}$. These equations define two important observables: present Hubble parameter $\mechanical{H}_0=\dot{\mechanical{a}}/\mechanical{a}$ and deceleration parameter $q_0=-\ddot{\mechanical{a}}/(\mechanical{a}\mechanical{H}_0^2)$. They describe expansion and its changing rate. The next section discusses physical insights into expansion and deceleration.

E. A. Milne highlighted the Cosmological Principle in the velocity–distance relation. He proved that even a real explosion from a small volume centred at $\mechanical{r}=0$, with random outward velocities up to light speed [14, 15], eventually erases the initial spatial distribution and develops a linear relation between velocity and distance from the old centre, $\mechanical{d}=\mechanical{v}t$. The same relation is measured by any observer at any location, a consequence of its linearity (Fig. 8.4). The Milne Universe contains matter obeying the STR, with insignificant gravity and general-relativistic evolutionary effects. It expands freely from the beginning, with $\mechanical{a}(t)=\mechanical{c}t$ and present age $\mechanical{T}_0=1/\mechanical{H}_0$. Although model analysis is not our concern, this model is not entirely excluded by observations despite its difference from general-relativistic models.

Right: linearity gives the same radial velocity–distance relation from any position: v/r = v′/r′ = v″/r″ = 1/t = H.]]

## Interpretation and checks

For pressureless matter, density scales inversely with volume. Radiation loses an extra factor through photon redshift. Check the acceleration equation independently of the first Friedmann equation. The printed identification of volume acceleration with three times relative scale-factor acceleration omits a term quadratic in expansion rate: the second derivative of a cube is not just three times the second derivative of its base. This does not change the standard scale-factor acceleration equation stated later.
