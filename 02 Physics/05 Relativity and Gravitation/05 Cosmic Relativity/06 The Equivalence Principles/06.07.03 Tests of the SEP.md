> [!TLDR]
> Tests of the strong equivalence principle compare free fall with sensitivity beyond a body’s gravitational self-energy fraction, using lunar, pulsar, and wave observations.

## Physical question and model

Why does self-gravity set the scale of a strong-equivalence test? Estimate the binding-energy fraction and compare it with the experiment's fractional acceleration uncertainty. The simple spherical-body estimate neglects detailed density structure and relativistic corrections.

## Lecture account

A significant SEP test requires the precision in the differential acceleration $\delta\mechanical{a}/\mechanical{g}$ in a gravitational field $\mechanical{g}$ to be better than the fractional gravitational self-energy $\energy{\epsilon}_g=\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}^2/\mechanical{R}$ relative to the total energy equivalent $\energy{E}$ in the body. Since $\energy{E}$ is essentially the rest-mass energy of a massive test body,

$$
\frac{\energy{\epsilon}_g}{\energy{E}}\simeq\frac{\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}^2}{\mechanical{R}(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c}^2)}=\frac{\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}}{\mechanical{R}\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{6.5}
$$

This is the gravitational potential at the body’s surface divided by the square of the enormous velocity of light. Even for a body as large as Earth, $\energy{\epsilon}_g/\energy{E}$ is only $5\times10^{-10}$. Hence, a significant test requires observational access to the relative trajectories of planet-sized or stellar-scale test objects in a third body’s gravitational field. The intrinsic precision of the UFF test must be better than $\energy{\epsilon}_g/(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c}^2)$ for the SEP test to have significance [5]. This is why no test with laboratory-scale masses is a useful SEP test. For a practical laboratory test mass of about $3000\,\unit{kg}$, $\energy{\epsilon}_g/(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c}^2)<10^{-23}$, a billion times too small compared with the best sensitivity achievable in a laboratory experiment today. All SEP tests are also WEP tests, but the converse is not true.

The SEP has been tested and verified directly with LLR data by comparing Earth’s and the Moon’s free fall towards the Sun [14]. With gravitational self-energy $\energy{E}_g/(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2)\simeq5\times10^{-10}$ for Earth and relatively negligible self-energy for the Moon, the observational constraint on any SEP violation is $<0.04\%$. This is remarkable considering the small contribution of gravitational self-energy to these bodies’ total mass–energy.

A better observational constraint comes from monitoring the orbits of the triple stellar system PSR J0337+1715. It consists of a binary system of a millisecond radio pulsar ($366\,\unit{Hz}$) and a white dwarf, with a $1.6\,\unit{d}$ orbital period, itself in a much longer-period orbit ($327\,\unit{d}$) with another white dwarf. The orbits can be monitored with radio pulses from the neutron star (pulsar). A neutron star’s gravitational self-energy is about $10\%$, and it is comparatively negligible for a white dwarf. With a limit of 2 parts in $10^6$ on the UFF in this triple system, the SEP is established with precision $10^{-5}$, more than an order of magnitude tighter than the LLR result [19].

The most precise SEP test was due to a recent insight of mine: a gravitational wave propagating in the gravitational potential of large galaxy clusters is the purest form of gravitational energy. One can compare whether gravitational energy behaves as light does in a gravitational potential. That is an ideal SEP test, comparing the gravitational coupling of gravitational energy itself (‘gravitons’) with the gravitational coupling of photons. The comparison concerns the relative gravitational delay in wave propagation, called the Shapiro delay, when gravitational waves and light (gamma rays) from a binary neutron-star merger reach Earth more or less together. This test was done recently in work with my collaborator and friend George Gillies, confirming the SEP to a precision of $10^{-9}$ [20].

## Interpretation and checks

The ratio of gravitational constant times mass to radius times light speed squared is dimensionless. It becomes small for weakly bound laboratory bodies and larger for compact objects. Thus a precise material test need not be a strong test of gravitational self-energy. Ask whether an apparent composition signal instead tracks binding-energy fraction; these are different experimental design questions.
