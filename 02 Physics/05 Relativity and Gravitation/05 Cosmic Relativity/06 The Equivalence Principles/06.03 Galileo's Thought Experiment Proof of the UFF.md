> [!TLDR]
> Galileo's linked-masses argument tests the assumption that heavier bodies fall faster by comparing two descriptions of a connected pair.

## Physical question and model

Can tying a light and a heavy body prove universal free fall by logic alone? Galileo's argument tests the consistency of a proposed speed-versus-weight rule with an assumed rule for joining bodies. It is a powerful thought experiment, but it already uses physical assumptions about interaction and combination.

## Lecture account

In his ‘Dialogues Concerning Two New Sciences’, Galileo presented his arguments for the validity of the UFF [2]. It is of course well known that he might have performed some experiments (from the leaning tower of Pisa or other places), and there is documentary evidence that he compared oscillation periods of simple pendulums made of different materials to test the UFF. (Newton is usually credited with such experiments to test the UFF.) Therefore, Galileo was certainly convinced about the UFF, empirically. What is interesting is that *he presented a purely logical proof for the UFF*, without referring to anything empirical. If such a proof is possible, then one is admitting the amazing possibility that a fundamental feature of the physical world, that too pertaining to an interaction, can be discovered by pure thought, involving only logic! Since the issue of the UFF is very important for the foundations of dynamics, we should examine this ‘proof’ briefly, yet closely and carefully.

![[cosmic-relativity-06-01-galileo-linked-masses.svg|**Fig. 6.1** Galileo's logical ‘proof’ of the UFF. (a) Assume a heavy mass falls faster than a light mass. (b) If joined, the slower light mass should retard the heavier one. (c) The joined pair is also a single still heavier mass, which should fall faster. The two predictions contradict one another, leading to equal fall rates independent of mass. The source identifies a fallacy in combining the assumptions that the linked masses remain independent and that they form a single whole.]]

Galileo's argument is very simple (Fig. 6.1). Suppose that bodies of different weights fall at different rates, say heavier bodies falling faster as Aristotelians liked to believe. Now consider a lighter body A attached to the heavier body B. The lighter body will pull back on the heavier part slowing the entire system A+B down to some intermediate rate of fall. However, the combined system A+B is heavier than its individual components A and B, and hence should fall faster than either, applying literally the assumption that heavier bodies fall faster. We get a contradiction and *the only consistent solution is that all the bodies fall at the same rate*. Hence from a pure thought experiment a deep fact of fundamental importance for the gravitational interaction is discovered.

If this were a genuine instance of a ‘discovery by pure thought’, of a special feature of natural phenomena with its deep roots in gravitational physics and dynamics, it would be really remarkable and would in fact undermine the predominantly empirical basis of the physical sciences. The possibility of such discoveries is extremely important for the philosophy of science, because even a single instance of a substantial discovery points to the possible adequacy of pure thought for arriving at all truths of physical nature, without any empirical aid.

Nobody seems to have found any defect in Galileo's proof till now. However, there is indeed one central flaw, as I had found while analysing Galileo's seemingly amazing proof. Before I discuss the logical fallacy in Galileo's argument, I want to point out that physicists continue to this day finer experiments to check whether all bodies fall at the same rate in a gravitational field, more or less disregarding Galileo's elegant argument. Einstein, who relied on the universality of free fall for constructing his celebrated theory of gravity (General Relativity) did not refer to Galileo's thought experiment, but relied on the actual experimental observation by R. von Eötvös that it was a nearly perfect fact of nature [3].

The logical fallacy in Galileo's proof is straightforward to state, *after pinpointing it*. In fact, what Galileo does is to assume and juxtapose, in a seemingly convincing way, two contradictory assumptions in the same context, without simultaneous justification. One assumption in his proof is that *the two bodies act independently, and fall according to their individual nature even when they are bound together*. We call this ‘assumption P1’, from which follows the deduction that the rate of fall of the combined system is at an intermediate value. The other assumption ‘P2’ used in the second part of the proof is that *the two bodies do not fall according to their individual nature after they are linked together, but fall as a single heavier body*. This implies that the rate of fall of the combined body is larger than that of the larger body. Taken individually, these assumptions sound reasonable. But they should not be stated together, as part of the same argument since it is equivalent to simultaneously assuming the statements $S$ and $\neg S$ (NOT S) in the same argument. The crux of the matter is the undecidability of whether to treat two objects joined together as a single heavier object, or as two independently acting objects merely physically side by side with a joining constraint. By allowing both incompatible possibilities in a single argument chain, Galileo created confusion and came out with an apparent proof that was illusory and logically fallacious. *To see which one of P1 and P2 is correct, a real experiment is required*.

Later, when we learn that the physical reason for the UFF and WEP is the gravitational interaction with the distributed matter in the Universe, we will appreciate the reality that such a result can never be reached with only pure logic in a thought experiment. The bodies are not really ‘freely’ falling in the vacuum of empty space; they are falling in the gravitational presence of an enormous amount of matter. A remarkable consequence is the UFF.

## Interpretation and checks

The tied pair introduces tension and changes the system boundary. A prediction about its acceleration needs a law for those forces. The contradiction refutes a simple model only under those assumptions; it does not replace experiments for every possible force law. As a transfer question, consider bodies of different shapes in air: why does tying them together no longer isolate mass dependence?
