> [!TLDR]
> Lunar laser ranging measures the Earth–Moon distance and tests whether Earth and Moon fall towards the Sun with the same acceleration.

## Physical question and model

How does a lunar ranging time become a test of equivalence? First infer a round-trip light distance with station and reflector motion, atmospheric delay, and clock corrections. Then fit the Earth–Moon orbit to search for differential acceleration towards the Sun.

## Lecture account

The distance from particular points on Earth to specified points on the Moon has been measured by *laser ranging* for about four decades, with a precision of a few parts in $10^{14}$ [14]. This is possible because the orbital distance of about $384{,}000\,\unit{km}$ is monitored to a few millimetres. Pulses of a strong laser beam are sent to the Moon and reflected by arrays of corner-cube reflectors placed there during the Apollo and Lunakhod missions. A small part of the beam can be intercepted, and its arrival time relative to launch measured with great accuracy. Averaged over many pulses, the mean distance is measured from the two-way delay $\Delta t$ with high precision as

$$
\mechanical{D}_{\mathrm{e-m}}=\mechanical{c}\Delta t/2.
$$

One may consider this a test of the equivalence principle concerning Earth’s and the Moon’s free fall towards the Sun. The relative precision is of the same order as the relative timing precision, which has improved steadily over the past two decades. For a long time, this was the only experiment capable of testing the strong equivalence principle (SEP), involving the free fall of gravitational binding energy itself, while being relatively model-independent. It is also one of the most precise tests of the UFF in general. If Earth and Moon have different free-fall accelerations towards the Sun, the lunar orbit acquires a systematic polarisation called the Nordtvedt effect [18]. The Lunar Laser Ranging (LLR) data show no evidence for this. Hence, the UFF and the WEP are verified to a precision of about $10^{14}$.

> [!note]
> The source prints the last precision as $10^{14}$. In context, this denotes about one part in $10^{14}$, or a fractional precision of $10^{-14}$.

The LLR data contain more information about the relativistic nature of gravity than is required to establish the UFF. Therefore, LLR is an important milestone in the deep link between space exploration and fundamental physics, involving global cooperation.

From a civilisational point of view, Earth’s possession of a natural satellite was crucial to Newton’s noticing the link between falling terrestrial objects and the *falling* Moon, and discovering the universal law of gravity. The same system now provides some of the best tests of the relativistic theory of gravity that replaced Newton’s theory. Our resources for empirical tests may remain the same while theories change, sometimes drastically.

## Interpretation and checks

The factor of two in the basic range relation is required because the pulse returns. Millimetre precision over a lunar distance is at the order of parts in 10¹², not the 10¹⁴ fractional range precision stated in the lecture. The orbital test also depends on tides, reflector geometry, station motion, and gravitational dynamics. A small range residual is not itself the equivalence parameter.
