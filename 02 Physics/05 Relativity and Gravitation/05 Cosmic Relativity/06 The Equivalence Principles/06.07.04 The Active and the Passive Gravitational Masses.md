> [!TLDR]
> Active gravitational mass produces a gravitational field, passive gravitational mass responds to it, and inertial mass determines the body’s acceleration.

## Physical question and model

Why separate active, passive, and inertial mass in a test model? Active mass sources the field, passive mass sets response to it, and inertial mass sets response to a force. They can be independently named before a theory or data relates them.

## Lecture account

There has been a progressive refinement of the mass concept from Newtonian gravity to the General Theory of Relativity and its variants. In a generalised theory of gravitation, three distinct types of *mass* are relevant. The response of a body to the gravitational interaction, or any force, is determined by its *inertial mass*. The mass that is the source of gravity, the gravitational charge, is the *active gravitational mass*. The *passive gravitational mass* is the mass acted on by gravity. This finer distinction is made in certain speculative modifications of gravity theory. Then the question of equivalence between active and passive gravitational mass can arise. But there is no compelling empirical or theoretical reason yet for such speculation. After all, no such distinction is made between an *active charge* that sources the field and a *passive charge* that responds, either in electrodynamics or other interactions.

Standard WEP and UFF tests probe the universality of the gravitational-to-inertial mass ratio. Establishing the assumed universality of the active-to-passive gravitational mass ratio requires a conceptually different test, first performed by Kreuzer [21]. The equivalence of active and passive gravitational mass is not of fundamental importance in our discussion of dynamics. It is mentioned because the distinction was discussed in alternative gravity theories and a few ingenious experiments were conducted [5].

## Interpretation and checks

For an isolated pair, reciprocal forces and total momentum conservation constrain how source and response couplings can differ. In a field theory, include field momentum before making a momentum-balance claim. Introducing separate parameters is a way to formulate a test, not evidence that nature distinguishes the masses. A useful exercise is to identify which ratio a pendulum comparison measures and which it cannot measure.
