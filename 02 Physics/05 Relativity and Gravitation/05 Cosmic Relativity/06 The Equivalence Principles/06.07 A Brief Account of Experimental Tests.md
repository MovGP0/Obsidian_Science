> [!TLDR]
> Free-fall tests compare inertial-to-gravitational mass ratios. Their sensitivity to a particular binding energy depends on that energy’s contribution to the total mass.

## Physical question and model

What does a differential free-fall experiment constrain? It measures a difference between responses of two bodies after common acceleration and instrumental effects are controlled. Specify the convention used for its dimensionless violation parameter before comparing published bounds.

## Lecture account

Experimental tests of the UFF and the WEP were taken seriously from the time of Galileo. What experiments test is effectively the difference in the ratios of the inertial mass and gravitational mass of two bodies,

$$
\left(\frac{\mechanical{m}_i}{\mechanical{m}_g}\right)_1-\left(\frac{\mechanical{m}_i}{\mechanical{m}_g}\right)_2\equiv\mechanical{\eta}. \tag{6.4}
$$

When the inertial accelerations of the two bodies are compared in a gravitational field $\mechanical{g}$, one gets the differential acceleration $\delta\mechanical{a}=\mechanical{a}_1-\mechanical{a}_2$. Then $\mechanical{\eta}=\delta\mechanical{a}/\mechanical{g}$. When an experiment is done with precision $\mechanical{\eta}\simeq\mechanical{\eta}_{\mathrm{ex}}$, it tests the equivalence of total gravitational mass and total inertial mass. The rest mass of matter dominates what we call ‘mass’. However, there are various forms of energy in matter, which count as mass through $\energy{E}=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2$. For example, the electromagnetic energy of the interaction of charged electrons with the nucleus in light atoms can be about $10^{-7}$ of the rest mass in the nucleus. Then one can ask whether electromagnetic binding energy specifically respects the UFF. If it does not, two objects with a significant difference in their electrostatic energy will fall with slightly different accelerations in a gravitational field. If the difference in electrostatic energy is denoted by $\energy{\epsilon}$, the sensitivity (precision) of the specific UFF test for electrostatic energy is only $\mechanical{\eta}_{\mathrm{ex}}/\energy{\epsilon}$, not the full $\mechanical{\eta}_{\mathrm{ex}}$. For example, an experiment with sensitivity $\mechanical{\eta}_{\mathrm{ex}}\simeq10^{-12}$ to measure $\delta\mechanical{a}/\mechanical{g}$ can test the WEP to the same precision for rest mass, but its sensitivity to test the UFF of electrostatic energy is limited to about $10^{-5}$.

> [!note]
> In this precision estimate, the source uses $\energy{\epsilon}$ for a dimensionless difference in electrostatic-energy fractions of the total rest energy, rather than an energy in joules. This interpretation makes $\mechanical{\eta}_{\mathrm{ex}}/\energy{\epsilon}$ dimensionless.

It is important to distinguish a test of the UFF from a test of the WEP, which does not guarantee the UFF. The great interest in experimental activity in this field is not only because of its importance to the foundations of the theory of gravity. The same experiment can check whether there are new interactions [5, 13]. Such a hypothetical interaction is generally called a ‘fifth force’ and depends on the properties of fundamental particles, such as their *baryonic* or *leptonic* nature and spin. Then a differential acceleration can violate the UFF although the WEP is strictly valid. This is obvious from the familiar ‘second force’ of electromagnetism violating the UFF.

There are at least three ‘popular’ methods for a direct precision test of the WEP. One follows Galileo and tests the universality of free fall by dropping two bodies with different properties, such as mass, nucleon content, or binding-energy content, in a nearly uniform gravitational field, and looks for differential acceleration. An advantage is the relatively large value of the Earth’s gravitational field ($9.8\,\unit{m/s^2}$). However, high precision is difficult in terrestrial tests because of the limited free-fall duration, at best a second or so. Hence, space-borne experiments are needed for a precision test of this kind.

Another method, invented and performed with high precision for a wide range of materials by R. von Eötvös in Hungary [3], compares the balance between inertial and gravitational forces on two different materials in a beam balance that is also a torsion balance (free to turn with a small torque), at a high-latitude location on the rotating Earth. The centrifugal force due to Earth’s rotation acts on the inertial mass. Earth’s gravity acts on the gravitational mass and has a small horizontal component at a non-equatorial latitude. If the gravitational-to-inertial mass ratios differ for the two bodies, these horizontal components of the total force differ. This produces a small torque that turns the balance until torsion in the fibre balances the torque. Reversing the positions of the two bodies by turning the whole balance through $180^\circ$ changes the torque and deflection directions. Despite the technical difficulty of this last step in a precision experiment, von Eötvös established the equivalence principle to about 1 part in $10^8$, an enormous improvement over previous tests. The first results, which impressed and influenced Einstein, were published in 1890. Refined experiments were completed before 1910 and published only in 1922. This meant that $\mechanical{m}_i/\mechanical{m}_g$ is the same for all tested bodies to an accuracy of $10^{-8}$. Einstein knew the importance and reliability of these results, which is why he used them as a founding principle of the GTR.

A third method also relies on *free fall* in a gravitational field: all objects on Earth are falling towards the Sun in a field of about $0.6\,\unit{cm/s^2}$ while also moving in their orbit around it. The motions are decoupled. If two bodies with different material properties are suspended on Earth so that their motion towards the Sun is essentially free, relative acceleration due to a violation of the equivalence principle produces a relative displacement towards the Sun (Fig. 6.3b). This direction reverses every $12\,\unit{h}$ due to Earth’s rotation, so the relative displacement is modulated at the diurnal period. The most stable configuration, as realised by Dicke [9], uses a low-frequency torsion balance with the two bodies forming a ‘compositional dipole’ (Fig. 6.4). This torsion balance, suspended as a torsional pendulum, is nearly free to move in a plane perpendicular to Earth’s gravity. If the WEP is violated, its angle is modulated at the diurnal period, over and above its natural oscillations.

The advantages of this method are multiple: (a) Earth’s gravity is cancelled by tension in the torsional fibre and is decoupled from the EP-violating signal due to forces in a perpendicular plane; (b) the signal is periodic, with an essentially *infinite* free-fall duration, and can be monitored and integrated for months in a stable, regulated laboratory to improve the signal relative to noise; (c) the experiment is passive, since it rotates with Earth, which is essentially *noise-free*. The recognised problems are the difficulty of isolating other diurnal influences, such as thermal changes and other environmental parameters that affect the balance angle, and the relatively low relevant gravitational field ($0.6\,\unit{cm/s^2}$ compared with Earth’s $9.8\,\unit{m/s^2}$ in terrestrial free-fall experiments).

The precision to which the differential free-fall acceleration is constrained relative to the local gravitational field, $\delta\mechanical{a}/\mechanical{g}$, is the sensitivity of an experimental test of the UFF and the WEP. For a given precision in the differential-acceleration measurement, a larger actual gravitational acceleration gives a better test. Most high-sensitivity terrestrial experiments use torsion balances [10]. Our own experiment [11, 12], in a dedicated underground laboratory at a site remote from anthropogenic noise, was designed to probe the WEP in the Sun’s field with precision $\mechanical{\eta}\simeq10^{-13}$ (Fig. 6.4). Thermal noise at the diurnal frequency limited the ultimate sensitivity to about $\mechanical{\eta}\simeq10^{-12}$. The best terrestrial torsion-balance result to date comes from a progressively improved experiment at Washington University in Seattle, USA [13]. The experiment confirms the WEP at the level of $\mechanical{\eta}\approx10^{-13}$.

## Interpretation and checks

The source's mass-ratio difference is related to, but not identical in every convention with, the usual normalised differential-acceleration ratio. State signs and the reference acceleration. A quoted sensitivity is not automatically a detected violation. A complete result also reports statistical and systematic uncertainties, possible composition-dependent disturbances, and the confidence convention.
