> [!TLDR]
> The author contrasts a free-particle Hamiltonian in empty space with one containing the gravitational potential of universal matter.

## Physical question and model

Does moving relative to a homogeneous matter distribution break the symmetry of local laws? It changes the observed state of matter into a current distribution. A background state can select a reference flow while the equations remain Lorentz covariant.

## Lecture account

The STR’s empty space is homogeneous and isotropic for all inertial observers. Its spatial and temporal measures, the metric, retain those properties in every moving frame. Lorentz-invariant isotropy belongs specifically to Minkowski metric $\operatorname{diag}\{-1,1,1,1\}$ under transformation to motion, the STR’s basis.

There is a simple distinction between problems in empty STR space and the factual matter-filled Universe. A system’s Hamiltonian often identifies interaction energy with external potentials. Empty space has no gravitational energy, so a free particle of mass $\mechanical{m}$ has $\energy{H}=\mechanical{p}^2/(2\mechanical{m})$. In reality it is within the Universe’s gravity, so its Hamiltonian should be $\energy{H}=\mechanical{p}^2/(2\mechanical{m})-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{\Phi}_u$, with cosmic potential $\mechanical{\Phi}_u$. Such a Hamiltonian produces observable noninertial effects.

Universal matter is approximately homogeneous and isotropic on large scales. *Its potentials inherit these properties with even greater smoothness.* This appears in the FLRW metric for an absolute-rest observer, with local form $\operatorname{diag}\{-1,1,1,1\}$. Its superficial identity with Minkowski space can mislead one into treating a matter-filled Universe as empty. *FLRW isotropy applies only to an observer at rest and nonrotating relative to mean cosmic matter.* Motion creates an opposing relative matter current, just as any physical charge current. A directed current creates spatial anisotropy proportional to observer velocity: homogeneous isotropic matter-filled space becomes homogeneous but anisotropic for an inertially moving observer.

The anisotropic current and relativistic potentials make the metric anisotropic. This alone discards always-isotropic Minkowski space and Lorentz transformations as the correct basis, in the author’s account. Those transformations preserve metric isotropy at arbitrary velocities, contrary to observed cosmic velocity-dependent anisotropy. *The real Universe is maximally Lorentz violating.* The CMBR Doppler dipole measures cosmic-frame motion accurately; uniform motion cannot simply equal rest. Its decreasing temperature also provides universal absolute time, synchronised throughout the Universe better than one part in $10^5$, removing objections to universal simultaneity.

One might reject this despite its logic because testable Lorentz effects—dilation, velocity addition, and Doppler shifts—are confirmed. An absolute frame instead requires Galilean coordinate transformations. How can these give relativistic dilation? Surprisingly, it follows from absolute motion, as the next section shows. *Galilean transformations change metric measures, with time acquiring factor $\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}$ for absolute speed $\mechanical{v}$.* Once metric components $\mechanical{g}_{00},\mechanical{g}_{0i}$ follow, other relativistic results emerge through cosmic gravity.

## Interpretation and checks

Compare an electromagnetic plasma at rest with the same plasma viewed from a moving detector. Fields and particle distributions change, but this does not by itself violate covariance. Likewise, the cosmic matter flow is not proof of a new local vacuum law. A discriminating prediction must isolate a response not already due to the measured environment and standard gravitational geometry.
