title: "The Addition of Velocities in Cosmic Relativity"

> [!TLDR]
> The source examines relativistic velocity addition through the position-dependent time term introduced by clock synchronisation.

## Physical question and model

What does a measured relative velocity compare? Specify positions and times in one frame before composing motions. The source algebra recovers the relativistic addition formula after inserting rod and clock factors.

## Lecture account

Einstein derived the non-Galilean velocity-addition law in STR:

$$
\mechanical{w}=\frac{\mechanical{u}\pm\mechanical{v}}{1\pm\mechanical{u}\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{10.31}
$$

The nonlinear law depends on the $\mechanical{v}\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{c}^2$ term in the Lorentz transformation for time. The source calls this a local time, or position-dependent time in a moving frame, and relates it to synchronisation of spatially separated clocks.

The source traces this term to synchronisation with Galilean light signals or with clocks transported at the common frame's undetected velocity. Cosmic Relativity identifies it as an unavoidable synchronisation gap when one-way relative light velocity is assumed constant.

Consider a source at rest in frame A that emits a body, possibly light, at relative velocity $\mechanical{u}$. Frame A moves at velocity $\mechanical{v}$ relative to frame B. The source argues that the naive result $\mechanical{w}=\mechanical{u}+\mechanical{v}$ directly adds velocities referred to different frames. They must first be expressed in one frame.

Cosmic Relativity formulates the problem in the absolute cosmic frame: a source moving at $\mechanical{v}$ emits a body at source-relative velocity $\mechanical{u}$. The requested result is the body's absolute velocity. The author identifies this as the relevant quantity in high-energy physics and astrophysics.

The source-frame measurement is $d\mechanical{x}'/dt'=\mechanical{u}$. Before adding it to $\mechanical{v}$, transform it to the cosmic frame. Length contraction gives

$$
\Delta\mechanical{x}=\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}\,\Delta\mechanical{x}'.
$$

A moving-clock duration corresponds to $\Delta t=\signal{\gamma}\Delta t'$ in the cosmic frame. A velocity measurement, however, compares readings of spatially separated clocks. For separation $\Delta\mechanical{x}'$, the source adds the synchronisation gap $\mechanical{v}\Delta\mechanical{x}'/\mechanical{c}^2$:

$$
\Delta t=\signal{\gamma}\left(\Delta t'+\frac{\mechanical{v}\Delta\mechanical{x}'}{\mechanical{c}^2}\right).
$$

Writing the transformed velocity as $\mechanical{u}_a$ gives

$$
\mechanical{u}_a=\frac{d\mechanical{x}}{dt}=\frac{\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}\,d\mechanical{x}'}{\signal{\gamma}\left(dt'+\mechanical{v}\,d\mechanical{x}'/\mechanical{c}^2\right)}=\frac{(1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2)\,d\mechanical{x}'}{dt'+\mechanical{v}\,d\mechanical{x}'/\mechanical{c}^2}=\frac{(1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2)\mechanical{u}}{1+\mechanical{v}\mechanical{u}/\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{10.32}
$$

The source describes the magnitude of the transformed velocity as smaller than its source-frame value. Since $\mechanical{u}_a$ and $\mechanical{v}$ now refer to the same cosmic frame, they can be added:

$$
\mechanical{w}=\mechanical{v}+\frac{(1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2)\mechanical{u}}{1+\mechanical{v}\mechanical{u}/\mechanical{c}^2}=\frac{\mechanical{v}(1+\mechanical{v}\mechanical{u}/\mechanical{c}^2)+(1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2)\mechanical{u}}{1+\mechanical{v}\mechanical{u}/\mechanical{c}^2}=\frac{\mechanical{v}+\mechanical{u}}{1+\mechanical{v}\mechanical{u}/\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{10.33}
$$

The author identifies this as the correct velocity-addition law [2].

## Interpretation and checks

Recovering that formula shows agreement for the stated inputs, not Galilean addition of locally measured speeds. Check that input light speed remains light speed and that low speeds give the leading sum. A rod length measured from simultaneous endpoints is not the displacement between arbitrary trajectory events. Applying contraction to a trajectory differential needs justification; the complete Lorentz differential transformation avoids that ambiguity.
