> [!TLDR]
> Transportable atomic clocks allow direct reunion comparisons and tests of whether eastward and westward journeys produce different accumulated times.

## Physical question and model

How does an atomic clock comparison test elapsed time? Identical devices are initially compared, transported or moved under controlled conditions, then read together or linked by calibrated signal transfer. Their local frequency standards can also respond to environmental fields.

## Lecture account

Louis Essen built the first practical atomic time reference at the UK National Physical Laboratory in 1955 [12, 13], using caesium’s hyperfine transition at the source’s stated frequency of $9.18\,\unit{GHz}$. Transportable caesium clocks became available in the next decade, enabling comparison of carried clocks with stationary laboratory clocks and direct time-dilation tests.

Transported clocks typically move at hundreds of metres per second, much below laboratory absolute speed. In the STR, the laboratory reference has no dilation. All relative dilation comes from the transported clock, with proper time

$$
d\mechanical{\tau}=\sqrt{dt^2-d\mechanical{x}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}=dt\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{9.17}
$$

Relative motion through an inertial frame always lowers the clock’s rate. Einstein’s 1905 statement was:

> If one of two synchronous clocks at A is moved in a closed curve with constant velocity until it returns to A, the journey lasting $t$ seconds, then by the clock which has remained at rest the travelled clock on its arrival at A will be $t\mechanical{v}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)$ seconds slow.

We have already examined the different preferred-frame prediction. For a circuit of Earth, the source writes the relative factor as

$$
-\frac{\mechanical{v}^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}-\frac{\mechanical{v}_{\mathrm{ref}}^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}=-\frac{\mechanical{v}^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}\pm\frac{\mechanical{u}_r\mechanical{v}\cos\mechanical{\phi}}{\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{9.18}
$$

The negative sign is for eastward transport. Over $t$ seconds, the final term gives $\pm\mechanical{T}\mechanical{u}_r\mechanical{v}\cos\mechanical{\phi}/\mechanical{c}^2=\mechanical{u}_r\mechanical{L}\cos\mechanical{\phi}/\mechanical{c}^2$, with circuit circumference $\mechanical{L}$.

In summary, the STR predicts $-\mechanical{T}\mechanical{v}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)$ for a transported clock moving at laboratory-relative velocity $\mechanical{v}$, with no dilation when relative velocity is zero. An absolute-frame theory instead uses absolute velocity. The reference clock’s laboratory dilation is $\mechanical{u}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)$, with absolute reference speed $\mechanical{u}$. *A clock transported opposite to that velocity has smaller absolute speed, $\mechanical{u}-\mechanical{v}$, and runs faster.* This ‘anti-time dilation’ gives the decisive test. Synchronise two clocks, take one westward around Earth, and compare them on return. Hafele and Keating did this in 1971, early in caesium-clock development [14]. If the westward clocks returned older than the stationary ones, the STR is falsified in this argument.

## Interpretation and checks

The proper-time integral is the geometric prediction; magnetic shifts, temperature, collisions, and transport disturbances are instrument corrections. Modern laboratory [optical-clock measurements](https://www.nist.gov/publications/relativity-and-optical-clocks) separately demonstrate velocity and height effects at small scales. Such results test the complete relativistic clock model, not the existence of a uniquely selected absolute laboratory rest frame.
