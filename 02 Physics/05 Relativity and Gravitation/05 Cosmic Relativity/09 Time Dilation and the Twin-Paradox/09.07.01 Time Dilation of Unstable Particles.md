title: "Time Dilation of Unstable Particles"

> [!TLDR]
> Relativistic particle lifetimes confirm time dilation but, the author argues, give nearly identical predictions for relative-frame and absolute-frame theories.

## Physical question and model

How can particle decay act as a clock? Assume the decay probability per proper time is fixed for the prepared state. Predict a laboratory survival distribution using the particle velocity distribution and flight distance, then compare detected counts.

## Lecture account

H. J. Bhabha first proposed unstable cosmic-ray particles as fast clocks for relativistic dilation tests [7]. Two years later B. Rossi independently reached the same conclusion and measured muons generated by upper-atmosphere interactions [6].

These tests differ from comparing two previously synchronised clocks. The unstable-particle clock is a statistical average over many particles with average lifetime $\mechanical{\tau}$. It is ‘born’ in a nuclear interaction, at some instant $t\simeq0$, and the mean time before spontaneous decay is measured. The actual birth time may be unknown. Muons travel near light speed; an approximate production distance estimates their flight duration. If it greatly exceeds intrinsic lifetime, time dilation operates. Since exact speed is unknown, quantitative deductions use theory. Known particle energy and mass, with $\energy{E}=\mechanical{\gamma}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2$ or $\energy{E}^2=\mechanical{p}^2\mechanical{c}^2+\mechanical{m}^2\mechanical{c}^4$, determine $\mechanical{\gamma}=1/\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}$. Observed dilation is then checked against the source’s expression $\mechanical{\tau}'=\mechanical{\tau}\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}$.

For particle speed $\pm\mechanical{v}$ in a laboratory moving at $\mechanical{u}$ in the same direction, absolute speed is $\mechanical{v}'=(\mechanical{v}\pm\mechanical{u})/(1\pm\mechanical{u}\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}^2)$, from velocity addition valid in both theories. For terrestrial experiments, the laboratory’s absolute velocity is essentially the Solar System’s velocity through the CMBR frame, below $400\,\unit{km/s}$. For $\mechanical{v}\approx\mechanical{c}$,

$$
\mechanical{v}'=\frac{\mechanical{v}\pm\mechanical{u}}{1\pm\mechanical{u}/\mechanical{c}}\approx(\mechanical{v}\pm\mechanical{u})(1\mp\mechanical{u}/\mechanical{c})\approx\mechanical{v}\pm\mechanical{u}^2/\mechanical{c},
$$

since $\mechanical{u}\ll\mechanical{c}$. The absolute-frame time-dilation factor is

$$
\mechanical{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{Ab}}=\sqrt{1-\frac{\mechanical{v}'^2}{\mechanical{c}^2}}\approx\sqrt{1-\frac{\mechanical{v}^2}{\mechanical{c}^2}\mp\frac{2\mechanical{v}\mechanical{u}^2}{\mechanical{c}^3}}\approx\sqrt{1-\frac{\mechanical{v}^2}{\mechanical{c}^2}}\sqrt{1\mp2\mechanical{\gamma}^2\frac{\mechanical{u}^2}{\mechanical{c}^2}}. \tag{9.12}
$$

The signs show asymmetry from the frame’s fixed velocity: dilation depends on travel with or against its motion. STR dilation is independent of the selected rest frame’s motion, $\mechanical{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{STR}}=\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}$.

The asymmetry is small for fast particles. At $\mechanical{v}\approx0.9999\mechanical{c}$ and $\mechanical{\gamma}\approx70$, the factor $\sqrt{1\mp2\mechanical{\gamma}^2\mechanical{u}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}$ differs by less than $\pm1\%$, though a precise cosmic-ray experiment could observe it in principle.

Accelerators and storage rings routinely measure unstable-particle lifetimes, giving abundant accurate confirmation of time dilation. The important point here is that these measurements agree with both theories’ nearly identical predictions.

## Interpretation and checks

The laboratory mean lifetime is increased by the Lorentz factor under that model. Detector acceptance, production altitude, energy loss, and momentum spread affect a cosmic-ray comparison. The source velocity-addition expansion contains inconsistent near-light-speed substitutions; an exact relativistic composition must be used before expanding. Taking a small-speed approximation for one parameter does not justify dropping terms amplified by a large Lorentz factor.
