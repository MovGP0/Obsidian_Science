> [!TLDR]
> Proper time accumulates along a clock’s motion. The author contrasts this persistent difference with reversible length contraction and introduces the twin-clock question.

## Physical question and model

Why does reciprocal time dilation not give contradictory reunion ages? Each inertial observer compares different sets of distant simultaneous events. A reunion instead compares proper-time integrals along two complete paths with common endpoints.

## Lecture account

Motional time dilation has always been the difficult point in Einstein’s STR. Length contraction is kinematic: the interval between $\mechanical{x}_1$ and $\mechanical{x}_2$, of length $\delta\mechanical{x}$, changes in a frame moving at $\mechanical{v}$. It exists only during relative motion and vanishes at relative rest. Thus, there is no residual effect when the frames reunite. In contrast, *time duration accumulates*. Consider clocks A and B synchronised together. If moving A ticks at rate $\signal{n}'$ relative to B’s $\signal{n}$, A reads $\mechanical{T}'=\mechanical{T}\signal{n}'/\signal{n}$ when B reads $\mechanical{T}$. Reunited at rest after $\mechanical{T}$ seconds, the difference $\Delta\mechanical{T}=\mechanical{T}-\mechanical{T}'=\mechanical{T}(1-\signal{n}'/\signal{n})$ persists for inspection. This makes time dilation a crucial test of relativity.

Its mathematical expression follows from the STR’s kinematic basis. Proper time $d\mechanical{\tau}$ in a clock’s rest frame is invariant. If it covers relative distance $d\mechanical{x}$ in time $dt$, with $\mechanical{v}=d\mechanical{x}/dt$, it reads

$$
d\mechanical{\tau}=\sqrt{dt^2-d\mechanical{x}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}=dt\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2} \tag{9.1}
$$

instead of $dt$. Any relative spatial motion with respect to an inertial frame lowers its time rate.

Despite this kinematic basis, without an apparent physical reason for the slower rate, Einstein recognised its empirical importance. His 1905 statement is often quoted [5]:

> ... it follows that the time marked by the clock (viewed in the stationary system) is slow by $1-\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}$ seconds per second...
>
> If one of two synchronous clocks at A is moved in a closed curve with constant velocity until it returns to A, the journey lasting $t$ seconds, then by the clock which has remained at rest the travelled clock on its arrival at A will be $t\mechanical{v}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)$ s slow.

Einstein interpreted this as universal, beyond electrodynamics. But STR velocity is relative velocity between frames A and B, each equally entitled to rest status. If A is chosen at rest, moving B runs slower. If B is chosen at rest, moving A records less. When reunited, which clock shows less time? Since the STR treats inertial frames symmetrically, it appears to have no reliable answer. This is the source of Langevin’s 1911 twin-clock problem [1].

## Interpretation and checks

The clock on an inertial straight worldline has the maximal proper time between timelike-separated endpoints in flat spacetime. A piecewise inertial returning path generally has less. This statement does not choose an absolute rest frame. Check the zero-relative-speed limit, where identical paths give identical elapsed time. The clock hypothesis assumes that acceleration does not add an independent ideal-clock rate term beyond its role in the path.
