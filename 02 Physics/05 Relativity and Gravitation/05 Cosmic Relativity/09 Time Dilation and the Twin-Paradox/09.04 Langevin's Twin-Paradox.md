> [!TLDR]
> Langevin illustrated time dilation through radioactive samples and a returning space traveller. The author questions acceleration as the criterion selecting the less-aged clock.

## Physical question and model

What makes the travelling-twin experiment asymmetric? The two paths between departure and reunion differ. During long outbound and inbound legs the travelling clock changes its inertial motion relative to the home frame; both paths can still be calculated in one inertial chart.

## Lecture account

P. Langevin presented STR time dilation in his 1911 lecture at the International Congress of Philosophy in Bologna. He gave two concrete examples. The first compared lifetimes of two relatively moving radioactive-radium samples:

> Imagine a laboratory attached to the Earth, the motion of which can be considered as uniform translation, and in this laboratory there are two perfectly identical samples of radium. What we know about the spontaneous evolution of radioactive materials allows us to say that if the samples are kept in the laboratory, they will lose both their activity the same way over time and their activities remain continuously equal. But then send one of these samples with a sufficiently high velocity and then bring it back to the laboratory; this requires that at least at certain times this sample has undergone accelerations. We can say that on return, its proper time between departure and return is less than the measured time interval between these events by observers attached to the laboratory, so that it has less evolved than the other sample and therefore it will be more active than the latter; it will have aged less, having been more agitated.

This first preferentially treats one frame as ‘really moved’, using acceleration to select where physical time dilation occurs. It recalls Newton’s distinction between kinematic relative motion and real physical motion, and his use of centrifugal acceleration to argue for an absolute spatial reference. Langevin’s acceleration criterion therefore conflicts with the STR’s denial of physical absolute rest. A further problem arises during unaccelerated relative motion: A runs slower relative to B and B slower relative to A. One must wait for either to accelerate before selecting ‘real’ dilation, an ambiguity unexpected in a physical theory. Further difficulties follow below.

Langevin next compared a fast hypothetical space traveller with people ageing on Earth:

> For this it is sufficient that our traveller consents to be locked in a projectile that would be launched from the Earth with a velocity sufficiently close to that of light but lower, which is physically possible, while arranging an encounter with, for example, a star that happens after one year of the traveller’s life, and which sends him back to the Earth with the same velocity. Returned to the Earth he has aged two years, then he leaves his ark and finds our world two hundred years older, if his velocity remained in the range of only one twenty-thousandth less than the velocity of light. The most established experimental facts of physics allow us to assert that this would actually be so.

This became the ‘twin paradox’ in Einstein’s relativity. Textbooks usually omit its origin and present it using absolute-motion language: one brother takes a fast space voyage and returns younger than his stationary brother. Langevin recognised the conflict between reciprocal STR symmetry and unambiguous identification of the younger twin. Any inertial observer can claim rest and symmetric ageing. Thus, an answer needs kinematic asymmetry, for which he used the acceleration required to return and compare ages.

**Fig. 9.1(b)** The source’s two space-time diagrams, with B at rest on the left. The author describes initial kinematic symmetry, except for a brief gravity-like force felt in B’s frame (red arrow), and asserts equal proper times on the corresponding kinematic trajectories in the two descriptions. This is presented as the STR paradox. The diagrams are schematic, with arbitrary coordinate scales.

Neither the STR nor earlier Lorentz–Poincaré theory relates a physical effect to acceleration. Both rely on Lorentz transformations, from which acceleration cannot be deduced to cause real time dilation. Dilation depends on speed, not acceleration. Langevin’s asymmetric-acceleration criterion was therefore unjustified by the theory. Invoking an absolute criterion indirectly introduces absolute motion and undermines the STR. If only relative motion matters, either observer can claim rest. Earth observer A sees B’s outward and return trip; traveller B sees Earth moving out and back. Kinematics is symmetric except for the brief inertial, gravity-like force in B’s rest frame during acceleration. Figure 9.1 clarifies this. For those accepting equal inertial-observer status, deciding who ages less is a real paradox.

Time dilation was observed around 1940 through unstable cosmic-ray muons [6]. Their intrinsic lifetime was about $2.2\,\unit{\mu s}$, enough to travel below $700\,\unit{m}$ near light speed. Yet they travelled several kilometres from atmospheric nuclear-production sites to ground detectors. They must live longer than their intrinsic lifetime. This confirmed time dilation but did not select between the STR and an absolute-frame theory. Early criticism denying dilation is therefore irrelevant. A practical reversed experiment with a resting muon and moving observer cannot be done. Muon dilation thus establishes the phenomenon rather than resolving which twin ages less.

Later experiments, including slowly moving atomic clocks, confirmed dilation. The question remained whether relative-velocity STR or an absolute master-frame theory explained it. Fast unstable-particle observations cannot decide, because both give essentially identical predictions. *At speeds much lower than light, their predictions differ significantly.* An STR reference frame’s instantaneous velocity relative to a global frame must never enter its clock-dilation prediction. We therefore examine quantitative predictions of both theories.

## Interpretation and checks

Acceleration marks the change of worldline, but the elapsed-time difference is not proportional simply to the duration of a turning manoeuvre. A short turnaround can separate long legs with a large total difference. Reversing the whole description to another inertial chart preserves both proper times. Predict the effect of lengthening the coasting legs while keeping their speed fixed: the difference grows.
