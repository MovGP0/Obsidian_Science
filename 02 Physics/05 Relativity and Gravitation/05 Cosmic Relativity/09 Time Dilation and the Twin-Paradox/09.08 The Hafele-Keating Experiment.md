> [!TLDR]
> Hafele and Keating compared atomic clocks flown eastward and westward around Earth with US Naval Observatory clocks, accounting for motion and altitude.

## Physical question and model

Why do eastward and westward flying clocks differ? In an Earth-centred approximately inertial calculation, the ground and aircraft already have rotational velocities. Add the aircraft's velocity vector before squaring it, and include gravitational potential differences from flight altitude.

## Lecture account

J. C. Hafele and R. E. Keating of the US Naval Observatory (USNO) measured relative dilation by carrying caesium clocks on commercial round-the-world flights [14, 15]. One reference set remained at the observatory, like the home twin. Four identical Hewlett-Packard 5061A clocks first flew eastward, were compared with the laboratory, then flew westward and were compared again. Flight times were about $40$–$50\,\unit{h}$, with mean ground speeds $220$–$240\,\unit{m/s}$, around $830\,\unit{km/h}$. Routes began and ended in Washington and included northern-hemisphere stops from $42^\circ$ N at Rome to $13^\circ$ N at Guam, averaging about $32^\circ$ N (Fig. 9.2). The routes differed slightly; eastward travel had fewer stops and took less time.

![[cosmic-relativity-09-02-flight-directions.svg|**Fig. 9.2** Eastward and westward clock transport in the Hafele–Keating experiment. An explanatory direction diagram replaces the source route map and aircraft photos. Both circuits start and finish in Washington, with different intermediate stops. Rome and Guam illustrate the latitude range stated in the text; the drawing is not a full itinerary.]]

At a mean altitude near $9\,\unit{km}$, airborne clocks had a different gravitational potential from the references. Besides motional dilation, include gravitational dilation,

$$
\Delta\mechanical{T}_g=\mechanical{T}\mechanical{g}\mechanical{h}/\mechanical{c}^2,
$$

where $\mechanical{T}$ is duration at height $\mechanical{h}$ above surface clocks in Earth’s field $\mechanical{g}$ (Section 6.8). Since the height is much smaller than Earth’s radius, this accurately approximates the potential difference. It gives a faster rate of about $0.39\,\unit{ns/(h\,km)}$ of altitude. Mean altitude and duration therefore determine the gravitational contribution well. A $40\,\unit{h}$ flight at $9\,\unit{km}$ gives about $140\,\unit{ns}$, with ground clocks ageing less. It is independent of transport direction except for duration differences.

| --- | --- | --- |

| Average latitude | $34^\circ$ N | $31^\circ$ N |

Because commercial routes had many stops, the expected dilation requires actual ground speed $\mechanical{v}(t)$ and altitude above mean sea level $\mechanical{h}(t)$. In the STR, laboratory clocks are at rest, so relative velocity is the aircraft’s ground speed.

The four-clock data had substantial statistical spread. Preliminary results reported in 1971 were $(-110,+3,-52,-56)\,\unit{ns}$, mean $-54\,\unit{ns}$, eastward, and $(+74,+209,+240,+116)\,\unit{ns}$, mean $+160\,\unit{ns}$, westward [16]. The 1972 *Science* paper revised these to $(-74,-55,-57,-51)\,\unit{ns}$, mean $-59\pm10\,\unit{ns}$, and $(284,266,277,266)\,\unit{ns}$, mean $+273\pm7\,\unit{ns}$.

**Table 9.2** Observed flight-clock duration differences relative to laboratory clocks and the predictions compared by the author. Negative means slower flight-clock ageing; positive means faster. Subtracting the altitude contribution leaves motional dilation. All entries are in nanoseconds.

| Quantity | Eastward trip | Westward trip |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total observed difference, O | $-59\pm10$ | $+273\pm7$ |
| Gravitational difference $\delta\mechanical{T}_g$, G | $+144\pm14$ | $+179\pm18$ |
| Motional difference $\delta\mechanical{T}_v$, O − G | $-203\pm17$ | $+94\pm19$ |
| Einstein’s prediction $\delta\mechanical{T}_v$ (STR), as presented by the author | $-41\pm8$ | $-49\pm10$ |
| Absolute-frame prediction $\delta\mechanical{T}_v$ | $-185\pm10$ | $+104\pm10$ |

The experimenters calculated altitude contributions from detailed flight data as $+144\pm14\,\unit{ns}$ eastward and $+179\pm18\,\unit{ns}$ westward. Their 22% difference comes from the 19% flight-duration difference and partly the 5% higher mean westward altitude. At $9\,\unit{km}$, $\Delta\mechanical{T}_g/\mechanical{T}=\mechanical{g}\mechanical{h}/\mechanical{c}^2\simeq3.5\,\unit{ns/h}$, giving approximate totals $+141\,\unit{ns}$ and $+172\,\unit{ns}$, close to detailed estimates with known $\mechanical{h}(t)$. To test the STR’s velocity-dependent term, subtract gravitational dilation from observed total differences, O − G in Table 9.2.

The author takes the motional-data row as unambiguous evidence against the STR: after subtracting gravity, westward clocks aged faster than stationary clocks. The final row is presented as support for an absolute frame. The travelling twin returned older, contrary to the usual textbook account.

The source gives Einstein’s time-varying-speed formula as

$$
\Delta\mechanical{T}_v\simeq\int-\frac{\mechanical{v}^2(t)\,dt}{2\mechanical{c}^2}-\frac{\mechanical{T}_f\bar{\mechanical{v}}^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{9.19}
$$

Here $\mechanical{T}_f$ is flight duration and $\bar{\mechanical{v}}$ mean relative speed. Recorded mean speeds and total durations give about $-48\,\unit{ns}$ eastward and $-47\,\unit{ns}$ westward. Hafele and Keating’s actual-path estimates appear in Table 9.2. Both are time dilations, independent of direction.

> [!note]
> The source prints a minus before the mean-speed term in (9.19). The surrounding explanation treats that term as an estimate of the integral, rather than an additional contribution. The printed form is retained.

Eastward dilation was about five times the author’s stated STR expectation. Averaging the opposite-direction observations gives $-55\pm18\,\unit{ns}$, consistent with the source’s STR estimate $-45\pm9\,\unit{ns}$. The asymmetric absolute-frame signature cancels in this average.

The preferred-frame prediction, discussed earlier, is

$$
\frac{\mechanical{v}'^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}-\frac{\mechanical{v}_{\mathrm{ref}}^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}=\frac{\mechanical{v}^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}\pm\frac{\mechanical{u}_r\mechanical{v}}{\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{9.20}
$$

Earth’s tangential rotational velocity at latitude $\mechanical{\lambda}$ is $\mechanical{v}_r=\mechanical{R}\mechanical{\Omega}\cos\mechanical{\lambda}$. The westward route averages $\mechanical{v}_{rw}\approx399\,\unit{m/s}$ at $31^\circ$ N; the eastward route gives $\mechanical{v}_{re}\approx386\,\unit{m/s}$ at $34^\circ$ N. A circuit returns the clocks to their start, so relative speed times duration equals distance at mean latitude: $\mechanical{v}\mechanical{T}=2\pi\mechanical{R}_e\cos\mechanical{\lambda}$. Predicted total motional dilation is $-185\pm10\,\unit{ns}$ eastward and $+104\pm10\,\unit{ns}$ westward. Both closely match observation; the westward sign means faster ageing of the transported clock.

This difference should have prompted reconsideration of time dilation and the twin paradox: one travelling twin returned older. Instead, most people, including Hafele and Keating, fitted results into the STR by changing the calculation frame. Choosing a global frame effectively identical to an absolute frame produces agreement. For Earth circuits, only Earth’s rotation must be removed because orbital and Solar-System motion are common to reference and flight clocks. The experimenters chose an Earth-centred nonrotating frame, also nonrotating relative to the average stellar frame (Fig. 9.3). Thus, the author argues, they effectively used an absolute-frame theory for the predicted dilation. A chance for a paradigm-changing discovery was lost, along with investigation of the absolute frame’s physical nature and the cause of moving-clock dilation. As we will see, this was costly for physics and its community.

## Interpretation and checks

The cross term between Earth rotation and aircraft motion changes sign with direction. It is present in the standard relativistic calculation and is not by itself evidence against special relativity. Height and velocity histories must be integrated; mean speed alone does not capture every term. Read the historical tables with their uncertainties and clock-drift treatment. A comparison using an Earth-fixed rotating chart must retain the corresponding metric terms.
