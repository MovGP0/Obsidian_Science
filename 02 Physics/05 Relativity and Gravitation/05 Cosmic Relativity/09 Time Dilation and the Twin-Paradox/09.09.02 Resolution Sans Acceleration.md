title: "Resolution Sans Acceleration"

> [!TLDR]
> The author examines clock-transfer, Doppler-signal, and simultaneity arguments that seek to resolve the twin-clock problem without acceleration.

## Physical question and model

Can a relay of inertial clocks reproduce a twin-style comparison? Yes, but the relay clock is set by a transfer rule at a meeting event. Its displayed time is not automatically the proper age of one continuous traveller.

## Lecture account

Many texts favour an acceleration-free resolution. This is needed because the problem can be posed without either twin accelerating. A and B synchronise when meeting, then separate inertially at relative velocity $\mechanical{v}$. According to the STR, each clock ages less relative to the other. B later transfers its reading to an inertial observer C moving oppositely at relative velocity $-2\mechanical{v}$. At transfer, C and B have the same age. C finally compares with A at the same location. None of the clocks has accelerated [13]. Which shows less time, A or C?

The expected relative dilation, printed as $\Delta\mechanical{T}\approx-\mechanical{T}\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2$, is independent of how the problem is set up. Thus, acceleration cannot resolve it. A physical problem should have one resolution through a definite effect: unequal readings of initially synchronised clocks need a unique physical mechanism. Yet some accept any calculation matching experience without consistent physical justification.

One early resolution invoked Doppler shifts of signals between twins, whether signals were actually sent or not. Another attributes the clock-reading difference to a large synchronisation shift, a change of the ‘line of simultaneity’, when a frame reverses its trajectory.

Both admit symmetric time dilation during purely inertial segments: B is younger by $\mechanical{T}\mechanical{v}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)$ in A’s rest frame and vice versa in B’s. On reversing one trajectory, two different accounts appear. The first assumes regular electromagnetic pulses throughout the journey. This ‘Doppler resolution’ uses the supposed coincidence, from $\mechanical{v}\mechanical{T}=2\mechanical{L}$, between second-order dilation $-\mechanical{T}\mechanical{v}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)$ and first-order two-way Doppler asymmetry $-2\mechanical{L}\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}^2$. Here $\mechanical{T}$ is total duration and $\mechanical{L}$ one-way distance. Since many clock-comparison experiments exchange no signals during relative motion, we need not take this Doppler explanation seriously.

> [!note]
> The source first prints the relative dilation without the factor $1/2$, then uses that factor later. Also, substituting $\mechanical{v}\mechanical{T}=2\mechanical{L}$ into the later second-order expression gives $-\mechanical{L}\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}^2$, half the quoted Doppler term. These printed expressions are retained.

The term $\mechanical{L}\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}^2$ is also the first-order Lorentz time modification in A relative to B. It expresses nonsimultaneity of clocks separated by $\mechanical{L}$ in a frame moving at $-\mechanical{v}$. The claim is then made that, when B reverses, distant A suddenly changes synchronisation relative to B by $2\mechanical{L}\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}^2$. B’s reading cannot jump, since a nearby inertial clock would reveal it. A supposedly jumps ahead and becomes older than B by $2\mechanical{L}\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}^2=\mechanical{T}\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2$.

Figure 9.4 depicts this account. In A’s frame, B ages less by $\mechanical{T}\mechanical{v}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)$ during a trip with $2\mechanical{L}=\mechanical{v}\mechanical{T}$. In equally valid B coordinates, moving A ages less until B returns. At halfway, A’s deficit is $\mechanical{T}\mechanical{v}^2/(4\mechanical{c}^2)$. B’s reversal allegedly adds a sudden $2\mechanical{L}\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}^2=\mechanical{T}\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2$ to A’s age. Return travel loses another $\mechanical{T}\mechanical{v}^2/(4\mechanical{c}^2)$, giving net $\mechanical{T}\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2-\mechanical{T}\mechanical{v}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)=+\mechanical{T}\mechanical{v}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)$. At reunion, A is older by $\Delta\mechanical{T}=\mechanical{T}\mechanical{v}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)$. Thus, this explanation makes B younger through A’s sudden ageing far away, rather than time dilation. The change is assigned to A’s clock although B reversed. The author finds acceptance of this account surprising.

## Interpretation and checks

Keep track of the initial settings and the local handover separately from elapsed intervals. Velocities of successive inertial legs must be related by relativistic composition if the statement is exact; simply writing minus twice a speed mixes frames. The broken comparison path can be timed by several inertial clocks without making it identical to the home path. Recover the same result by summing proper-time segments with the declared settings.
