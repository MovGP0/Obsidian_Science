> [!TLDR]
> The author formulates a symmetric two-clock comparison, excludes acceleration intervals from clock readings, and contrasts relative-frame and absolute-frame predictions.

## Physical question and model

What does a symmetric two-clock separation actually compare? Two inertial clocks that meet only once have no later common event at which to compare their elapsed readings. A statement about their readings after equal coordinate durations already chooses a simultaneity slice.

## Lecture account

We formulate the paradox without an inadvertent absolute-frame bias. Two inertial frames A and B meet and separate at relative velocity $\mechanical{v}$, for example $3000\,\unit{km/s}$. B moves at $\mechanical{v}$ relative to A and A at $-\mechanical{v}$ relative to B. At proximity, clocks are synchronised to $t_A=t_B=0$. They separate for a long interval, say $10\,\unit{d}$ measured in either frame. At the end, readings are $t_{A1}$ and $t_{B1}$. Which has aged less: $t_{A1}>t_{B1}$ or the reverse? *The STR has no unique answer at this stage of the symmetric situation.*

The clocks function independently, as do each frame’s accelerations. If acceleration changes a clock’s time, only the accelerated clock can be affected, not clocks in unaccelerated frames.

To compare readings, the frames must reunite, reversing either or both trajectories. Each carries about a dozen rocket accelerators, each giving a tested burn of about $1\,\unit{s}$. A and B fire different numbers and sequences, producing different accelerations $\mechanical{a}_A,\mechanical{a}_B$. Each protects its clock by freezing its reading before a burn, keeping it off throughout acceleration, and restarting from the saved reading afterwards. The short excluded time is known and negligible. *Clock readings are never allowed to be affected by accelerations.* The frames now approach at average velocity $-\mechanical{v}$, maintaining average relative speed $\mechanical{v}$ apart from brief burns. They finally reunite. Figure 9.1 shows one scenario in each rest-frame description. Kinematics is approximately symmetric. Which clock records more time?

An absolute-frame theory gives a clear answer: the clock moving more relative to the unique preferred frame ages less. For A, $\Delta\mechanical{T}_A/\mechanical{T}_A=-\mechanical{v}_A^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)$; for B, $\Delta\mechanical{T}_B/\mechanical{T}_B=-\mechanical{v}_B^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)$. These are speeds relative to the master frame, and relative dilation is $\Delta\mechanical{T}_a-\Delta\mechanical{T}_b$. The STR cannot choose $t_A>t_B$ or $t_B>t_A$. This is its real twin paradox.

The popular resolution uses only the diagram with A at rest and says B ages less because its trajectory’s proper time is shorter. This is incorrect because A’s proper time relative to B was not evaluated. The same absolute-frame prejudice that led Einstein to call a platform observer ‘really stationary’ and a train observer ‘really moving’ in simultaneity is present here (Section 5.4).

We will examine Einstein’s 1918 resolution, developed after criticism and persuasion. First, we calculate both theories’ motional-dilation predictions and compare experiments, allowing a logical choice. This will expose weaknesses in STR resolutions and in Einstein’s GTR resolution based on gravitational time dilation during acceleration through the equivalence principle.

## Interpretation and checks

Neither distant clock must have a smaller invariant age at an unspecified later comparison. Each can assign slower coordinate ticking to the other using its own simultaneity rule. To create a reunion experiment, supply a return path or relay protocol and include its clock settings. That additional information resolves the underdetermination rather than exposing a contradiction in the original two inertial worldlines.
