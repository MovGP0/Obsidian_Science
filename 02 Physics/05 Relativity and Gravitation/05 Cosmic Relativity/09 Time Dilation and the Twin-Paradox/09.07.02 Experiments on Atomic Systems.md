> [!TLDR]
> Transverse Doppler measurements use atomic radiation as a clock and test the second-order frequency shift of moving atoms.

## Physical question and model

What does a transverse atomic spectroscopy experiment isolate? It compares source and receiver transition frequencies through a specified angular acceptance. First-order Doppler cancellation does not eliminate the need to define the angle in the correct frame.

## Lecture account

Several experiments measure the ‘transverse Doppler shift’. Einstein’s 1905 Doppler expression was

$$
\signal{\nu}'=\mechanical{\gamma}\signal{\nu}\left(1+\frac{\mechanical{v}}{\mechanical{c}}\cos\mechanical{\theta}\right). \tag{9.13}
$$

At angle $\mechanical{\theta}=\pi/2$, the residual second-order shift is

$$
\signal{\nu}'=\signal{\nu}\frac1{\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}}\approx\signal{\nu}(1+\mechanical{v}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)). \tag{9.14}
$$

This predicts an increased received frequency. Section 4.3.1 discussed ambiguity in Einstein’s original formula, since experiments instead showed a decrease. The measurement observes radiation from a fast atom travelling transverse to the atom–detector line. Atomic energy-level differences provide the clock. The transverse Doppler measurement is its transition’s time dilation. The pioneering Ives–Stilwell experiment [8] tested ether-theory time modification in Lorentz transformations and J. Larmor’s interpretation around 1900 [9]. Einstein suggested accelerated-ion, or canal-ray, emission for such tests in 1907 [10], also in his 1907 *Jahrbuch* paper [11]. Ives cited Einstein but never described the test as one of the STR, causing later controversy. That history is irrelevant here: the experiment did not distinguish the two theories, for reasons discussed below.

To isolate time dilation, Ives and Stilwell built apparatus simultaneously observing light emitted forwards and backwards relative to the atoms’ motion. A small concave mirror, offset and tilted from the beam axis, reflected backward light. The source was ionised hydrogen accelerated to $\mechanical{v}\approx0.01\mechanical{c}$.

The STR expression for forward and backward Doppler emission, *time-dilated by source velocity*, should be

$$
\signal{\nu}'=\signal{\nu}(1\pm\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c})\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}\approx\signal{\nu}(1\pm\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c})(1-\mechanical{v}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2))\approx\signal{\nu}(1\pm\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{v}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)). \tag{9.15}
$$

The ignored third-order term is below one per cent of the second-order term. Two spectral lines corresponding to $\pm\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$ should have a small measurable asymmetry from $-\mechanical{v}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)$, below $0.5\%$.

In a preferred-frame theory, the first-order Doppler term is unchanged because detector and source share laboratory absolute velocity $\mechanical{u}$. The second-order term becomes

$$
\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}'^2/\mechanical{c}^2}=\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2-\mechanical{u}^2/\mechanical{c}^2-2\mechanical{u}\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}^2}\approx1-\mechanical{v}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)-\mechanical{u}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)-\mechanical{u}\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}^2. \tag{9.16}
$$

The laboratory reference’s $\mechanical{u}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)$ dilation cancels in comparison. With $\mechanical{u}\ll\mechanical{v}$, the last term corrects spectral asymmetry by about $0.1\%$, measurable in principle but easily missed in a difficult experiment.

When clock speed is comparable with or below frame speed, as for laboratory atomic clocks, a different approximation produces significant asymmetric predictions that falsify the STR. We now discuss such tests.

## Interpretation and checks

Use forward and backward observations or a controlled transverse geometry to isolate the second-order term. A source-frame transverse photon and a laboratory transverse photon are different rays. Fields, collisions, line shape, and detector geometry can shift the inferred line centre. The sign of the measured shift must be predicted from that full setup before comparing it with a clock-rate expression.
