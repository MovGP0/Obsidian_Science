## Lecture account

## Abstract

Space and Time are the primary and essential notions in the construction of physical theories. The physical meaning of these unobservable entities and their relation to sensible matter have changed significantly with the evolution of the fundamental theories of physics. We will explore this evolution from the early days of modern physics to the present time. This will then serve as a crucial basis for understanding the gravitational physics of dynamics and relativity.

## Introduction

Space and Time are the primary and essential notions in the construction of physical theories. Our intuitive understanding of these concepts is made precise in physics by defining their properties and measurability. However, we should be aware that their postulated features are factually extrapolated from our empirical experience, and not fixed by any prior principles or knowledge. What is demanded is the overall consistency between the definitions and the observable aspects of the dynamics of matter, the description of which requires these definitions and measures. However, this consistency is very difficult to achieve if one specifies any physical property to space that is similar to the familiar physical properties of matter. That is why these primary notions have remained half-metaphysical and half-physical, so to say.

I do not intend to review the progressive development of the ideas on space and time in physics and philosophy, starting from the antiquity. Instead, I begin with a description of space and time as discussed by Newton in his ‘Principia Mathematica’, as required for the mature quantitative description of dynamics [1]. He carefully distinguished absolute space and time from relative space and time. His ‘water-in-a-rotating-bucket’ experiment was meant to bring out the necessity of absolute space in the description of dynamics. Two centuries later, Ernst Mach recognised the logical and empirical gap in Newton's assertion about an absolute space and critically analysed it [2]. He speculated that the appearance of a centrifugal force in a non-inertial frame could be because the motion was relative to the stars and indeed the entire matter in the Universe, and not because of the rotation in an absolute space. Mach was suggesting an interaction with the surrounding matter as the source of the centrifugal force. Even though he was not explicit about gravitation as the interaction responsible, the idea was logically compelling enough to influence and motivate Einstein to try to incorporate the Machian connection in his theory of gravitation, the General Theory of Relativity (GTR). However, in spite of the theory containing some features of relativistic inertial forces in the vicinity of moving and rotating matter, *the primary problem of the origin of inertia and inertial forces has remained unsolved in physics*. In fact, the present-day physics rejects Mach's speculation. In effect, the Machian critique of Newton's absolute space is the defining debate that is most relevant for any discussion of relativity and dynamics.

However, these events happened at a time when we had no significant knowledge about the true matter content of the Universe. The GTR was completed in 1915 and Edwin Hubble's discovery of the systematic recession of distant galaxies happened another 14 years later. A reasonable model of the Universe, with supporting observations and measurements, took even more time. Hence, *the current theories of relativity have a space devoid of matter and gravity as their fundamental theoretical space*. The equations of the GTR do not incorporate the gravitational effects of the cosmic matter while discussing the motion of a test particle or the time dilation of a moving clock in the space of this Universe. In contrast, all empirical evidence and results of the experimental tests of the theories unavoidably contain the effects of the cosmic gravitational potentials, of the factual matter in the Universe. *Our fundamental theories have missed incorporating genuine cosmic gravitational effects because of their relative historical precedence over modern cosmology. Therefore, even if a majority of experiments agree with the current theories, there still will be some crucial experiments that definitely disagree*.

As we have already seen briefly, it is indeed true that the cosmic gravitational effects are large and their existence demands drastic revisions in our understanding of relativity, dynamics, and the concepts of space and time.

## Interpretation and checks

For each model, identify a clock procedure, a length procedure, and a transformation between observers. A change of coordinates is not automatically a change of physical law. The main transfer question is whether two descriptions predict different readings for the same instruments. If they do not, an experiment cannot select between them on that basis.
