## Physical question and model

What part of the Einstein–Bergson disagreement concerns physical clocks, and what part concerns experienced duration? A philosophical account of lived time and a coordinate rule for distant events need not answer the same question.

## Lecture account

The reading of the previous section would have shaken up anybody with some trust in the current fundamental physical theories. What just happened in the analysis of a simple physical situation was the most unexpected outcome, of exposing the internal inconsistency in the prevalent theory of relativity. I proved that the relativity of simultaneity is not consistent with the light hypothesis. The immediate natural question is why didn't anybody spot this fatal fallacy in Einstein's STR? In fact, it was spotted, criticised and even debated face-to-face once. The occasion was Einstein's eventful visit to Paris in 1922, for the exposition of his theory of relativity at the Collège de France and at the Sorbonne [5]. In a session organised by the Philosophical society, Einstein discussed some points concerning his theories, mostly as replies to the questions and comments from prominent philosophers. There, the philosopher Henri Bergson, who had been studying the philosophy concerning time, duration and simultaneity, made some brief comments on Einstein's theory of relativity. After mentioning the need to carefully analyse the Lorentz transformations and the multitude of times in the theory, Bergson focussed his remarks on Einstein's analysis of simultaneity in the theory.

Bergson's point of contention was not whether simultaneity for one observer could become succession for another in natural phenomena, but whether it happens in Einstein's Special Relativity that relies on the light hypothesis and the equal status for all inertial observers. Bergson's thorough analysis is presented in his monograph ‘Duration and Simultaneity’, published in the same year [10]. There is even an insightful analysis of the Lorentz transformations, by the mathematically well-trained Bergson, in the book. Bergson analysed Einstein's own example, of the observers in a train and on the platform, and showed that Einstein's argument on the relativity of simultaneity in the STR was incorrect. Bergson was very careful in the mathematical and graphical presentation of the problem, taking care to remove even the slightest ambiguity in the logical argument. After presenting Einstein's example and his conclusion (quoted in the previous section), Bergson wrote,

> This passage enables us to catch on the wing an ambiguity that has been the cause of a good many misunderstandings...we must not forget that the train and the track are in a state of reciprocal motion... Let us now emit our two flashes of lightning. The points from which they set out no more belong to the ground than to the train; the waves advance independently of the motion of their source.
>
> It then becomes evident at once the two systems are interchangeable, and that exactly the same thing will occur at M′ as at the corresponding point M. If M is at the middle of AB and if it is at M we perceive a simultaneity on the track, it is at M′, the middle of B′A′, we shall perceive this same simultaneity in the train...what is simultaneity with respect to the track is simultaneity with respect to the train.

So, Bergson's rigorous logical deduction, from the postulate of the invariant relative velocity of light, showed that Einstein's analysis was flawed. The asymmetrical conclusion on simultaneity that Einstein arrived at was merely a result of the preferential choice of the frame of the platform as “really stationary” and that of the train as “really moving”, biased by the ordinary experience in the real world. Such a prejudiced choice obviously was against the basic postulates of the STR, which asserted that all inertial frames were equivalent and any one of them could be chosen as the rest frame. *If we had started with the train as the rest frame and the platform as the relatively moving frame, we would have come to the opposite conclusion*. Since both the frames are equivalent, their simultaneities are also identical. Bergson's criticism was completely misunderstood during the brief meeting, and it was dismissed as naive and incorrect. But, there is not a single written account from anybody that counters and disproves Bergson's straightforward and logically rigorous demonstration in his book ‘Duration and Simultaneity’. In fact, anybody who examines the argument in Bergson's book can verify convincingly that it is flawless.

## Interpretation and checks

Historical disagreement does not by itself establish an algebraic inconsistency. To test the physical objection, repeat the train-event calculation with a fixed chart and explicit synchronisation. Ask which measured arrival differs from special relativity, rather than which wording about time seems preferable. Retain the lecture's historical interpretation as an attributed account, and use original correspondence or debate records before assigning motives to either participant.
