## Physical question and model

Review simultaneity by separating three tasks: recording a local reception, inferring a distant emission time, and comparing two clock networks. Each task needs its own stated assumptions.

## Lecture account

Continuing with a detailed consideration of the central elements of Einstein's STR, we took up the vital problem of defining a common synchronised time for dynamics. The precise operational notion of the simultaneity of physical events at different spatial locations is important in the synchronisation of spatially separated clocks. I reiterated Einstein's assertion that judging the simultaneity of two events requires the comparison of the arrival times of the messenger signals, like light, at a single spatial location. Therefore, it is expected that different observers (O and O′) in relative motion will have different judgements on the simultaneity, or the lack of it, of two events at different spatial locations. Then I discussed the relativity of simultaneity in detail, first in the case of the Galilean propagation of light, as when an absolute frame for dynamics exists, and then in Einstein's STR, assuming an invariant relative velocity of light (the light hypothesis). For this purpose, we considered the simple situation where the two events happened when the two relatively moving observers coincided at a spatial location. If one of them receives the signals simultaneously, does the other observer also receive them simultaneously?

We concluded that *with the Galilean propagation of light, events that one observer O perceives as simultaneous will not be simultaneous for another observer O′ in relative motion, and vice versa*. I quantified the lack of simultaneity and showed that it is identical to the first order ($\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$) term in the Lorentz transformation of time.

Then we analysed how this conclusion on the relativity of simultaneity changes if we assume the light hypothesis of the STR. Of course, we arrived at *a different conclusion with the light hypothesis, that two events which are simultaneous with reference to O are also simultaneous relatively to O′*. Both observers' judgements on simultaneity are identical in their respective rest frames. This just reaffirms the central statement of Einstein's relativity, that all inertial observers are completely equivalent and that there is no physical sense in a privileged state of rest.

However, our analysis of simultaneity in this simple situation revealed a fatal internal inconsistency in the light hypothesis and in Einstein's STR. The universal consensus of simultaneity that follows from the light hypothesis contradicts both the empirical experience of the relativity of simultaneity and the logical structure of dynamics. This is because if the signals from two distant events reach one spatial location simultaneously, they couldn't be coincident in another spatial location as well. But, two observers in relative motion cannot be in the same spatial location when the signals arrive from the events that occurred (in the past) when the observers were in the same location. This fact turns out as fatal for Einstein's STR. With such an inconsistency at the very basis of the theory, the physical validity and the correctness of the STR cannot be maintained.

I recounted a well documented incident from a century ago, when the philosopher Henri Bergson had tried vainly to point out the very same inconsistency to Einstein. The details of Bergson's clear argument are contained in his monograph ‘Duration and Simultaneity’.

Then we discussed the problem of the synchronisation of spatially separated clocks. The two-way protocol that is practised, involving the exchange of electromagnetic signals (light), has its origins in the writings of Poincaré and Einstein. I pointed how the factual physical situation depends on the exact assumption regarding the propagation of light. When the light hypothesis is used, one is treating the synchronisation as a consistent convention. Since there is no way to check the actual time in a distant clock without bringing back a signal, any synchronisation gap that depends on the velocity of the inertial motion of the frame remains unknowable. From this, we found how the Lorentz's prescription for a position-dependent local time allows us to formally consider the relative velocity of light as an invariant constant, even though the physical relative velocity of light is Galilean. However, there are special physical situations, which can be arranged as real experiments, where the inconsistency of the light hypothesis is revealed.

Finally, I mentioned the protocol for the synchronisation of two clocks by the physical transport of the clocks. The discussion showed that there is a crucial factor of motional time dilation that comes from the movement of the common frame (like the Earth) in which the two clocks need to be synchronised. Then the transported clock lags behind the reference clock by a synchronisation gap in time, which exactly equals the excess delay that Galilean light takes to travel between the clocks. This creates the false impression that the relative velocity of light is an invariant quantity. All past experiments on the one-way relative velocity of light suffer from this error of assuming a true synchronisation in moving frames.

In the next chapter, we take the step of going beyond the restriction of inertial motion so that we can deal with dynamics and relativity in full generality. It turns out that this step of extending a theory of relativity also leads to a nearly perfect relativistic theory of gravity. This discovery of the *General Theory of Relativity* was Einstein's greatest achievement. The crucial insight that paved the way for these developments came from a simple yet non-intuitive fact that was known for nearly 400 years: that the gravitational acceleration of bodies that are freely falling in a gravitational field does not depend on any property of the bodies, including the quantity of their mass. Since this fact is at the core of the relation between dynamics, gravity, and the matter-filled Universe, we will examine the physics of free fall in a gravitational field next, before we take up a brief study of Einstein's GTR.

We have in fact completed a *milestone chapter* in this book. Independent of what follows regarding a new consistent theory of relativity, we have already shown the fatal inconsistency of the light hypothesis and Einstein's STR; they cannot any more serve as the basis for relativity and dynamics. The severity of this situation gets strengthened when we acknowledge the fact that there is indeed an absolute reference for the description of dynamics, with the isotropic CMBR as the reference for inertial motion and also as an absolute universal time. Thus, even before we examine Einstein's GTR as a theory of gravity, we have to consider the STR as an already refuted theory, on the basis of what was discussed in this chapter. Of course, the direct experimental proof for the Galilean propagation of light will be discussed in [[11.04 The Experiment on the One-Way Relative Velocity of Light|Chapter 11]]. The construction of the full Lorentz transformations from the factual Galilean propagation of light and its consistency with the PoR severs the attributed association between the tested relativistic effects and the STR. Since we understand the exact physical structure of the Lorentz transformations now, we will be able to see clearly which features of the fine theory of General Relativity remain valid and what kind of modification is required to make the theory consistent with the factual existence of an absolute frame.

## Interpretation and checks

A useful exercise is to predict arrival order from simultaneous platform flashes for a moving receiver, then describe the same events in the receiver's inertial frame. The second description changes the emission times as well as the detector worldline. Recovering the same reception events is the consistency test. The author's proposed universal time adds a physical interpretation that must yield a distinct observable to be tested.
