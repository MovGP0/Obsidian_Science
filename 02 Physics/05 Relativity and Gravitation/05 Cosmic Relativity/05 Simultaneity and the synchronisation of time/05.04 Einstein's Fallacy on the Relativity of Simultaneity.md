> The author argues that Einstein's train analysis conflicts with the light postulate and compares it with a proposed alternative analysis.

## Physical question and model

Does the train example contradict invariant light speed? Place both flashes, both detectors, and their worldlines in one inertial chart first. Use invariant light propagation to find where each ray meets each moving detector. Then transform the complete event coordinates to the train chart.

## Lecture account

In his concrete example to discuss simultaneity of events, Einstein had considered two observers, O on a railway platform and O′ in a moving train. We will look at the exact steps in his analysis after we examine his actual conclusion [6], which was surprisingly the following:

> We thus arrive at the important result: Events which are simultaneous with reference to the embankment are not simultaneous with respect to the train, and *vice versa* (relativity of simultaneity).

How did he arrive at this patently Galilean conclusion after assuming the light hypothesis? There must be a slip in his logic because the conclusion stated is clearly that of the situation when the messenger waves are Galilean, with their relative velocity dependent on the velocity of the observer (as we established in the previous two sections). A reading of the logical steps in his analysis reveals the precise nature of this slip:

> When we say that the lightning strokes A and B are simultaneous with respect to the embankment, we mean: the rays of light emitted at the places A and B, where the lightning occurs, meet each other at the mid-point M of the length A–B of the embankment. But the events A and B also correspond to positions A and B on the train. Let M′ be the mid-point of the distance A–B on the travelling train. ... Now in reality (considered with reference to the railway embankment) he is hastening towards the beam of light coming from B, whilst he is riding on ahead of the beam of light coming from A. Hence the observer will see the beam of light emitted from B earlier than he will see that emitted from A. Observers who take the railway train as their reference-body must therefore come to the conclusion that the lightning flash B took place earlier than the lightning flash A. We thus arrive at the important result: Events which are simultaneous with reference to the embankment are not simultaneous with respect to the train, and *vice versa* (relativity of simultaneity).

It is painfully clear that Einstein forgot his own postulate about the relative velocity of light and the equivalence of all inertial frames while carrying out his analysis of simultaneity. The observer O′ in the train is at rest in his own frame, according to the STR; the platform and the rest of the world are moving relatively. The light signals that were emitted when O′ was at the midpoint M′ of the events travel towards O′ at the equal relative speeds $\mechanical{c}$, and they will necessarily reach O′ simultaneously. Einstein errs within his own theory when he writes “in reality he is hastening towards the beam of light coming from B”. *The observer is at rest in his frame and the source of light is coming towards him, but that does not affect the relative speed of light, or of any wave*. It is clear that Einstein would have been saved from this fatal error of inadvertent preference for the embankment as “really stationary” if he had considered two trains in relative motion, instead of a train and a platform (Fig. 5.4).

To recapitulate, if we accept the reality of a master absolute frame and the Galilean nature of the propagation of light, the experience of the simultaneity of events is relative and not identical for two observers in relative motion. This is exactly what is experienced in the empirical world as well. However, if we assume that light propagates with the same invariant speed relative to all inertial observers, the signals from two events, that happened when the two relatively moving observers coincided, will be received by both observers with the identical temporal delay, in their respective rest frames. Thus, if the events are simultaneous for one observer, they will be simultaneous for the second one also. But this conclusion is not consistent with what is experienced in the real physical situation. We can firmly conclude that Einstein's light hypothesis is in conflict with the notion of simultaneity and clock synchronisation in the STR. Einstein's Special Theory of Relativity is clearly inconsistent, as per the analysis of its creator himself.

*What Einstein describes is not what the observer in the train experiences, but what the person on the platform, who considers O′ as ‘really’ moving, confers on the moving observer as his experience*. On the one hand, the experience of simultaneity for both observers should be identical if the light hypothesis is used. But on the other hand, each observer is moving relative to the other towards one of the sources, and hence should receive that signal earlier. However, the same pair of signals cannot be both simultaneous and successive. This proves that the light hypothesis is inconsistent [7].

> [!note]
> In the STR, all inertial observers are equivalent and each can claim a state of rest. Once the constancy of the relative velocity of light is assumed, the signals from two equidistant events will reach an inertial observer simultaneously. But this universality of simultaneity for every inertial observer is inconsistent for a set of such observers in relative motion. Einstein's light hypothesis is inconsistent with the relativity of simultaneity.

Our transparent analysis has ended in a conclusion that revealed an internal inconsistency in the central pillar of modern theoretical physics. This was most unexpected. There have been occasions when the result of a crucial experiment invalidates a fundamental theory or paradigm. But, it is rare that an internal inconsistency, revealed as the conflict between the defining fundamental postulate of the theory and a simple factual situation of physical nature, demands the replacement of the central theory of physics that has been in use for over a century.

(1) Two opposite light pulses are sent when relatively moving observers O and O′ coincide. (2) Under invariant relative light speed, O at the room midpoint claims simultaneous arrival at the comoving walls; O′ claims successive arrivals because the walls move as the pulses propagate. The source argues that neither observer can directly check these distant arrival claims.]]

We discussed a situation where two signals arrive from A and B to a common location M. By comparing with a local clock (or using one signal as a temporal reference for the other) one can reach an unambiguous and verifiable decision about simultaneity. It is instructive to be aware of a common fallacy committed in the discussion of the relativity of simultaneity. One can see in many writings the discussion of *the simultaneity of the arrival of two signals sent out from a point M, at two separate points A and B* (Fig. 5.5). This example perhaps originated in an exposition of the relativity of simultaneity in a book of popular exposition written in 1938 by Einstein and Infeld [8]. A moment's thought will alert that such a discussion is physically meaningless because no observer can ascertain when exactly a signal reaches a spatially distant point! One can only make untestable and unverifiable deductions based on the assumptions made about the propagation of the signals. For example, consider the case where light is sent to the points A and B on the opposite walls of a room from their midpoint M. The room with an observer O at M is moving in the direction $\overrightarrow{BA}$ relative to another observer O′. *If we assume that the relative velocity of light is invariant*, the same $\mechanical{c}$ for all observers, then O will conclude that the light pulses will reach the walls A and B simultaneously. But, relative to O′, the walls are in motion in the direction $\overrightarrow{BA}$ as the pulses of light propagate. Then O′ will deduce that the signal will reach B earlier than it reaches A. Therefore, simultaneity (of the two events of the light reaching the points A and B) for one observer becomes succession for another when there is relative motion. This apparently proves the revolutionary idea of the relativity of simultaneity in the STR. ‘Revolutionary’ because *the events are in succession according to both observers in pre-STR physics*; light reaches B earlier than it reaches A according to both O and O′. For O′, it is because the walls are moving as light propagates. For O, the lack of simultaneity is because the relative velocity of light is lower than $\mechanical{c}$ in the direction of A and larger in the direction of B. Quoting from the book by Einstein and Infeld, “Comparing the predictions of our two observers, we find a most astonishing result which flatly contradicts the apparently well-founded concepts of classical physics. Two events, i.e., the two light beams reaching the two walls, are simultaneous for the observer on the inside, but not for the observer on the outside.”

But, how does one verify that this is what actually happens? In fact, one cannot. The situation that we just discussed is *untestable and unverifiable*. The conclusions vary with the assumptions made about the propagation of the signals. Therefore, such arguments are in the realm of faith and prejudice, and do not reflect on any physical reality. The lack of logic and physics in such arguments in the literature on relativity is appalling.

The only physical situation with the facility to verify simultaneity is what we discussed earlier, when the signals reach to a common location M from the distant points A and B.

## Interpretation and checks

For flashes simultaneous on the platform, the Lorentz time rule assigns a nonzero train-frame emission-time difference when their platform positions differ. In the train frame the detector may be stationary and both rays move at light speed, yet unequal emission times give unequal arrivals. No contradiction follows. The source's argument treats a midpoint or simultaneity assignment from one frame as if it were unchanged in the other. Use [the operational definition of now](https://www.einstein-online.info/en/spotlight/now/) to check that distinction. The title records the source's claim, not an established fallacy in special relativity.
