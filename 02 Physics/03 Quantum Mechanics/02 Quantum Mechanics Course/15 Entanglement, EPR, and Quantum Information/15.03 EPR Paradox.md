---
title: "EPR Paradox"
---

> [!TLDR]
> The EPR argument used perfect correlations to question the completeness of quantum mechanics. Bohm's version uses spin measurements on a singlet pair.

Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen published their argument in 1935. It uses three premises.

1. **Realism.** If a physical quantity can be predicted with certainty without disturbing the system, it corresponds to an element of reality.
2. **Locality.** An action in one region cannot instantly influence an event in a spacelike separated region.
3. **Completeness.** The theory must represent every element of reality.

EPR considered a pair with correlated positions and momenta. They concluded that quantum mechanics must be incomplete because it does not assign simultaneous definite values to noncommuting observables.

Bohm reformulated the argument for two spin-$1/2$ particles in the singlet state.

$$
\lvert\signal{\Psi}^-\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\lvert\uparrow\downarrow\rangle-\lvert\downarrow\uparrow\rangle).
$$

If Alice measures the spin component of her electron along $\mathbf a$ and obtains $+\mechanical{\hbar}/2$, Bob will obtain $-\mechanical{\hbar}/2$ along the same direction, regardless of the separation. With parallel detector axes, the joint outcomes $(+,+),(+,-),(-,+),(-,-)$ have probabilities $(0,1/2,1/2,0)$.

Alice can predict Bob's result with certainty. By the EPR argument, if her measurement does not disturb his system, Bob's spin component along $\mathbf a$ corresponds to an element of reality. The same reasoning applies to a different direction $\mathbf a'$. This would give simultaneous elements of reality for noncommuting spin components, in conflict with the quantum description.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\mathbf a,\mathbf a'$ | $1$ | Measurement axes | Unit vectors along the two possible directions. |
| $\mechanical{\hbar}$ | $\unit{J\,s}$ | Reduced Planck constant | The spin component along each axis has outcomes $\pm\mechanical{\hbar}/2$. |
