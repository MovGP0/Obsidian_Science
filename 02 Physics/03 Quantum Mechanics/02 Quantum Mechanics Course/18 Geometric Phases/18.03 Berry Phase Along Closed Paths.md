---
title: "Berry Phase Along Closed Paths"
---

> [!TLDR]
> The Berry phase of a closed path is the circulation of the Berry connection. A change of eigenstate gauge leaves it invariant modulo $2\pi$.

Let $C$ be a closed path with $R(T)=R(0)$, as in Fig. 18.1 of [[18.02 Dynamical and Geometric Phases]]. In a gauge in which the eigenstate is single valued along the loop, the geometric phase for one traversal is

$$
\gamma_n(C)=\oint_C\mathbf A_n(R)\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf R.
$$

This is the **Berry phase**.

> [!theorem] Proposition 18.1 · Gauge invariance of the Berry phase
> For a smooth, single-valued change of phase $e^{i\beta(R)}$ along $C$, the Berry phase is invariant modulo $2\pi$.

The transformed connection differs by $-\nabla_R\beta$. Its integral along $C$ vanishes if $\beta$ itself is single valued. For an angle variable with winding, the integral is an integer multiple of $2\pi$.

> [!definition] Definition 18.2 · Berry curvature
> In a three-dimensional parameter space, the Berry curvature is
>
> $$
> \mathbf F_n(R)=\nabla_R\times\mathbf A_n(R).
> $$
>
> For an oriented smooth surface $S$ bounded by $C$, with a smooth gauge on $S$, Stokes' theorem gives
>
> $$
> \gamma_n(C)=\int_S\mathbf F_n(R)\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf S.
> $$

The curl of the gauge term is zero, so the curvature is gauge invariant. It gives the density of geometric flux in parameter space. Degeneracies can be singular points of the curvature. In geometric terms, the phase is the holonomy of the eigenstate. Parallel transport around a loop gives a phase shift equal to the curvature flux through the enclosed surface modulo $2\pi$.

For components $\mu,\nu$ of the parameter coordinates, insert the resolution of identity in the instantaneous basis and use the derivative formula from perturbation theory. With $\partial_\mu=\partial/\partial R_\mu$,

$$
F_{n,\mu\nu}(R)=i\sum_{m\ne n}
\frac{
\langle n\rvert\partial_\mu\energy{\hat H}\lvert m\rangle
\langle m\rvert\partial_\nu\energy{\hat H}\lvert n\rangle
-(\mu\leftrightarrow\nu)
}{
(\energy{E}_n-\energy{E}_m)^2
}.
\tag{18.3}
$$

The inverse squared energy gap shows why nearby coupled levels can give a large contribution to the curvature and the phase.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\gamma_n(C)$ | $\unit{rad}$ | Berry phase | The geometric phase around $C$, defined modulo $2\pi$. |
| $\mathbf A_n$ | Inverse parameter unit | Berry connection | Its circulation along $C$ gives the phase. |
| $F_{n,\mu\nu}$ | Inverse product of parameter units | Berry curvature component | The geometric flux density in the plane of parameters $R_\mu,R_\nu$. |
| $\energy{E}_n,\energy{E}_m$ | $\unit{J}$ | Instantaneous energies | The energy difference enters the curvature denominator. |
