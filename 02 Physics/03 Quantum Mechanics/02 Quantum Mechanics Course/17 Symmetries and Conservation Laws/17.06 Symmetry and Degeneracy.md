---
title: "Symmetry and Degeneracy"
---

> [!TLDR]
> A symmetry maps an energy eigenstate to another state of the same energy. If those states are linearly independent, the energy is degenerate.

> [!theorem] Theorem 17.2 · Degeneracy principle
> Let $\hat U$ be a symmetry of $\energy{\hat H}$ and $\lvert\signal{\psi}\rangle$ an eigenstate of $\energy{\hat H}$ with energy $\energy E$. Then $\hat U\lvert\signal{\psi}\rangle$ is an eigenstate of the same energy. If the two states are linearly independent, the level $\energy E$ is degenerate.

## Examples

- In a central potential, $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ gives orbital multiplets with $2\ell+1$ states at fixed $\ell$.
- For the Coulomb Hamiltonian of hydrogen, the bound orbital levels have an $\mathrm{SO}(4)$ symmetry associated with the Runge–Lenz vector. The additional degeneracy gives $n^2$ states at fixed $n$, without spin.
- In a one-dimensional even potential, parity labels the nondegenerate bound states as even or odd. In three dimensions, parity and spatial rotations can both label states.
- Exchange symmetry restricts the allowed states of identical particles according to their statistics.

## Symmetry breaking

A perturbation that breaks a symmetry can lift all or part of a degeneracy.

- A magnetic field $\magnetic{\mathbf B}$ reduces full rotational symmetry to rotations about the field. The Zeeman effect can lift the degeneracy in $m_j$.
- A uniform electric field breaks parity and full rotational symmetry and gives Stark shifts.
- Spin–orbit coupling breaks independent rotations of $\mechanical{\mathbf L}$ and $\mechanical{\mathbf S}$, while preserving rotations generated by $\mechanical{\mathbf J}=\mechanical{\mathbf L}+\mechanical{\mathbf S}$.
