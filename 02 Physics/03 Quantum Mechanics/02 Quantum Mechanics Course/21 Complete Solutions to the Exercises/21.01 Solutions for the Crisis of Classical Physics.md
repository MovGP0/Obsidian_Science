> [!TLDR]
> Work through the experiments and estimates that show where classical physics fails and quantum effects become important.

Problems are in [[01.09 The Crisis of Classical Physics Exercises]].

## Exercise 1 · Photon energies for visible light and X-rays

Use $\energy E=hc/\lambda$ with $hc\simeq\quantity{1240}{eV\,nm}$. For visible light,

$$
\energy E(\lambda=\quantity{550}{nm})\simeq\frac{1240}{550}\unit{eV}\simeq\quantity{2.25}{eV}.
$$

For an X-ray of wavelength $\quantity{0.1}{nm}$, $\energy E=(1240/0.1)\unit{eV}=\quantity{12400}{eV}\simeq\quantity{12.4}{keV}$. The energy ratio is about $5500$, the inverse ratio of the corresponding wavelengths.

## Exercise 2 · Photoelectric threshold and kinetic energy

The threshold frequency is $\nu_0=\energy W/h=\quantity{4.5}{eV}/\quantity{4.136\times10^{-15}}{eV\,s}\simeq\quantity{1.09\times10^{15}}{Hz}$. At $\lambda=\quantity{200}{nm}$, the photon energy is $h\nu=(1240/200)\unit{eV}=\quantity{6.2}{eV}$. Therefore $\energy K_{\max}=h\nu-\energy W=\quantity{1.7}{eV}$.

## Exercise 3 · De Broglie wavelengths

Use $\lambda=h/\mechanical p$ and $\mechanical p=\sqrt{2\mechanical m\energy K}$. For an electron with $\mechanical m_ec^2=\quantity{0.511}{MeV}$ at $\energy K=\quantity{1}{eV}$,

$$
\lambda_e=\frac{hc}{\sqrt{2\mechanical m_ec^2\energy K}}
=\frac{1240}{\sqrt{2(5.11\times10^5)(1)}}\unit{nm}\simeq\quantity{1.23}{nm}.
$$

For a proton at the same kinetic energy with $\mechanical m_pc^2\simeq\quantity{938}{MeV}$, $\lambda_p\simeq\quantity{2.87\times10^{-11}}{m}$. For a tennis ball of mass $\quantity{0.05}{kg}$ at speed $\quantity{30}{m\,s^{-1}}$, $\lambda=h/(\mechanical m v)\simeq\quantity{4.4\times10^{-34}}{m}$.

## Exercise 4 · Hydrogen Hα line

The $n=3\to2$ transition emits energy $\Delta\energy E=13.6(1/4-1/9)\unit{eV}\simeq\quantity{1.89}{eV}$. Then $\nu=\Delta\energy E/h\simeq\quantity{4.57\times10^{14}}{Hz}$ and $\lambda=hc/\Delta\energy E\simeq\quantity{656}{nm}$. This is the red Balmer line.

## Exercise 5 · Bohr radius and ground-state energy

Equate the centripetal and Coulomb forces. Then $\mechanical m_ev^2=\electric e^2/(4\pi\electric\varepsilon_0 r)$. The quantization rule $\mechanical m_evr=n\mechanical{\hbar}$ gives $v=n\mechanical{\hbar}/(\mechanical m_e r)$. Substitution gives

$$
r_n=\frac{4\pi\electric\varepsilon_0\mechanical{\hbar}^2}{\mechanical m_e\electric e^2}n^2=a_0n^2.
$$

The total energy is $\energy E=\mechanical m_ev^2/2-\electric e^2/(4\pi\electric\varepsilon_0 r)=-\electric e^2/(8\pi\electric\varepsilon_0 r)$. Thus

$$
\energy E_n=-\frac{\mechanical m_e\electric e^4}{2(4\pi\electric\varepsilon_0)^2\mechanical{\hbar}^2 n^2}
=-\frac{\quantity{13.6}{eV}}{n^2}.
$$

## Exercise 6 · Compton scattering at 90°

At $\theta=90^\circ$, the shift is $\Delta\lambda=\lambda_C(1-\cos\theta)=\lambda_C=\quantity{2.43\times10^{-12}}{m}$. The initial wavelength at $\energy E_i=\quantity{50}{keV}$ is $\lambda_i=hc/\energy E_i=\quantity{2.48\times10^{-2}}{nm}$. After scattering, $\lambda_f=\quantity{2.723\times10^{-2}}{nm}$. Thus $\energy E_f=hc/\lambda_f\simeq\quantity{45.5}{keV}$ and the recoil electron carries $\energy K=\energy E_i-\energy E_f\simeq\quantity{4.5}{keV}$.

## Exercise 7 · Classical collapse time

For an electron on a circular orbit of radius $a_0$, the acceleration is $a=v^2/a_0=\electric e^2/(4\pi\electric\varepsilon_0\mechanical m_e a_0^2)$. The Larmor power is

$$
\energy P=\frac{\electric e^2 a^2}{6\pi\electric\varepsilon_0 c^3}.
$$

With $a\simeq\quantity{9\times10^{22}}{m\,s^{-2}}$, the power is about $\quantity{5\times10^{-8}}{W}$. Dividing the binding energy $\lvert\energy E_1\rvert\simeq\quantity{2.2\times10^{-18}}{J}$ by this initial power gives $\tau_{\mathrm{cl}}\sim\quantity{4\times10^{-11}}{s}$. Integrating the shrinking circular orbit gives $\tau_{\mathrm{cl}}\simeq a_0^3/(4r_e^2c)\simeq\quantity{1.6\times10^{-11}}{s}$, where $r_e$ is the classical electron radius. The source compares this time with an observed radiative time scale of about $\quantity{10^{-8}}{s}$ for excited states. A classical orbit collapses. A quantum atom has a stable ground state.

## Exercise 8 · Slit diffraction

With a slit width $a$, estimate $\Delta y\simeq a$ and $\Delta\mechanical p_y\sim\mechanical{\hbar}/a$. The small-angle half-divergence is

$$
\Delta\theta\simeq\frac{\Delta\mechanical p_y}{\mechanical p}
\simeq\frac{\mechanical{\hbar}}{\mechanical p a}
\simeq\frac{1.055\times10^{-34}}{(9.11\times10^{-31})(10^6)(10^{-6})}\unit{rad}
\simeq\quantity{1.16\times10^{-4}}{rad}.
$$

## Exercise 9 · Wien's law in wavelength form

Use $\nu=c/\lambda$ and the magnitude of the Jacobian $\lvert\mathrm d\nu/\mathrm d\lambda\rvert=c/\lambda^2$ to convert the frequency density into the wavelength density.

$$
u(\lambda,T)=\frac{8\pi hc}{\lambda^5}\frac{1}{e^{hc/(\lambda k_B T)}-1}.
$$

Set the derivative with respect to $\lambda$ to zero. With $y=hc/(\lambda_{\max}k_BT)$, the condition is $(5-y)e^y=5$. The nonzero root is $y\simeq4.965$, so $\lambda_{\max}T=hc/(4.965 k_B)\simeq\quantity{2.90\times10^{-3}}{m\,K}$.

The frequency maximum has $h\nu_{\max}/(k_BT)\simeq2.82$. Thus $\nu_{\max}\lambda_{\max}=2.82c/4.965\simeq0.568c$. The maxima of the two spectral densities occur at different points because the densities use different frequency and wavelength intervals.

## Exercise 10 · Slit width for electron diffraction

Use $a\sin\theta_1=\lambda$ with $\theta_1=3^\circ$ and $\lambda=\quantity{0.15}{nm}$. Then $a=\lambda/\sin3^\circ\simeq0.15\unit{nm}/0.0523\simeq\quantity{2.87}{nm}$.

## Exercise 11 · Planck integral and Stefan–Boltzmann law

Integrate the energy density in frequency. With $x=h\nu/(k_BT)$ and $\mathrm d\nu=(k_BT/h)\,\mathrm dx$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
u(T)
&=\int_0^\infty\frac{8\pi h\nu^3}{c^3}\frac{\mathrm d\nu}{e^{h\nu/(k_BT)}-1}\\
&=\frac{8\pi(k_BT)^4}{h^3c^3}\int_0^\infty\frac{x^3\,\mathrm dx}{e^x-1}
=\frac{8\pi^5(k_BT)^4}{15h^3c^3}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The radiated flux is $cu(T)/4=\sigma T^4$, with $\sigma=\pi^2k_B^4/(60\mechanical{\hbar}^3c^2)$.

> [!note] Differential in the source
> The source omits $T$ in the expression for $\mathrm d\nu$ after substitution. Its integral uses the full factor $k_BT/h$.
