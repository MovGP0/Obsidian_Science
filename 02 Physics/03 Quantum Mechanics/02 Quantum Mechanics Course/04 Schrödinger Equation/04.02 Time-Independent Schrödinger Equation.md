---
title: "Time-Independent Schrödinger Equation"
---

> [!TLDR]
> The time-independent Schrödinger equation is the eigenvalue equation for the Hamiltonian.

For a time-independent Hamiltonian, solve the spectral problem

$$
\energy{\hat H}\lvert E\rangle=\energy{E}\lvert E\rangle.
$$

In one-dimensional position space, this becomes the **time-independent Schrödinger equation**.

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mechanical{m}}\signal{\psi}''(\mechanical{x})+
\energy{V}(\mechanical{x})\signal{\psi}(\mechanical{x})=
\energy{E}\signal{\psi}(\mechanical{x}).
\tag{4.3}
$$

The solution satisfies boundary conditions set by the potential.

- **Bound states.** The energy is usually discrete. The wave function is square-integrable, $\int\lvert\signal{\psi}\rvert^2\,d\mechanical{x}<\infty$, and typically tends to zero as $\lvert\mechanical{x}\rvert\to\infty$.
- **Scattering states.** The energy is in a continuum. In the usual scattering examples the generalized eigenfunctions are bounded but not square-integrable. Their normalization uses distributions.

## Stationary states

A solution $\signal{\psi}_E(\mechanical{x})$ gives $\signal{\psi}(\mechanical{x},t)=\signal{\psi}_E(\mechanical{x})e^{-i\energy{E}t/\hbar}$ by Eq. (4.2), with initial time zero. The probability density remains $\lvert\signal{\psi}_E(\mechanical{x})\rvert^2$, and the expectation of any time-independent observable is constant.

## Example. Superposition of stationary states

For $\lvert\psi(0)\rangle=(\lvert E_1\rangle+\lvert E_2\rangle)/\sqrt2$, the evolved state is

$$
\lvert\psi(t)\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt2}
\left(e^{-i\energy{E}_1t/\hbar}\lvert E_1\rangle+
e^{-i\energy{E}_2t/\hbar}\lvert E_2\rangle\right).
$$

Its position density is

$$
\lvert\signal{\psi}(\mechanical{x},t)\rvert^2=\frac12
\left[\lvert\signal{\psi}_1(\mechanical{x})\rvert^2+
\lvert\signal{\psi}_2(\mechanical{x})\rvert^2+
2\operatorname{Re}\left(\signal{\psi}_1^*\signal{\psi}_2e^{-i(\energy{E}_2-\energy{E}_1)t/\hbar}\right)\right].
$$

The interference term oscillates with angular frequency $\signal{\omega}_{21}=(\energy{E}_2-\energy{E}_1)/\hbar$ for $\energy{E}_2>\energy{E}_1$. A superposition of different energy levels is generally nonstationary.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\energy{\hat H},\energy{V},\energy{E},\energy{E}_i$ | $\unit{J}$ | Hamiltonian and energies | The energy operator, potential energy, and stationary levels. |
| $\mechanical{m}$ | $\unit{kg}$ | Mass | The particle mass. |
| $\mechanical{x}$ | $\unit{m}$ | Position | The spatial coordinate in one dimension. |
| $\signal{\psi},\signal{\psi}_E,\signal{\psi}_i$ | — | Position wave functions | Bound states normalized over position have unit $\unit{m^{-1/2}}$. Scattering normalization depends on the spectral variable. |
| $t$ | $\unit{s}$ | Time | The time since preparation. |
| $\hbar$ | $\unit{J\,s}$ | Reduced Planck constant | The scale relating energy to the rate of phase change. |
| $\signal{\omega}_{21}$ | $\unit{rad\,s^{-1}}$ | Beat angular frequency | The energy gap divided by the reduced Planck constant. |
