> [!TLDR]
> The Born rule gives measurement probabilities from the squared norm of a state's projection onto the corresponding eigenspace.

> [!definition] Definition 2.3 | Postulate III. Measurement with a Nondegenerate Spectrum
> Let $\hat A$ have discrete, nondegenerate eigenvalues $\{a_n\}$ with normalized eigenstates $\{\lvert a_n\rangle\}$. A measurement of $A$ on the normalized state $\lvert\psi\rangle$ yields one of the values $a_n$ with probability
>
> $$
> P(a_n\mid\psi)=\lvert\langle a_n\mid\psi\rangle\rvert^2.
> $$
>
> The complex number $\langle a_n\mid\psi\rangle$ is the **probability amplitude** for outcome $a_n$.

This is the **Born rule** (1926). For $\lvert\psi\rangle=\sum_n c_n\lvert a_n\rangle$, the probabilities are $P_n=\lvert c_n\rvert^2$. The state normalization gives $\sum_n\lvert c_n\rvert^2=1$, so the probabilities form a normalized distribution.

## Degenerate eigenvalues

If $a$ is an eigenvalue of multiplicity $d$, its eigenspace $\mathcal H_a\subset\mathcal H$ has dimension $d$. Let $\hat P_a$ be the orthogonal projector onto that eigenspace. The probability of outcome $a$ is

$$
P(a\mid\psi)=\langle\psi\mid\hat P_a\mid\psi\rangle
=\bigl\lVert\hat P_a\lvert\psi\rangle\bigr\rVert^2.
$$

## Continuous spectrum

For an observable with a continuous spectrum, such as $\mechanical{\hat x}$, the generalized eigenstates $\lvert a\rangle$ are distributions. They cannot be normalized as ordinary Hilbert-space vectors. The Born rule gives a **probability density**. The probability in the infinitesimal interval $[a,a+da]$ is

$$
p(a\mid\psi)\,da=\lvert\langle a\mid\psi\rangle\rvert^2\,da.
$$

In the position representation, $\lvert\signal{\psi}(\mechanical{x})\rvert^2$ is the probability density for finding the particle at $\mechanical{x}$.

## Physical significance

The state supplies the probabilities for a measurement, while the individual outcome is stochastic. Between measurements, the state evolves deterministically under Postulate V. In general, the theory assigns probabilities to possible values before measurement. Bell's and Kochen–Specker's theorems constrain models that assign pre-existing values subject to locality or noncontextuality, respectively.
