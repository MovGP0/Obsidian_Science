> [!TLDR]
> Bragg reflection mixes degenerate plane waves at a zone boundary. The first gap is twice the magnitude of the corresponding Fourier component of the potential.

At $k=\pi/a$, the wavelength is $\lambda=2a$. This satisfies the condition for coherent Bragg reflection from the lattice. The incident wave $e^{ikx}$ and the reflected wave $e^{-ikx}$ combine into two standing waves. For an inversion-symmetric lattice with a site at the origin, these can be chosen with definite parity. One has peaks at the sites, and the other has peaks between sites. An attractive ionic potential lowers the energy of the state concentrated at the sites relative to the other state. Repulsive delta barriers reverse their order. Their energy separation opens the gap.

For nearly free electrons, expand the potential in reciprocal-lattice components.

$$
\energy V(x)=\sum_G\energy V_G e^{iGx},\qquad G=\frac{2\pi n}{a},\quad n\in\mathbb Z.
$$

Degenerate perturbation theory at the first zone boundary gives the leading gap

$$
\energy\Delta_1=2\lvert\energy V_{2\pi/a}\rvert.
$$

Thus the first Fourier component sets the first gap. Figure 19.2 shows the first few bands in the reduced-zone scheme.

## Physical meaning

The Pauli principle allows at most two electrons per band and Bloch wave number when the two spin states are degenerate. If the number of electrons per cell fills an integer number of bands and the next gap is large relative to thermal energy, the material is a band insulator. The filled bands have no nearby empty states for low-energy excitations. Bragg scattering opens band gaps that can produce this behavior in a crystal. The gap size helps distinguish conventional insulators from semiconductors.
