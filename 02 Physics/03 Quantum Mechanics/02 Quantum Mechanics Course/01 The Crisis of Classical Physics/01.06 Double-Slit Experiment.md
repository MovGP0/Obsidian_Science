title: "Double-Slit Experiment"

> [!TLDR]
> The double-slit experiment shows that individual detections can build up an interference pattern.

In the double-slit experiment, a source sends particles toward a wall with two slits. The particles can be electrons, photons, neutrons, or molecules. Experiments have used C₆₀ and much larger molecules. A detector beyond the wall counts the arriving quanta. Figure 1.3 shows the arrangement.

![[quantum-mechanics-fig-1-3-double-slit.svg|**Fig. 1.3** Source S sends quanta toward slits 1 and 2 in an opaque wall. The curve at the detector shows interference fringes. Adding independent particle counts from the two slits would not produce this pattern.]]

**High flux.** The count distribution shows interference fringes. The wave contributions from the two slits interfere.

**Low flux.** Reduce the flux so that the source emits the particles one at a time. Each particle gives a localized detection event. After many events, the interference pattern appears again.

**Which slit?** If a detector identifies the slit for each particle, the fringes disappear and the counts follow the sum of the two single-slit distributions. A measurement that records which path the particle took removes the interference.

## Physical significance

- Individual quanta arrive as localized events.
- Arrival probabilities follow the intensity of a wave.
- When the paths remain coherent, the amplitudes add as $\signal{\psi}_{\mathrm{tot}}=\signal{\psi}_1+\signal{\psi}_2$, so $P\propto\lvert\signal{\psi}_1+\signal{\psi}_2\rvert^2$.
- If a measurement gives complete path information, the alternatives contribute probabilities without the interference term. The fringes disappear.
