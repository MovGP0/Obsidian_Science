---
title: "Wave–Particle Duality and the de Broglie Hypothesis"
---

> [!TLDR]
> A particle of momentum magnitude $p$ has a de Broglie wavelength $h/p$.

In his 1924 doctoral thesis, Louis de Broglie proposed that a material particle with momentum magnitude $\mechanical{p}$ has an associated wave with wavelength

$$
\signal{\lambda}=\frac{h}{\mechanical{p}}.
$$

The proposal extends the relation between waves and particles to matter. Light already showed wave behavior and particle momentum $\mechanical{p}=h/\signal{\lambda}$. De Broglie applied the same relation to material particles.

Apply the relation to an electron and require a standing wave around the circumference of a Bohr orbit, $2\pi\mechanical{r}=n\signal{\lambda}$. This gives $\mechanical{L}=\mechanical{m}_e\mechanical{v}\mechanical{r}=n\hbar$. The Bohr quantization rule can then be read as a resonance condition for the matter wave.

Davisson and Germer confirmed the hypothesis in 1927 in the United States. Thomson confirmed it independently in Scotland. Electrons accelerated through a voltage $\electric{V}$ and directed at a crystal produced diffraction patterns similar to those of X-rays. The measured spacing agreed with $\signal{\lambda}=h/\mechanical{p}=h/\sqrt{2\mechanical{m}_e\electric{e}\electric{V}}$ in the nonrelativistic regime.

## Example. Electron and macroscopic wavelengths

For an electron accelerated through $\electric{V}=\quantity{100}{V}$, $\mechanical{p}=\sqrt{2\mechanical{m}_e\electric{e}\electric{V}}$, so

$$
\signal{\lambda}=\frac{h}{\mechanical{p}}
\simeq\frac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{\sqrt{2\cdot9.11\times10^{-31}\cdot1.6\times10^{-17}}}\,\unit{m}
\simeq\quantity{1.23\times10^{-10}}{m}.
$$

This wavelength is comparable to the spacing of atoms in a crystal, so electron diffraction is observable. For an object of mass $\quantity{1}{g}$ and speed $\quantity{1}{m\,s^{-1}}$, the de Broglie wavelength is about $\quantity{6.6\times10^{-31}}{m}$. This wavelength is far below accessible probe scales and helps explain why matter-wave effects do not appear in ordinary experience.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\signal{\lambda}$ | $\unit{m}$ | de Broglie wavelength | The wavelength of a particle with the specified momentum. |
| $\mechanical{p}$ | $\unit{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$ | Momentum | The magnitude of the particle momentum. |
| $h,\hbar$ | $\unit{J\,s}$ | Planck constants | The Planck constant and its reduced form. |
| $\mechanical{r}$ | $\unit{m}$ | Orbital radius | The radius of a circular Bohr orbit. |
| $\mechanical{L}$ | $\unit{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}}$ | Orbital angular momentum | The magnitude for a circular Bohr orbit. |
| $\mechanical{m}_e$ | $\unit{kg}$ | Electron mass | The mass used in the electron diffraction calculation. |
| $\mechanical{v}$ | $\unit{m\,s^{-1}}$ | Speed | The speed of the electron in the Bohr orbit. |
| $\electric{V}$ | $\unit{V}$ | Accelerating voltage | The potential difference that accelerates the electrons. |
| $\electric{e}$ | $\unit{C}$ | Elementary charge | The magnitude of the electron charge. |
| — | $\unit{g}$ | Macroscopic mass | The mass in the wavelength comparison. |
| — | $\unit{m\,s^{-1}}$ | Macroscopic speed | The speed in the wavelength comparison. |
