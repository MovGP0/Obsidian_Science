---
title: "Principle of Inertia"
---

> [!TLDR]
> In an inertial frame, a body with zero net force keeps a constant velocity.

> [!definition] Definition 2.1 — Newton's First Law (Principle of Inertia)
> There is a class of reference frames called **inertial frames**. In these frames, a body keeps its state of motion when no force acts on it or when the forces cancel. It stays at rest or continues at constant velocity along a straight line.

**Meaning.** The principle of inertia is an axiom that identifies the reference frames in which the laws apply. Once an inertial frame is known, frames that translate uniformly relative to it are also inertial.

**Examples.** A frame at rest relative to the fixed stars is a good approximation to an inertial frame for an observer on Earth. A laboratory on Earth is approximately inertial. Earth's rotation causes small, measurable effects such as the Coriolis effect, which can be neglected in many everyday problems.

**Galileo's principle of relativity.** All inertial frames are physically equivalent. A mechanical experiment cannot distinguish one inertial frame from another.

For aligned axes and coincident origins at $t=0$, the transformation between two inertial frames is the Galilean transformation.

$$
\mechanical{\vec r}\,'=\mechanical{\vec r}-\mechanical{\vec V}t,
\qquad t'=t,
$$

where $\mechanical{\vec V}$ is the relative velocity of the frames. Classical mechanics is *invariant under Galilean transformations*.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\mechanical{\vec r}, \mechanical{\vec r}\,'$ | $\unit{m}$ | Position vector | The position of the same point in the two inertial frames. |
| $\mechanical{\vec V}$ | $\unit{m\,s^{-1}}$ | Relative velocity | The constant velocity of the primed frame relative to the unprimed frame. |
| $t, t'$ | $\unit{s}$ | Time | The time assigned to an event in the two frames. |
