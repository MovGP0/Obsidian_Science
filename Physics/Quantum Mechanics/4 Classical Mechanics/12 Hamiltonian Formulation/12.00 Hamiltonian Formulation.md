> [!TLDR]
> Hamilton's first-order equations describe motion in phase space using generalized coordinates and their conjugate momenta.

The Hamiltonian formulation replaces generalized velocities $\dot q_k$ with conjugate momenta $p_k$. The Hamiltonian $\energy{H}(q,p,t)$ replaces the Lagrangian $\energy{L}(q,\dot q,t)$. The resulting first-order equations have a symmetric form and define a flow in phase space. Phase space is central to classical mechanics and has an analogue in quantum theory.

## Chapter summary

- The Legendre transformation takes $\energy{L}(q,\dot q,t)$ to $\energy{H}(q,p,t)$ using $p_k=\partial\energy{L}/\partial\dot q_k$.
- The Hamiltonian is $\energy{H}=\sum_k p_k\dot q_k-\energy{L}$. For scleronomic constraints and a velocity-independent potential, $\energy{H}=\energy{T}+\energy{U}=\energy{E}$.
- Hamilton's equations $\dot q_k=\partial\energy{H}/\partial p_k$ and $\dot p_k=-\partial\energy{H}/\partial q_k$ give a system of $2n$ first-order equations.
- Trajectories in the $2n$-dimensional phase space are deterministic.
- Poisson brackets $\{f,g\}$ obey antisymmetry, the Leibniz rule, and the Jacobi identity.
- A phase-space function evolves according to $df/dt=\{f,\energy{H}\}+\partial f/\partial t$.
- Liouville's theorem states that the flow preserves phase-space volume.
