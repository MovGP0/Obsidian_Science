---
title: "Viscosity and Laminar Flow"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Viscosity transfers momentum between neighboring layers. In laminar pipe flow, the volume flow rate scales with the fourth power of the radius.

A flowing layer in a viscous liquid exerts shear stress on adjacent layers. For a Newtonian fluid,

$$
\mechanical{\tau}=\mechanical{\eta}\frac{d\mechanical{v}}{d\mechanical{z}},
$$

where $\mechanical{\eta}$ is the **dynamic viscosity**, measured in $\unit{Pa\,s}$. For water at $20\unit{{}^\circ C}$, $\mechanical{\eta}\approx\quantity{10^{-3}}{Pa\,s}$.

**Poiseuille flow.** For fully developed laminar flow in a cylindrical pipe of radius $\mechanical{R}$ and length $\mechanical{L}$, a pressure drop $\Delta\mechanical{p}$ gives volume flow rate

$$
\mechanical{Q}=\frac{\pi\mechanical{R}^4\Delta\mechanical{p}}{8\mechanical{\eta}\mechanical{L}}.
$$

Small radius changes strongly affect the volume flow rate because it scales as $\mechanical{R}^4$.

**Reynolds number.** The ratio $\mathrm{Re}=\mechanical{\rho}\mechanical{v}\mechanical{L}/\mechanical{\eta}$ uses a characteristic length $\mechanical{L}$. It helps distinguish flow regimes. In a circular pipe, use the diameter as the characteristic length. Laminar flow is typical below $\mathrm{Re}\approx2000$.

At larger values, the flow can transition to turbulence. The threshold depends on the flow and disturbances. Bernoulli's ideal balance neglects viscous losses. If viscosity is appreciable, the mechanical energy per unit volume can decrease even in laminar flow.

> [!note] Analytical Mechanics
> The Lagrangian and Hamiltonian descriptions of fluids use fields. The source writes the action as $S=\int\mathcal L\,d^4x$ and relates the field description to the Euler and Navier–Stokes equations. An ordinary conservative action gives the ideal-fluid equations. Viscous dissipation requires additional terms or a different variational formulation.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\mechanical{\tau}$ | $\unit{Pa}$ | Shear stress | The tangential force per unit area between layers. |
| $\mechanical{\eta}$ | $\unit{Pa\,s}$ | Dynamic viscosity | The coefficient that relates shear stress to velocity gradient. |
| $\mechanical{v}$ | $\unit{m\,s^{-1}}$ | Flow speed | The local velocity component or the characteristic flow speed. |
| $\mechanical{z}$ | $\unit{m}$ | Transverse position | The coordinate across the fluid layers. |
| $\mechanical{R}$ | $\unit{m}$ | Pipe radius | The internal radius of the cylindrical pipe. |
| $\mechanical{L}$ | $\unit{m}$ | Length | The length of the pipe or the characteristic length used in the Reynolds number. |
| $\Delta\mechanical{p}$ | $\unit{Pa}$ | Pressure drop | The pressure difference that drives the pipe flow. |
| $\mechanical{Q}$ | $\unit{m^3\,s^{-1}}$ | Volume flow rate | The volume passing through a section per unit time. |
| $\mechanical{\rho}$ | $\unit{kg\,m^{-3}}$ | Density | The fluid mass per unit volume. |
| $\mathrm{Re}$ | 1 | Reynolds number | The ratio of inertial to viscous effects. |
| — | $\unit{{}^\circ C}$ | Water temperature | The temperature at which the example viscosity is given. |
| $S$ | $\unit{J\,s}$ | Action | The integral of the Lagrangian density over space and time. |
| $\energy{\mathcal L}$ | $\unit{J\,m^{-3}}$ | Lagrangian density | The Lagrangian per unit spatial volume in the field description. |
