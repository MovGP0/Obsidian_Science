---
title: "Worked Examples of Coupled Systems"
---

> [!TLDR]
> The normal modes of coupled systems explain molecular vibrations and other oscillation patterns.

## Diatomic and triatomic molecules

Model the vibration of a diatomic molecule $A$–$B$ as a one-dimensional harmonic oscillator. The motion is along the molecular axis. The stiffness $\mechanical{k}$ is the curvature of the interatomic potential at its minimum. The reduced mass is $\mechanical{\mu}=\mechanical{m}_A\mechanical{m}_B/(\mechanical{m}_A+\mechanical{m}_B)$, and $\signal{\omega}=\sqrt{\mechanical{k}/\mechanical{\mu}}$. Infrared spectra give the corresponding spectroscopic wavenumbers.

A linear triatomic molecule such as carbon dioxide has three distinct vibrational frequencies. The bending frequency is doubly degenerate.

| Vibration | Symbol | Typical wavenumber |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Symmetric stretch | $\signal{\nu}_1$ | $\quantity{1300}{cm^{-1}}$ |
| Bend | $\signal{\nu}_2$ | $\quantity{670}{cm^{-1}}$ |
| Antisymmetric stretch | $\signal{\nu}_3$ | $\quantity{2350}{cm^{-1}}$ |

The bend and antisymmetric stretch are infrared-active. Absorption of terrestrial infrared radiation by carbon dioxide is relevant to climate modeling.

## Building with multiple modes

Model a building with $N$ floors as $N$ masses $\mechanical{m}$ coupled by $N$ equivalent springs of stiffness $\mechanical{k}$, representing the supporting structure. For a fixed base and free top, the natural frequencies are

$$
\signal{\omega}_j=2\sqrt{\frac{\mechanical{k}}{\mechanical{m}}}\sin\left(\frac{(2j-1)\pi}{2(2N+1)}\right),\qquad j=1,\ldots,N.
$$

The lowest frequency is the fundamental and is important for seismic response. In this model, a tall building has a low fundamental frequency and can resonate with long-period earthquake waves.

## Coupled pendulums and beats

Couple two identical pendulums with a weak spring. The symmetric mode is in phase with frequency $\signal{\omega}_+$. The antisymmetric mode is out of phase with a slightly higher frequency $\signal{\omega}_-$. If initially only one pendulum moves, energy passes back and forth between the pendulums at the beat angular frequency $\signal{\omega}_--\signal{\omega}_+$. This effect is easy to observe in a laboratory.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\mechanical{m}_A,\mechanical{m}_B,\mechanical{\mu},\mechanical{m}$ | $\unit{kg}$ | Masses | The two atomic masses, reduced mass, and equivalent mass of each floor. |
| $\mechanical{k}$ | $\unit{N\,m^{-1}}$ | Stiffness | The bond stiffness or equivalent stiffness of a floor. |
| $\signal{\omega},\signal{\omega}_j,\signal{\omega}_+,\signal{\omega}_-$ | $\unit{rad\,s^{-1}}$ | Angular frequencies | The molecular, building, and pendulum frequencies. |
| $\signal{\nu}_1,\signal{\nu}_2,\signal{\nu}_3$ | $\unit{cm^{-1}}$ | Spectroscopic wavenumbers | The three distinct vibrational bands of carbon dioxide. |
