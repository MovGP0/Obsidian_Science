---
title: "History of the Principle of Least Action"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Extremum principles in optics led to the formulation of stationary action for mechanics.

The idea that motion follows an extremum principle is old. In the first century CE, Heron of Alexandria showed that a reflected ray follows a shortest path in the reflection problem. In 1657, Pierre de Fermat generalized the idea to the *principle of least time* for refraction and obtained Snell's law. In 1744, Maupertuis extended it to material motion through a *principle of least action*. His reasoning was teleological: nature was thought to act in the most economical way.

Euler gave the principle a mathematical form. He calculated what is now called the **abbreviated action**, $S_0=\int p\,dq$, and showed that the actual motion makes it stationary.

Lagrange formulated the principle more generally in 1788 and applied it to constrained systems. William Rowan Hamilton gave it its modern form in 1834–1835. For $\energy{L}=\energy{T}-\energy{U}$ and fixed endpoint times and configurations, the action $S=\int\energy{L}\,dt$ is stationary on the physical path.

**Physical meaning.** Hamilton's principle gives the same equations as Newton's laws, using a condition on a whole path. On a given time interval, the physical path is stationary against variations that fix its endpoints. Optics gives an analogy, and Feynman's path integral extends the role of the action to quantum mechanics.
