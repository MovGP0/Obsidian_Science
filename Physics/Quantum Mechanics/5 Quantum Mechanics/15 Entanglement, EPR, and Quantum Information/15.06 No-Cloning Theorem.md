---
title: "No-Cloning Theorem"
---

> [!TLDR]
> A single unitary operation cannot copy an arbitrary unknown quantum state. A unitary can copy states drawn from a known orthonormal basis.

The no-cloning theorem follows from unitary preservation of inner products.

> [!theorem] Theorem 15.1 · No-cloning
> There is no unitary $\hat U$ on $\mathcal H\otimes\mathcal H$ and reference state $\lvert0\rangle$ such that
>
> $$
> \hat U(\lvert\signal{\psi}\rangle\otimes\lvert0\rangle)=\lvert\signal{\psi}\rangle\otimes\lvert\signal{\psi}\rangle
> $$
>
> for every $\lvert\signal{\psi}\rangle\in\mathcal H$.

Suppose such a unitary exists. It takes $\lvert\signal{\psi}\rangle\lvert0\rangle$ to $\lvert\signal{\psi}\rangle\lvert\signal{\psi}\rangle$ and $\lvert\signal{\varphi}\rangle\lvert0\rangle$ to $\lvert\signal{\varphi}\rangle\lvert\signal{\varphi}\rangle$. The inner product must be the same before and after the operation.

$$
\langle\signal{\varphi}\mid\signal{\psi}\rangle
=\langle\signal{\varphi}\mid\signal{\psi}\rangle\langle0\mid0\rangle
=\langle\signal{\varphi}\mid\signal{\psi}\rangle^2.
$$

The equation $z=z^2$ for $z=\langle\signal{\varphi}\mid\signal{\psi}\rangle\in\mathbb C$ has only the solutions $0$ and $1$. Therefore the two normalized vectors must be orthogonal or equal. The same physical state can also be represented by a vector that differs by a global phase. A single device cannot clone two distinct nonorthogonal states, so it cannot clone arbitrary superpositions.

A fixed orthonormal basis can be copied, as in a classical bit-copy operation. Approximate copies can also be produced with fidelity below one. The theorem excludes a perfect, universal, deterministic copy. This prevents the amplification of arbitrary quantum signals by copying, as in classical telecommunications, with consequences for quantum cryptography.
