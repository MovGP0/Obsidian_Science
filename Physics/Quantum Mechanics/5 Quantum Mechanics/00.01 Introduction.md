> [!TLDR]
> Quantum mechanics describes systems at atomic and subatomic scales through a new account of states, observables, dynamics, and measurement.

Quantum mechanics is a foundation of modern physics. It gives the concepts and mathematical tools necessary to describe physical systems at atomic and subatomic scales, where classical mechanics is insufficient. At these scales, the concepts of state, observable, time evolution, and measurement require a new formulation.

The starting point is a series of experiments and discoveries from the late nineteenth century and the first decades of the twentieth. Blackbody radiation, the photoelectric and Compton effects, atomic spectra, wave–particle duality, the double-slit experiment, and the Stern–Gerlach experiment revealed the limits of the classical description. These results motivated a new theory based on the postulates of quantum mechanics, the Born rule, and the Schrödinger equation. The book then introduces the main tools of the formalism, including states and observables, uncertainty relations, unitary time evolution, symmetries, and conservation laws.

The next chapters examine basic physical systems such as free particles, potential wells, barriers and tunneling, and the harmonic oscillator, which is central to theoretical physics. They also present Feynman path integrals as an equivalent formulation and a route to more advanced theories. The book then treats orbital angular momentum, spin, their coupling, and the Wigner–Eckart theorem, followed by the hydrogen atom, fine-structure corrections, and the Zeeman effect.

The second half of the book treats collective phenomena and more advanced concepts. It covers the density matrix and decoherence, identical particles and the Pauli exclusion principle, entanglement and Bell inequalities, and the basics of quantum information. It then develops time-independent and time-dependent perturbation theory, symmetries and conservation laws, geometric phases, and band theory for periodic potentials. The final topics are variational methods, the Wentzel–Kramers–Brillouin (WKB) approximation, and scattering theory.

Worked examples show how to use these methods. The chapters contain more than two hundred exercises in total, with final results, and the last chapter provides detailed solutions.

The book has four parts.

- **Part I** starts with the experiments that exposed the limits of classical physics. It introduces the postulates of quantum mechanics and examines measurement and the uncertainty relation.
- **Part II** develops quantum dynamics and applies it to basic one-dimensional systems, the harmonic oscillator, and path integrals.
- **Part III** treats angular momentum, spin, their coupling, and the hydrogen atom, with emphasis on algebraic methods.
- **Part IV** introduces many-particle systems, the density matrix and decoherence, entanglement and quantum information, perturbation theory, symmetries, geometric phases, band structure, and approximation methods.

Quantum mechanics has withstood a century of experimental tests. In some comparisons, theory and experiment agree to about one part in a billion. Many of its predictions remain counterintuitive. This book gives the reader the tools to understand both the theory's predictive power and its unfamiliar concepts through precise reasoning.
