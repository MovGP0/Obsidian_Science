---
title: "Helium Atom"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Helium has two electrons in a nuclear Coulomb potential. Electron–electron repulsion raises the ground-state energy and couples the two motions.

For nuclear charge number $Z=2$ and a fixed nucleus,

$$
\energy{\hat H}=\energy{\hat H}_0^{(1)}+\energy{\hat H}_0^{(2)}
+\frac{\electric e^{\,2}}{4\pi\electric{\varepsilon}_0\lvert\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2\rvert},
\qquad
\energy{\hat H}_0^{(i)}
=\frac{\mechanical{\hat{\mathbf p}}_i^{\,2}}{2\mechanical m_e}
-\frac{2\electric e^{\,2}}{4\pi\electric{\varepsilon}_0r_i}.
$$

Omitting the interaction would give two independent hydrogen-like ground-state energies, $-4(13.6\,\unit{eV})-4(13.6\,\unit{eV})=-108.8\,\unit{eV}$. The source gives the measured total binding energy as $\energy E\simeq-78.98\,\unit{eV}$ relative to the separated nucleus and electrons. The roughly $30\,\unit{eV}$ difference shows the effect of repulsion and the associated change in the electron state.

## Ground state

In the orbital approximation, both electrons occupy $1s$. The spatial product $\signal{\phi}_{1s}(1)\signal{\phi}_{1s}(2)$ is symmetric, so the spin factor must be the antisymmetric singlet.

$$
\lvert\signal{\Psi}_0\rangle
=\signal{\phi}_{1s}(1)\signal{\phi}_{1s}(2)\lvert0,0\rangle_{\mathrm{spin}}.
$$

## Excited states

When one electron occupies $2s$ or $2p$, two exchange symmetries are possible.

- **Parahelium.** The spatial factor is the symmetric combination of $\signal{\phi}_{1s}$ and $\signal{\phi}_{n\ell}$. The spin state is the antisymmetric singlet.
- **Orthohelium.** The spatial factor is the antisymmetric combination. The spin state is a symmetric triplet.

The Coulomb repulsion gives different energies to the two spatial factors. The resulting splitting is due to **exchange energy**. The antisymmetric spatial amplitude suppresses configurations in which the electrons are near each other. Within a given singly excited configuration, the triplet is generally lower than the singlet, consistent with Hund's rule.

## Variational estimate

Use hydrogen-like $1s$ trial orbitals with effective charge $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}$. Optimizing this parameter gives $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}=27/16\simeq1.69$ and $\energy E_0^{\mathrm{var}}\simeq-77.5\,\unit{eV}$. Mutual screening makes $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}$ smaller than $2$. More flexible trial functions can improve the estimate. See [[20.00 Advanced Methods]] for the variational method.
