---
title: "The Quantum State"
---

> [!TLDR]
> A pure quantum state is a ray in a complex Hilbert space. Relative phase can affect measurement outcomes.

> [!definition] Definition 2.1 | Postulate I. Quantum State
> At a given time, a nonzero vector $\lvert\psi\rangle$ in a complex Hilbert space $\mathcal H$ represents the **pure state** of an isolated quantum system. Vectors that differ by a nonzero complex factor represent the same state.
>
> $$
> \lvert\psi\rangle\sim c\lvert\psi\rangle,\qquad c\in\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}.
> $$
>
> One can choose a normalized representative with $\langle\psi\mid\psi\rangle=1$.

The space $\mathcal H$ depends on the system. A point particle in one dimension uses $L^2(\mathbb R)$. An isolated spin-½ degree of freedom uses $\mathbb C^2$. A spin-½ particle in three dimensions uses $L^2(\mathbb R^3)\otimes\mathbb C^2$.

A **global phase** $\lvert\psi\rangle\to e^{i\alpha}\lvert\psi\rangle$ with real $\alpha$ has no measurable effect. A **relative phase** can change measurement outcomes. For example, $\lvert\psi\rangle=a\lvert u\rangle+b\lvert v\rangle$ and $\lvert\psi'\rangle=a\lvert u\rangle+e^{i\beta}b\lvert v\rangle$ generally give different results in a suitable measurement when both components contribute and $e^{i\beta}\ne1$. Relative phase is a feature of a coherent superposition. A classical mixture of the same states has no such phase.

**Superposition principle.** If $\lvert\psi_1\rangle$ and $\lvert\psi_2\rangle$ represent states, any nonzero linear combination $\alpha\lvert\psi_1\rangle+\beta\lvert\psi_2\rangle$ also represents a state after normalization. This follows from the linear structure of $\mathcal H$ and is the basis of quantum interference.
