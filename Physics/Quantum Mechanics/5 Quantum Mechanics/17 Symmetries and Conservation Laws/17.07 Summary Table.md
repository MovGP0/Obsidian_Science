> [!TLDR]
> Spatial translations, rotations, and time translations give conserved momentum, angular momentum, and energy. Discrete and antiunitary symmetries impose different constraints.

| Symmetry | Generator or type | Conserved quantity or constraint | Associated property |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Spatial translation | $\mechanical{\hat{\mathbf p}}$ | Momentum | Spatial homogeneity |
| Rotation | $\mechanical{\mathbf J}$ | $\mechanical{\mathbf L}+\mechanical{\mathbf S}$ | Isotropy |
| Time translation | $\energy{\hat H}$ | Energy | Time-independent Hamiltonian |
| Global phase $e^{i\alpha\hat I}$ | $\hat I$ | Norm | Related field-phase symmetries in quantum field theory can give conserved charge |
| Parity $\hat\Pi$ | Discrete | Parity | Even potential |
| Time reversal $\hat\Theta$ | Antiunitary | Constraints such as Kramers degeneracy when $\hat\Theta^2=-\hat I$ | Reversibility |
| Exchange $\hat P_{ij}$ | Discrete | Particle statistics | Indistinguishability |

> [!note] Scope of the table
> The source associates time reversal with absence of dissipation. Time-reversal invariance is a symmetry of the specified dynamics. Norm preservation follows from unitarity, and the charge associated with a field-phase symmetry in quantum field theory comes from the generator of that symmetry.

## Physical meaning

Gauge symmetries constrain the form of fundamental interactions. The Higgs mechanism connects spontaneous symmetry breaking to particle masses. Quantum anomalies occur when a classical symmetry fails in the quantum theory and can permit decays that the classical symmetry would forbid. Representations of symmetry groups organize quantum states and observables.
