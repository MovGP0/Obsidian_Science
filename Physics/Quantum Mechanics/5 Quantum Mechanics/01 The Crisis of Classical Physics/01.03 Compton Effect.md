---
title: "Compton Effect"
---

> [!TLDR]
> When a photon scatters from an electron, its wavelength increases by an amount set by the scattering angle.

In 1923, Arthur Compton studied the scattering of X-rays from graphite. In classical Thomson scattering, a free electron scatters an electromagnetic wave without changing its frequency. Compton found a longer wavelength in the radiation scattered at an angle $\theta$ to the incident direction. The wavelength shift is

$$
\Delta\signal{\lambda}=\signal{\lambda}'-\signal{\lambda}
=\signal{\lambda}_C(1-\cos\theta),\qquad
\signal{\lambda}_C=\frac{h}{\mechanical{m}_ec}\simeq\quantity{2.43\times10^{-12}}{m}.
\tag{1.2}
$$

The constant $\signal{\lambda}_C$ is the **Compton wavelength of the electron**. Figure 1.2 shows the geometry of the collision.

![[quantum-mechanics-fig-1-2-compton-scattering.svg|**Fig. 1.2** An incident photon strikes an electron initially at rest. The photon scatters through angle θ with a lower frequency, and the electron recoils. Conservation of four-momentum gives the wavelength shift.]]

Treat the photon–electron interaction as an elastic relativistic collision. The photon has energy $\energy{E}=h\signal{\nu}=hc/\signal{\lambda}$ and momentum magnitude $\mechanical{p}=h/\signal{\lambda}$. The total energy and momentum of the incident photon and stationary electron equal those of the scattered photon and recoiling electron.

## Derivation of the wavelength shift

Let the incident photon move along $x$. Its four-momentum in the plane of the collision is $(h\signal{\nu}/c,h\signal{\nu}/c,0)$. The stationary electron has four-momentum $(\mechanical{m}_ec,0,0)$. After the collision, the photon has components $(h\signal{\nu}'/c,h\signal{\nu}'\cos\theta/c,h\signal{\nu}'\sin\theta/c)$, and the electron has components $(\energy{E}_e/c,\mechanical{\vec p}_e)$.

Use four-momentum conservation and the electron mass-shell relation $\energy{E}_e^2-\mechanical{p}_e^2c^2=\mechanical{m}_e^2c^4$ to obtain

$$
\frac{1}{\signal{\nu}'}-\frac{1}{\signal{\nu}}
=\frac{h}{\mechanical{m}_ec^2}(1-\cos\theta).
$$

Multiplication by $c$ gives Eq. (1.2). The derivation uses $\energy{E}=\mechanical{p}c$ for the massless photon and the massive dispersion relation for the electron.

Compton scattering confirmed that a photon carries both energy $h\signal{\nu}$ and momentum magnitude $h\signal{\nu}/c=h/\signal{\lambda}$.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\signal{\lambda},\signal{\lambda}',\signal{\lambda}_C,\Delta\signal{\lambda}$ | $\unit{m}$ | Wavelengths and shift | The incident, scattered, and electron Compton wavelengths and the scattering shift. |
| $\signal{\nu},\signal{\nu}'$ | $\unit{Hz}$ | Frequencies | The incident and scattered photon frequencies. |
| $\theta$ | $\unit{rad}$ | Scattering angle | The angle between the incident and scattered photon directions. |

| $\energy{E},\energy{E}_e$ | $\unit{J}$ | Energies | The photon energy and total electron energy. |
