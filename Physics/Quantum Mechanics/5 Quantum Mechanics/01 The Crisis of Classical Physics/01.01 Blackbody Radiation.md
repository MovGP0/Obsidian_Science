title: "Blackbody Radiation"

> [!TLDR]
> A blackbody absorbs incident radiation and emits a thermal spectrum set by its temperature.

A **blackbody** is an ideal object in thermal equilibrium that absorbs all incident radiation. Its emission spectrum depends only on its temperature $\thermal{T}$. An experiment approximates it with a cavity whose opaque walls remain at a fixed temperature. Radiation that leaves through a small hole has a thermal spectrum.

The **spectral energy density** $\energy{u}(\signal{\nu},\thermal{T})$ is the radiation energy per unit volume in the interval from $\signal{\nu}$ to $\signal{\nu}+d\signal{\nu}$, divided by the interval width $d\signal{\nu}$. The classical Rayleigh–Jeans prediction is

$$
\energy{u}_{\mathrm{RJ}}(\signal{\nu},\thermal{T})=
\frac{8\pi\signal{\nu}^2}{c^3}\thermal{k}_B\thermal{T}.
\tag{1.1}
$$

The formula counts the electromagnetic modes of the cavity and assigns each mode the mean energy $\thermal{k}_B\thermal{T}$ by equipartition. It agrees with observations at low frequencies. At high frequencies it grows as $\signal{\nu}^2$, so its integral over all frequencies gives an infinite energy density. This is the **ultraviolet catastrophe**.

The observed spectrum has a finite integral and falls exponentially at high frequencies. Its peak moves to higher frequency as the temperature increases. Wien's displacement law gives $\signal{\nu}_{\max}\propto\thermal{T}$ for the spectrum per unit frequency.

## Planck's hypothesis

In 1900, Max Planck reproduced the measured spectrum by introducing an assumption that later became central to quantum theory. The elementary oscillators of the cavity walls exchange energy with the electromagnetic field only in integer multiples of a minimum quantity

$$
\energy{\varepsilon}=h\signal{\nu},
$$

where $h\simeq\quantity{6.626\times10^{-34}}{J\,s}$ is the **Planck constant**. Combined with the Boltzmann distribution, this assumption gives **Planck's radiation law**.

$$
\energy{u}(\signal{\nu},\thermal{T})=
\frac{8\pi\signal{\nu}^2}{c^3}
\frac{h\signal{\nu}}{\exp(h\signal{\nu}/(\thermal{k}_B\thermal{T}))-1}.
$$

For $h\signal{\nu}\ll\thermal{k}_B\thermal{T}$, the last factor approaches $\thermal{k}_B\thermal{T}$, which recovers Eq. (1.1). For $h\signal{\nu}\gg\thermal{k}_B\thermal{T}$, the spectral density falls exponentially.

## Example. Peak of the spectrum

Differentiate $\energy{u}(\signal{\nu},\thermal{T})$ with respect to $\signal{\nu}$ and set the derivative to zero. With $x=h\signal{\nu}_{\max}/(\thermal{k}_B\thermal{T})$, the nonzero root of $(3-x)e^x=3$ is $x\simeq2.82$. This gives Wien's displacement law in frequency form.

$$
h\signal{\nu}_{\max}\simeq2.82\thermal{k}_B\thermal{T}.
$$

For the Sun, $\thermal{T}\simeq\quantity{5800}{K}$ gives $\signal{\nu}_{\max}\simeq\quantity{3.4\times10^{14}}{Hz}$ and $c/\signal{\nu}_{\max}\simeq\quantity{880}{nm}$, in the near infrared. In the spectrum per unit wavelength, $\energy{u}(\signal{\lambda},\thermal{T})$ instead peaks near $\signal{\lambda}_{\max}\simeq\quantity{500}{nm}$, in the visible range. This agrees with $\signal{\lambda}_{\max}\thermal{T}\simeq\quantity{2.90\times10^{-3}}{m\,K}$. The peaks of the two spectral densities occur at different physical wavelengths because changing the spectral variable changes the density. Exercise 9 verifies this distinction.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\thermal{T}$ | $\unit{K}$ | Absolute temperature | The temperature of the blackbody. |
| $\energy{u}(\signal{\nu},\thermal{T}),\energy{u}_{\mathrm{RJ}}$ | $\unit{J\,m^{-3}\,Hz^{-1}}$ | Spectral energy density per frequency | The thermal and Rayleigh–Jeans energy densities per unit frequency. |
| $\energy{u}(\signal{\lambda},\thermal{T})$ | $\unit{J\,m^{-4}}$ | Spectral energy density per wavelength | The energy density per unit wavelength. |
| $\signal{\nu},\signal{\nu}_{\max}$ | $\unit{Hz}$ | Frequency | The radiation frequency and the peak of the frequency spectrum. |
| $\signal{\lambda},\signal{\lambda}_{\max}$ | $\unit{m}$ | Wavelength | The radiation wavelength and the peak of the wavelength spectrum. |
| $\thermal{k}_B$ | $\unit{J\,K^{-1}}$ | Boltzmann constant | The constant that relates thermal energy to temperature. |
| $h$ | $\unit{J\,s}$ | Planck constant | The constant that relates a quantum of energy to frequency. |
| $c$ | $\unit{m\,s^{-1}}$ | Speed of light | The speed of light in vacuum. |
| $\energy{\varepsilon}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Energy quantum | The smallest increment of energy exchanged at the specified frequency. |
