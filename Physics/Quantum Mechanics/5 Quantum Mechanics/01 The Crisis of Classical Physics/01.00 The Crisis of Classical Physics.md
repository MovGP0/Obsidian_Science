> [!TLDR]
> Experiments on matter and radiation revealed where classical physics fails and introduced the ideas that led to quantum mechanics.

At the end of the nineteenth century, physics appeared nearly complete. Newtonian mechanics described motion precisely, Maxwell's electromagnetism unified electricity, magnetism, and optics, and thermodynamics supplied a coherent framework. In 1900, Lord Kelvin identified two outstanding problems. The account in this chapter associates the first with the null result of the Michelson–Morley search for an ether wind and the second with the failure of energy equipartition, familiar from the ultraviolet catastrophe in blackbody radiation. The problems foreshadowed special relativity and quantum mechanics, respectively.

This chapter follows the experiments from about 1900 to 1927 that showed the limits of the classical picture of matter and radiation. Each experiment introduced an idea that the later chapters formulate mathematically.

## Chapter summary

- Radiation is quantized in photons with $\energy{E}=h\signal{\nu}$ and $\mechanical{p}=h/\signal{\lambda}$, as shown by blackbody radiation, the photoelectric effect, and Compton scattering.
- Atomic spectra are discrete. The Bohr model predicts the main hydrogen levels $\energy{E}_n\simeq-\quantity{13.6}{eV}/n^2$.
- A material particle with momentum magnitude $\mechanical{p}$ has wavelength $\signal{\lambda}=h/\mechanical{p}$. Electron diffraction confirmed this de Broglie relation.
- In the double-slit experiment, coherent path amplitudes add before the probability is calculated. The probability is proportional to $\lvert\signal{\psi}_1+\signal{\psi}_2\rvert^2$.
- Stern–Gerlach splitting shows that an observable can have a discrete spectrum. The two components were later explained by spin.
- The uncertainty estimate $\Delta\mechanical{x}\Delta\mechanical{p}\gtrsim\hbar$ gives the characteristic stable size of the atom.
