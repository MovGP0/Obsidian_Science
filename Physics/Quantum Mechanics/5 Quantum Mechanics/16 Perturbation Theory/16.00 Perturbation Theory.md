> [!TLDR]
> Perturbation theory starts from a solvable Hamiltonian and expands the effects of a small correction. It gives energy shifts for static perturbations and transition probabilities for time-dependent perturbations.

The harmonic oscillator, hydrogen atom, and particle in a box have closed-form solutions in their basic models. Many systems, such as hydrogen in an electric field, helium, and molecules, require approximation. In perturbation theory, start from a solved Hamiltonian $\energy{\hat H}_0$ and treat a small correction $\energy{\hat V}$ by expanding in a parameter that controls its strength.

A time-independent perturbation changes the energy levels and eigenstates of a stationary system. A time-dependent perturbation, such as an oscillating electromagnetic field, can induce transitions. This chapter treats the two cases separately.

## Chapter summary

- For nondegenerate levels, $\energy{E}_n^{(1)}=\langle n\rvert\energy{\hat V}\lvert n\rangle$ and $\energy{E}_n^{(2)}=\sum_{m\ne n}\lvert\energy{V}_{mn}\rvert^2/(\energy{E}_n-\energy{E}_m)$, with unperturbed energies in the denominator.
- For a degenerate level, diagonalize $\energy{\hat V}$ within the degenerate eigenspace.
- The hydrogen $n=2$ level has linear Stark shifts of $\pm3\electric{e}\electric{E}a_0$ and an unshifted doublet.
- First-order time-dependent perturbation theory gives $c_f^{(1)}(t)=(i\mechanical{\hbar})^{-1}\int_0^t\energy{V}_{fi}(t')e^{i\omega_{fi}t'}\,\mathrm dt'$.
- Fermi's golden rule gives $\Gamma_{i\to f}=(2\pi/\mechanical{\hbar})\lvert\energy{V}_{0,fi}\rvert^2\rho(\energy{E}_f)$.
- Electric dipole absorption and emission obey $\Delta\ell=\pm1$ and $\Delta m=0,\pm1$ for the orbital states considered here.
- At resonance, a coherently driven two-level system exhibits Rabi oscillations with $P_{i\to f}(t)=\sin^2(\Omega_Rt/2)$.

> [!note] Second-order energy in the source summary
> The source summary omits the square on $\lvert\energy{V}_{mn}\rvert$. The expression above follows the derivation.
