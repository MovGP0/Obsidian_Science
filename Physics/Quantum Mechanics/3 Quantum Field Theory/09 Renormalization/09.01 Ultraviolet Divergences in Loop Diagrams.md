> [!TLDR]
> Ultraviolet-divergent loop integrals require a distinction between bare parameters and the renormalized quantities measured in experiments.

## Chapter introduction

> “The shell game that we play ... is technically called ‘renormalization’. But no matter how clever the word, it is still what I would call a dippy process!”
>
> — Richard P. Feynman, *QED: The Strange Theory of Light and Matter*, as quoted in the source

Perturbative field theory has a structural difficulty: Feynman diagrams with loops contain integrals that diverge in the ultraviolet. For example, the one-loop electron self-energy in QED has a logarithmically divergent integral over internal momentum as the upper energy scale increases.

This apparent obstacle affected the consistency of the theory for almost a decade. Between 1947 and 1949, Tomonaga, Schwinger, and Feynman developed a systematic answer: **renormalization**. Dyson then showed the equivalence of their approaches.

Ultraviolet divergences are not artifacts of a calculation error. In the effective-theory interpretation used by the source, they reflect incomplete knowledge of physics at arbitrarily high energies. A theory valid up to a scale $\energy{\Lambda}$ has parameters, including mass, charge, and field normalization, that depend on $\energy{\Lambda}$. Laboratory observations concern renormalized physical quantities rather than the bare parameters in the Lagrangian.

Renormalized parameters include quantum corrections. Once they have been fixed by experiment, the theory gives finite, testable predictions for the physical observables considered. The source reports agreement of the electron anomalous-moment prediction with experiment to about twelve significant figures.

This chapter treats QED renormalization. It first discusses divergences and power counting, then introduces dimensional regularization. It calculates the three divergent one-loop structures explicitly: electron self-energy, vacuum polarization, and vertex correction. The last calculation leads to Schwinger's result
$$
\magnetic{a}_e=\frac{\electric{\alpha}}{2\pi}.
$$

The practical question is how to predict a second observable after mass and charge have been fixed from a first set of measurements. An unregulated loop cannot answer that question: changing an undefined infinity is not a physical prediction. Keep a regulator until loop terms and counterterms have been combined, impose the chosen parameter definitions, and only then remove the regulator. The remaining finite amplitude is the object used to predict a cross section or an energy difference.

The cutoff scale in an effective description and an auxiliary regulator have different roles. A physical validity scale limits which processes the model can describe. An auxiliary regulator is a calculation device; a renormalized observable must not depend on it. A subtraction scheme defines the finite parts of parameters. Changing that scheme requires changing the parameter values consistently, so it cannot change an exact observable. At finite perturbative order, residual dependence can remain at higher order.

As a check, set the electromagnetic coupling to zero in the result above. The magnetic anomaly then vanishes, as required for the free Dirac electron. The one-loop result predicts only the leading term; the quoted experimental agreement concerns the full calculation described by the source, not this term alone.
