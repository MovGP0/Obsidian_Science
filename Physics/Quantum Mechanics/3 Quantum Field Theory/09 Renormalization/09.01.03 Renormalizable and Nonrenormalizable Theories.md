---
title: "Renormalizable and Nonrenormalizable Theories"
---

> [!TLDR]
> The mass dimension of an interaction coupling controls whether additional vertices worsen or improve ultraviolet power counting.

Equation (9.3) is specific to QED. For a general four-dimensional interaction, the source writes the interaction dimension as $[\lambda]=4-\Delta$ and identifies $\Delta$ as the engineering dimension of the coupling. It then gives
$$
D=4-E_B-\frac32E_F+\Delta V,
\tag{9.4}
$$
where $V$ counts vertices of this interaction type.

> [!note]
> The source's sign conventions are inconsistent. If $\Delta$ is the coupling's mass dimension as stated, power counting gives $D=4-E_B-\tfrac32E_F-\Delta V$. The printed plus sign instead requires $\Delta$ to mean the interaction-operator dimension minus four. In particular, negative coupling dimension worsens ultraviolet power counting.

For $\Delta<0$, corresponding to interaction dimension $[\lambda]>4$, the source states that $D$ grows with $V$. Infinitely many diagram types then diverge and require infinitely many counterterms. This is the nonrenormalizable case.

Examples of the nonrenormalizable case include Einstein gravity and the Fermi model.

- If $\Delta=0$, so the interaction dimension is $[\lambda]=4$, $D$ does not depend on $V$. Only finitely many external structures diverge. The theory is **renormalizable**. Examples are QED, scalar $\phi^4$ theory, and Yang–Mills theory.
- If $\Delta>0$, so $[\lambda]<4$, $D$ decreases as $V$ grows. Only finitely many diagrams diverge in total. The theory is **superrenormalizable**. Examples are $\phi^3$ theory in four dimensions and $\phi^4$ theory in two dimensions.

These classifications use $\Delta$ as the coupling dimension and therefore the corrected minus-sign form of the power-counting formula explained above.

Nonrenormalizable theories can still have physical meaning as **effective field theories** valid below a scale $\energy{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{UV}}$. New degrees of freedom can appear above that scale. The source states that renormalizability is needed for precise perturbative predictions at all energies.

> [!note]
> Renormalizability alone does not guarantee perturbative validity at arbitrarily high energy. Effective theories can give precise predictions within their range, with errors organized by powers of the energy divided by the cutoff.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
