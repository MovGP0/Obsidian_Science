> [!TLDR]
> A Lie group combines a smooth manifold with compatible group operations. Matrix groups give the main examples used in field theory.

The following definitions and examples specify the continuous groups used in the text.

## From continuous transformations to QFT symmetries

Begin with [[B.04.01 Definition]] to separate manifold dimension from rank. [[B.04.02 Basic Examples]] shows how real, complex, orthogonal, and unitary constraints change the parameter count. The next section, [[B.05 Lie Algebras]], replaces transformations near the identity by tangent generators and their commutators.

A concrete distinction guides this reading: $SU(2)$ has three real parameters but only one independent commuting generator in a maximal torus. The matrices are $2\times2$; neither their size nor their rank is the group's manifold dimension. In a field theory, selecting a representation specifies how the internal components transform. Selecting an invariant action is a further model-building step.

The Lie algebra controls local transformations. It need not distinguish groups with different global topology. The rotation example $SU(2)$ versus $SO(3)$ therefore prepares the covering-group issue for Lorentz spinors. Discrete symmetries and disconnected components also require information beyond the tangent algebra.
