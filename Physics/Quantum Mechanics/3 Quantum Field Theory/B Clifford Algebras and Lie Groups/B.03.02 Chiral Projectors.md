---
title: "Chiral Projectors"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Chiral projectors split a four-dimensional Dirac spinor into complementary left- and right-Weyl components.

The projectors onto the left- and right-chiral subspaces are
$$
P_L=\frac{1-\gamma^5}{2},
\qquad
P_R=\frac{1+\gamma^5}{2}.
\tag{B.31}
$$
They satisfy
$$
P_{L,R}^2=P_{L,R},
\qquad
P_LP_R=0,
\qquad
P_L+P_R=\mathbf1.
$$
The Weyl spinors are
$$
\signal{\psi}_L=P_L\signal{\psi},
\qquad
\signal{\psi}_R=P_R\signal{\psi}.
$$

## Why Lorentz transformations preserve the split

The projector identities follow by expanding the products and using $(\gamma^5)^2=\mathbf1$. There is a further step: anticommutation with each gamma matrix implies that $\gamma^5$ commutes with their commutators. Hence it commutes with the connected Lorentz spin transformations generated by those commutators. Each projected subspace is a representation of this group.

In the Weyl basis, $P_L=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf1_2,0)$ and $P_R=\operatorname{diag}(0,\mathbf1_2)$. This is an immediate rank-two check. A gamma matrix instead obeys $P_L\gamma^\mu=\gamma^\mu P_R$: it exchanges the two subspaces. A Weyl representation is therefore not a two-dimensional representation of the full four-dimensional Clifford algebra.

Parity exchanges the two chiralities, so invariance under the connected Lorentz group does not establish parity invariance. Also distinguish chirality from helicity, the projection of spin along momentum. Their relation requires a choice of on-shell states and a massless limit; it is not part of the projector algebra.
