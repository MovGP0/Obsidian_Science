title: "Berezin Integral"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Berezin integration equals Grassmann differentiation. It selects the coefficient of the integrated generator and transforms with an inverse determinant.

Berezin defined differentiation and integration for Grassmann variables so that the formal rules remain similar to ordinary calculus.
The **left derivative** satisfies
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta_j}{\mathrm{d}\theta_i}=\delta_{ij},
\qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}1}{\mathrm{d}\theta_i}=0,
\qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\theta_i}(\theta_j\theta_k)
=\delta_{ij}\theta_k-\theta_j\delta_{ik}.
$$
This integral is coefficient extraction, not an area under a curve on the real line. Translation invariance gives its first rule: shifting the generator by an independent odd constant changes the integral of the generator only by that constant times the integral of one, which must vanish. The second rule fixes the normalization. Neither rule assigns a positive probability measure to Grassmann values.

Define the integral to reproduce integration by parts without boundary terms:
$$
\int\mathrm{d}\theta\,1=0,
\qquad
\int\mathrm{d}\theta\,\theta=1.
\tag{11.18}
$$
Integration and differentiation therefore give the same operation on one generator.

For several variables, use the ordered exterior-product measure.
With the source's order,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int\mathrm{d}\bar\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,
f(\theta,\bar\theta)
&=\int\mathrm{d}\bar\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,
(a+b\theta+c\bar\theta+d\theta\bar\theta)\\
&=d.
\end{aligned}
$$
Only the term containing both generators survives.

Keep the displayed orientation fixed when doing successive integrals. In particular, the integral of $\theta\bar\theta$ in this order is one, and that of $\bar\theta\theta$ is minus one. The left-derivative product rule has the same exchange sign. Switching to right derivatives is possible, but the source-derivative formulas must change with that choice.

## Change of variables

For a scaling $\theta\to c\theta$ with ordinary commuting $c$,
the measure transforms as
$\mathrm{d}\theta\to c^{-1}\mathrm{d}\theta$.
This is inverse to the ordinary commuting-variable Jacobian.

For a linear transformation $\theta_i\to M_{ij}\theta_j$,
$$
\mathrm{d}^n\theta\to(\det M)^{-1}\mathrm{d}^n\theta.
$$

The one-variable check explains the inverse: after $\theta'=c\theta$, the normalization integral contains both the factor $c$ from its generator and a factor $c^{-1}$ from its measure. Their product is one. In several variables, the top-degree generator product transforms by the determinant, so the ordered measure must transform by its inverse. This change of variables assumes an invertible matrix; it cannot be used to remove a zero mode with a singular map.
