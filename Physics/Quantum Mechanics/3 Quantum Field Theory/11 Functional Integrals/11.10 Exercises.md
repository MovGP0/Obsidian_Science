## Exercise 11.1: Harmonic oscillator at finite temperature

Use the Wick rotation $\mechanical{t}\to-i\mechanical{\tau}$ to calculate the harmonic-oscillator partition function
$\signal{Z}=\operatorname{Tr}(e^{-\thermal{\beta}\energy{H}})$
as a Euclidean path integral over
$\mechanical{\tau}\in[0,\thermal{\beta}]$ with periodic boundary conditions.
Expand the coordinate $\mechanical{q}(\mechanical{\tau})$ in a Fourier series with Matsubara frequencies
$\signal{\omega}_n=2\pi n/\thermal{\beta}$.
Show that
$$
\signal{Z}=\prod_n\frac{1}{\sqrt{\signal{\omega}_n^2+\signal{\omega}^2}},
$$
then use the infinite-product formula to recover
$$
\signal{Z}=\left[2\sinh\left(\frac{\thermal{\beta}\signal{\omega}}{2}\right)\right]^{-1}.
$$
Check the result against the direct sum
$$
\signal{Z}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
e^{-\thermal{\beta}\signal{\omega}(n+1/2)}.
$$

> [!note]
> The determinant product is formal until the path-integral normalization and the infinite-product regularization are specified. The final partition function uses $\mechanical{\hbar}=1$.

## Exercise 11.3: Fermionic and bosonic determinants

Consider a real scalar field and a Dirac field with the same mass $\mechanical{m}$ in $d=4$ dimensions.
Calculate the ratio of their contributions to the vacuum partition function:
$$
\frac{\signal{Z}_{\mathrm{Dirac}}}{\signal{Z}_{\mathrm{scalar}}}
=\frac{\det(i\not{\partial}-\mechanical{m})}
{[\det(-\Box-\mechanical{m}^2)]^{-1/2}}.
$$

Use
$$
\det(i\not{\partial}-\mechanical{m})\,
\det(-i\not{\partial}-\mechanical{m})
=\det(-\Box-\mechanical{m}^2+\text{spin term})
$$
to show that a fermion loop has the opposite sign to a boson loop, with spinor degeneracy four.
How does this generalize to supersymmetry?

> [!note]
> The displayed determinant identity is copied from the source. For ordinary commuting derivatives, the product of the two displayed Dirac operators gives $\Box+\mechanical{m}^2$, with an identity matrix in spinor space. A spin–field-strength term occurs when covariant derivatives do not commute. The sign and phase of a functional determinant also require a consistent Minkowski or Euclidean prescription.

## Exercise 11.4: Grassmann Gaussian integral

Prove formula (11.19).
For $n$ pairs of Grassmann variables $\bar{\theta}_i,\theta_j$, with $i,j=1,\ldots,n$,
$$
\int\prod_{i=1}^{n}d\bar{\theta}_i\,d\theta_i\,
\exp\left(-\sum_{i,j}\bar{\theta}_iM_{ij}\theta_j\right)
=\det M.
$$

> [!note]
> **Hint from the source:** Expand the exponential in a Taylor series. Nilpotency makes the series terminate. Only the term of degree $n$ in $\bar{\theta}$ and degree $n$ in $\theta$ survives. It reproduces the Leibniz expansion of the determinant. Use the same Berezin-measure ordering convention as in (11.19).

## Exercise 11.5: One-loop effective action for φ⁴ theory

Consider a scalar theory with potential
$$
\energy{V}(\signal{\phi})
=\frac{\mechanical{m}^2}{2}\signal{\phi}^2
+\frac{\signal{\lambda}}{4!}\signal{\phi}^4.
$$
Expand the action about a constant configuration $\signal{\phi}_{\mathrm{cl}}$:
$\signal{\phi}=\signal{\phi}_{\mathrm{cl}}+\signal{\eta}$,
where $\signal{\eta}$ is the fluctuation field.
Perform the Gaussian functional integral over $\signal{\eta}$ to obtain the one-loop effective potential.
Use dimensional regularization with $d=4-2\varepsilon$.
The source gives
$$
\energy{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}
=\frac{[\mechanical{m}^2+\signal{\lambda}\signal{\phi}_{\mathrm{cl}}^2/2]^2}
{64\pi^2}
\left[
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}-\frac{3}{2}
+\ln\frac{\mechanical{m}^2+\signal{\lambda}\signal{\phi}_{\mathrm{cl}}^2/2}
{\energy{\mu}^2}
+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
\right].
$$
After renormalization in the $\overline{\mathrm{MS}}$ scheme, recover the Coleman–Weinberg formula (11.16).

> [!note]
> The expression above preserves the source's signs. In the usual Euclidean determinant convention with $d=4-2\varepsilon$, the unrenormalized scalar contribution has a negative pole when the logarithm has the displayed positive sign. The source also suppresses the usual $\overline{\mathrm{MS}}$ constants. State the determinant and subtraction conventions when checking this formula.

### Solution 11.1 — Harmonic oscillator at finite temperature

Under the Wick rotation $\mechanical{t}=-i\mechanical{\tau}$, $i\energy{S}=-\energy{S}_{\mathrm E}$, where
$$
\energy{S}_{\mathrm E}
=\int_0^{\thermal{\beta}}d\mechanical{\tau}
\left[
\frac{\mechanical{m}}2\dot{\mechanical{q}}^{\,2}
+\frac{\mechanical{m}\signal{\omega}^2}{2}\mechanical{q}^2
\right].
$$
The coordinate is periodic over the inverse thermal energy
$\thermal{\beta}$. Expand
$$
\mechanical{q}(\mechanical{\tau})
=\frac1{\sqrt{\thermal{\beta}}}
\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}\mechanical{q}_ne^{i\signal{\omega}_n\mechanical{\tau}},
\qquad
\signal{\omega}_n=\frac{2\pi n}{\thermal{\beta}},
\qquad
\mechanical{q}_{-n}=\mechanical{q}_n^*.
$$
Then
$$
\energy{S}_{\mathrm E}
=\frac{\mechanical{m}}2\sum_n
(\signal{\omega}_n^2+\signal{\omega}^2)|\mechanical{q}_n|^2.
$$
The formal Gaussian integral gives
$$
\signal{Z}
=\mathcal N\prod_{n\in\mathbb Z}
\frac1{\sqrt{\mechanical{m}(\signal{\omega}_n^2+\signal{\omega}^2)}}.
$$
Separating the zero mode and pairing $n,-n$, the frequency dependence is
$$
\signal{Z}
=\frac{\mathcal N'}{\signal{\omega}}
\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{\signal{\omega}_n^2+\signal{\omega}^2},
$$
where the remaining normalization includes the mass factors and the regulated measure.

> [!note] Zero-mode factor
> The source's second product starts at $n=1$ and puts everything else into $\mathcal N'$. The zero mode contributes $1/\signal{\omega}$, which cannot be absorbed into a frequency-independent normalization. It is retained explicitly here.

The source now explicitly identifies the $n=0$ contribution as $1/\signal{\omega}$.
Use the product identity
$$
\prod_{n=1}^\infty\left(1+\frac{x^2}{n^2}\right)
=\frac{\sinh(\pi x)}{\pi x}
$$
with $x=\thermal{\beta}\signal{\omega}/(2\pi)$:
$$
\prod_{n=1}^\infty
\frac{\signal{\omega}_n^2+\signal{\omega}^2}{\signal{\omega}_n^2}
=
\prod_{n=1}^\infty
\left(1+\frac{\thermal{\beta}^2\signal{\omega}^2}{4\pi^2n^2}\right)
=\frac{\sinh(\thermal{\beta}\signal{\omega}/2)}
{\thermal{\beta}\signal{\omega}/2}.
$$
With the canonically normalized path-integral measure, the frequency-independent factors give
$$
\signal{Z}
=\frac1{\thermal{\beta}\signal{\omega}}
\prod_{n=1}^\infty
\left(1+\frac{\thermal{\beta}^2\signal{\omega}^2}{4\pi^2n^2}\right)^{-1}
=\frac1{2\sinh(\thermal{\beta}\signal{\omega}/2)}.
$$
The direct energy-level sum verifies it:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\signal{Z}
&=\sum_{n=0}^\infty
e^{-\thermal{\beta}\signal{\omega}(n+1/2)}\\
&=\frac{e^{-\thermal{\beta}\signal{\omega}/2}}
{1-e^{-\thermal{\beta}\signal{\omega}}}
=\frac1{e^{\thermal{\beta}\signal{\omega}/2}
-e^{-\thermal{\beta}\signal{\omega}/2}}\\
&=\frac1{2\sinh(\thermal{\beta}\signal{\omega}/2)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

### Solution 11.2 — Free-theory four-point function

In the convention of [[11.02.02 The Free Scalar Field and Gaussian Functional Integrals]], the free normalized generating functional is
$$
\signal{Z}_0[\signal{J}]
=\exp\left[-\frac i2\int d^4\mechanical{x}\,d^4\mechanical{y}\,
\signal{J}(\mechanical{x})\signal{\Delta}_F(\mechanical{x}-\mechanical{y})\signal{J}(\mechanical{y})\right].
$$

> [!note] Propagator convention
> In this part of Chapter 11, $\signal{\Delta}_F$ denotes the inverse Klein–Gordon kernel with momentum numerator one. The time-ordered two-point correlator is $\signal{G}^{(2)}=i\signal{\Delta}_F$. Earlier chapters sometimes call the correlator itself $\Delta_F$. The factor $i$ is retained explicitly here.

The four-point function is
$$
\signal{G}^{(4)}(1,2,3,4)
=\left.\frac1{i^4}
\frac{\delta^4\signal{Z}_0}
{\delta\signal{J}_1\delta\signal{J}_2\delta\signal{J}_3\delta\signal{J}_4}
\right|_{\signal{J}=0}.
$$
Let $f=\ln\signal{Z}_0=-i\int\signal{J}\signal{\Delta}_F\signal{J}/2$. The source calls this exponent $W_0$; it differs from the chapter's convention $\signal{W}=-i\ln\signal{Z}$.
Since $f$ is quadratic in the source,
$$
\frac{\delta f}{\delta\signal{J}(\mechanical{x})}
=-i\int d^4\mechanical{y}\,\signal{\Delta}_F(\mechanical{x}-\mechanical{y})\signal{J}(\mechanical{y}),
\qquad
\frac{\delta^2f}{\delta\signal{J}(\mechanical{x})\delta\signal{J}(\mechanical{y})}
=-i\signal{\Delta}_F(\mechanical{x}-\mechanical{y}).
$$
The first derivative vanishes at zero source, and derivatives of $f$ above second order vanish identically. Applying the product rule to $\signal{Z}_0=e^f$ therefore gives all pairings of the four insertion points, beginning with
$$
(i\signal{\Delta}_F(1,2))(i\signal{\Delta}_F(3,4)).
$$

All three pairings give
$$
\begin{aligned}
\signal{G}^{(4)}(1,2,3,4)
={}&(i\signal{\Delta}_F(1,2))(i\signal{\Delta}_F(3,4))\\
&+(i\signal{\Delta}_F(1,3))(i\signal{\Delta}_F(2,4))\\
&+(i\signal{\Delta}_F(1,4))(i\signal{\Delta}_F(2,3)).
\end{aligned}
$$
With $\signal{G}^{(2)}(\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y})=i\signal{\Delta}_F(\mechanical{x}-\mechanical{y})$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\signal{G}^{(4)}(1,2,3,4)
={}&\signal{G}^{(2)}(1,2)\signal{G}^{(2)}(3,4)\\
&+\signal{G}^{(2)}(1,3)\signal{G}^{(2)}(2,4)\\
&+\signal{G}^{(2)}(1,4)\signal{G}^{(2)}(2,3).
\end{aligned}
$$

#### Connected four-point function

The connected function is the full function minus its disconnected products. Each of the three terms above is a product of two two-point functions. Since the free vacuum also has a vanishing one-point function,
$$
\signal{G}_c^{(4)}\big|_{\mathrm{free}}=0.
$$
For a Gaussian free field, all connected correlators of order three and higher vanish. Interactions are required to generate them.

### Solution 11.3 — Fermionic and bosonic determinants

Use a consistent Euclidean convention and set $\mechanical{\hbar}=1$. For a real scalar, let
$$
K=-\partial_{\mathrm E}^2+\mechanical{m}^2.
$$
The Gaussian integral gives, up to the measure normalization,
$$
\signal{Z}_{\mathrm{scalar}}
=\int\mathcal D\signal{\phi}\,
e^{-\frac12\int\signal{\phi}K\signal{\phi}}
=(\det K)^{-1/2}.
$$
For a complex Dirac field, define
$D_{\mathrm E}=\gamma_{\mathrm E}^\mu\partial_\mu+\mechanical{m}$. The Grassmann integral gives
$$
\signal{Z}_{\mathrm{Dirac}}
=\int\mathcal D\bar{\signal{\psi}}\,\mathcal D\signal{\psi}\,
e^{-\int\bar{\signal{\psi}}D_{\mathrm E}\signal{\psi}}
=\det D_{\mathrm E}.
$$
For free commuting derivatives and Hermitian Euclidean gamma matrices,
$$
D_{\mathrm E}^\dagger D_{\mathrm E}
=(-\gamma_{\mathrm E}^\mu\partial_\mu+\mechanical{m})
(\gamma_{\mathrm E}^\nu\partial_\nu+\mechanical{m})
=K\mathbf1_4.
$$
Taking determinants over spinor and spacetime indices,
$$
|\det D_{\mathrm E}|^2
=\det(K\mathbf1_4)=(\det K)^4.
$$
The power four is the Dirac spinor multiplicity.

> [!note] Operator convention
> The supplied solution mixes Euclidean Gaussian notation with Lorentzian operators. In Lorentzian signature its displayed product is $(i\not{\partial}-\mechanical{m})(-i\not{\partial}-\mechanical{m})=(\Box+\mechanical{m}^2)\mathbf1_4$, rather than $(-\Box-\mechanical{m}^2)\mathbf1_4$. The Euclidean form above keeps the positive scalar kernel and fermion determinant in one convention. No spin–field-strength term occurs for ordinary commuting derivatives.

For the free Euclidean operator with positive mass and a consistent determinant phase, take the positive result
$$
\det D_{\mathrm E}=(\det K)^2.
$$
Thus the partition-function ratio requested in the exercise is
$$
\frac{\signal{Z}_{\mathrm{Dirac}}}{\signal{Z}_{\mathrm{scalar}}}
=(\det K)^{5/2}.
$$

#### Relative loop signs and multiplicity

The logarithms are
$$
\ln\signal{Z}_{\mathrm{Dirac}}=2\operatorname{Tr}\ln K,
\qquad
\ln\signal{Z}_{\mathrm{scalar}}=-\frac12\operatorname{Tr}\ln K.
$$
Their determinant contributions have relative coefficient $-4$: a Dirac field gives four times the magnitude and the opposite sign of one real scalar. The source calls this the “ratio of vacuum contributions”; it is a comparison of logarithmic coefficients, distinct from the ratio of partition functions above.

#### Supersymmetry

In unbroken supersymmetry, matched bosonic and fermionic degrees of freedom have equal masses and corresponding internal quantum numbers. Their determinant factors cancel when the operators and multiplicities match. Schematically, for a matched pair of factors,
$$
(\det\mathcal O_B)^{-1/2}
(\det\mathcal O_F)^{+1/2}=1
\qquad\text{if }\mathcal O_B=\mathcal O_F.
$$
The equal-mass comparison of one Dirac field and one real scalar alone does not cancel: their multiplicities differ. A Dirac field requires four matching real scalar degrees of freedom for this free determinant count. Supersymmetric cancellations can remove quadratic mass sensitivities; exact cancellation requires the unbroken matching conditions.

> [!note]
> The source asserts reality of the Lorentzian determinant after regularization. Its phase is not fixed by the determinant-modulus identity alone. The free Euclidean positive-mass convention used here makes that choice explicit. Supersymmetry also pairs states of different spin; “same quantum numbers” refers to the corresponding internal quantum numbers, not equal spin.

### Solution 11.4 — Grassmann Gaussian integral

For $n$ pairs of Grassmann variables and the Berezin measure convention of (11.19), the target is
$$
\int\prod_{i=1}^n d\bar\theta_i\,d\theta_i\,
e^{-\bar\theta_iM_{ij}\theta_j}
=\det M.
$$
Expand the exponential:
$$
e^{-\bar\theta_iM_{ij}\theta_j}
=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}
(\bar\theta_iM_{ij}\theta_j)^k.
$$
Nilpotency terminates the series. A nonzero integral needs every variable exactly once, so only the term with $k=n$, containing
$\bar\theta_1\theta_1\cdots\bar\theta_n\theta_n$, can survive.

The surviving Taylor term is
$$
\frac{(-1)^n}{n!}(\bar\theta_iM_{ij}\theta_j)^n.
$$
A nonzero term uses each barred index and each unbarred index once. Let $\sigma,\tau$ denote their permutations. Reordering the Grassmann variables supplies
$\operatorname{sgn}\sigma\,\operatorname{sgn}\tau$.
Choose the measure convention
$$
\int d\bar\theta_i\,d\theta_i\,\bar\theta_i\theta_i=-1,
$$
which makes the one-pair Gaussian equal to its matrix coefficient. The measure sign cancels the Taylor factor $(-1)^n$. Thus
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int\prod_i d\bar\theta_i\,d\theta_i\,
e^{-\bar\theta M\theta}
&=\frac1{n!}\sum_{\sigma,\tau}
\operatorname{sgn}\sigma\,\operatorname{sgn}\tau
\prod_{k=1}^nM_{\sigma(k),\tau(k)}\\
&=\sum_\pi\operatorname{sgn}\pi
\prod_{k=1}^nM_{k,\pi(k)}
=\det M.
\end{aligned}
$$
For each relative permutation $\pi=\tau\circ\sigma^{-1}$, the sum has $n!$ choices of $\sigma$, canceling the Taylor factorial.

> [!note] Factorial and measure
> The source drops $1/n!$ in its displayed double-permutation sum and then writes $n!$ times the Leibniz sum as the determinant. The Taylor factorial must remain until the redundant permutation sum cancels it. Reversing the Berezin measure changes its orientation sign; the convention must match the stated Gaussian identity.

### Solution 11.5 — One-loop effective potential in quartic theory

Expand about a constant background:
$$
\signal{\phi}=\signal{\phi}_{\mathrm{cl}}+\signal{\eta},
\qquad
\energy{V}(\signal{\phi})
=\frac{\mechanical{m}^2}{2}\signal{\phi}^2
+\frac{\signal{\lambda}}{4!}\signal{\phi}^4.
$$
The background-dependent squared mass is
$$
\mechanical{M}^2(\signal{\phi}_{\mathrm{cl}})
=\energy{V}''(\signal{\phi}_{\mathrm{cl}})
=\mechanical{m}^2+\frac{\signal{\lambda}}2\signal{\phi}_{\mathrm{cl}}^2.
$$
In Lorentzian form, the quadratic fluctuation action is
$$
\energy{S}[\signal{\phi}_{\mathrm{cl}}+\signal{\eta}]
=\energy{S}[\signal{\phi}_{\mathrm{cl}}]
+\frac12\int\signal{\eta}(-\Box-\mechanical{M}^2)\signal{\eta}
+\mathcal O(\signal{\eta}^3),
$$
after cancellation of the linear fluctuation term.

> [!note] Background condition
> For an arbitrary constant background, the action alone also has a linear term. In the effective-action construction the chosen source cancels it. Without that source, the displayed expansion requires a stationary background.

The Gaussian fluctuation integral gives
$$
\int\mathcal D\signal{\eta}\,
e^{\frac i2\int\signal{\eta}(-\Box-\mechanical{M}^2)\signal{\eta}}
\propto[\det(-\Box-\mechanical{M}^2)]^{-1/2},
$$
with the causal prescription understood. After Wick rotation, the one-loop potential density is
$$
\energy{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}
=\frac12\energy{\mu}^{2\varepsilon}
\int\frac{d^d\mechanical{k}_{\mathrm E}}{(2\pi)^d}
\ln\frac{\mechanical{k}_{\mathrm E}^2+\mechanical{M}^2}{\energy{\mu}^2},
\qquad d=4-2\varepsilon,
$$
up to a field-independent normalization.

Here $\mathcal V$ denotes the spacetime volume. The Wick rotation is $\mechanical{k}^0=i\mechanical{k}_{\mathrm E}^0$. Dimensional regularization gives
$$
\energy{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}
=-\frac{\mechanical{M}^4}{64\pi^2}
\left[
\frac1\varepsilon-\gamma_{\mathrm E}+\ln4\pi
-\ln\frac{\mechanical{M}^2}{\energy{\mu}^2}
+\frac32+\mathcal O(\varepsilon)
\right].
$$
The factor $\energy{\mu}^{2\varepsilon}$ in the preceding integral restores the four-dimensional unit. Subtracting $1/\varepsilon-\gamma_{\mathrm E}+\ln4\pi$ in the $\overline{\mathrm{MS}}$ scheme gives
$$
\boxed{
\energy{V}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(1)}
=\frac{[\mechanical{m}^2+\signal{\lambda}\signal{\phi}_{\mathrm{cl}}^2/2]^2}{64\pi^2}
\left[
\ln\frac{\mechanical{m}^2+\signal{\lambda}\signal{\phi}_{\mathrm{cl}}^2/2}{\energy{\mu}^2}
-\frac32
\right].
}
$$
The full potential through one loop is the classical potential plus this correction. The real logarithmic form assumes $\mechanical{M}^2>0$.

> [!note] Radiative symmetry breaking
> The source identifies this determinant as the Coleman–Weinberg form and states that the massless limit produces radiative symmetry breaking. The formula is the scalar one-loop contribution. For a single massless quartic scalar, a formal nonzero minimum requires large logarithms relative to the tree term, so this fixed-order expression alone does not establish a controlled broken vacuum. Other interactions and a consistent treatment of running couplings can change this conclusion.

### Solution 11.6 — Faddeev–Popov determinant in Coulomb gauge

Choose the gauge condition
$$
G[\electric{A}]=\boldsymbol\nabla\cdot\mathbf{\electric{A}}=0.
$$
Under $\electric{A}_\mu\mapsto\electric{A}_\mu+\partial_\mu\alpha$,
$$
G[\electric{A}^{\alpha}]
=\boldsymbol\nabla\cdot\mathbf{\electric{A}}+\nabla^2\alpha.
$$

#### (a) Determinant

The Faddeev–Popov factor is
$$
\Delta_{\mathrm{FP}}
=\left.\det\frac{\delta G[\electric{A}^{\alpha}]}{\delta\alpha}\right|_{G=0}
=\det(\nabla^2).
$$
It is independent of the photon field and can be absorbed into the normalization. This statement assumes boundary conditions or a residual-gauge prescription that removes zero modes.

#### (b) Ghost decoupling

Because $\nabla^2$ is field independent, the Abelian ghost fields have no photon interaction. Ghost loops therefore do not contribute to Green functions of the physical photon fields.

> [!note] Determinant sign
> The exercise uses $\det(-\nabla^2)$, whereas the supplied solution uses $\det(\nabla^2)$. The sign depends on the definition of the gauge function and gauge parameter. Both give a field-independent factor after the same zero-mode prescription.

#### (c) Propagator and Lorentz invariance

Invert the quadratic Maxwell operator
$O^{\mu\nu}=g^{\mu\nu}\Box-\partial^\mu\partial^\nu$
on fields with $\partial_i\electric{A}^i=0$. Its components are
$$
\begin{aligned}
\electric{D}^{00}(\mechanical{k})
&=\frac{i}{\lvert\mathbf{\mechanical{k}}\rvert^2},
&
\electric{D}^{0i}(\mechanical{k})&=0,\\
\electric{D}^{ij}(\mechanical{k})
&=\frac{i}{\mechanical{k}^2+i0}
\left(\delta^{ij}
-\frac{\mechanical{k}^i\mechanical{k}^j}
{\lvert\mathbf{\mechanical{k}}\rvert^2}\right).
\end{aligned}
$$
The temporal component is instantaneous and gives the Coulomb interaction. The spatial component propagates transverse photons. The propagator is not manifestly Lorentz covariant. In physical amplitudes, gauge invariance combines the instantaneous and noncovariant transverse terms to give gauge-independent, Lorentz-invariant observables.

### Solution 11.7 — Schwinger–Dyson equation for the photon propagator

Translation invariance of the functional measure gives
$$
0=\int\mathcal D\electric{A}\,
\frac{\delta}{\delta\electric{A}_\mu(\mechanical{x})}
\exp\left[
i\energy{S}_{\mathrm{QED}}
+i\int\electric{J}\cdot\electric{A}
+i\int(\bar\eta\signal{\psi}+\bar{\signal{\psi}}\eta)
\right],
$$
with the fermion integration understood. Here $\eta,\bar\eta$ are auxiliary Grassmann sources.

For the gauge-fixed Maxwell action,
$$
\energy{S}_{\mathrm{gf}}
=\int d^4\mechanical{x}
\left[-\frac14\electric{F}_{\rho\sigma}\electric{F}^{\rho\sigma}
-\frac1{2\xi}(\partial\cdot\electric{A})^2\right],
$$
the interacting action derivative is
$$
\frac{\delta\energy{S}_{\mathrm{QED}}}{\delta\electric{A}_\mu}
=
\left[g^{\mu\nu}\Box
-\left(1-\frac1\xi\right)\partial^\mu\partial^\nu\right]\electric{A}_\nu
-\electric{J}_{\mathrm{mat}}^\mu,
\qquad
\electric{J}_{\mathrm{mat}}^\mu
=\electric{e}\bar{\signal{\psi}}\gamma^\mu\signal{\psi}.
$$
Differentiate the functional identity once with respect to the photon source, then set the sources to zero. With
$\electric{G}^{(2)}_{\nu\alpha}=\langle T\electric{A}_\nu\electric{A}_\alpha\rangle$,
define the momentum kernel
$$
K^{\mu\nu}(\mechanical{k})
=\mechanical{k}^2g^{\mu\nu}
-\left(1-\frac1\xi\right)\mechanical{k}^\mu\mechanical{k}^\nu.
$$
Absorbing the matter-current insertion into the proper polarization gives
$$
\left[K^{\mu\nu}(\mechanical{k})
-\electric{\Pi}^{\mu\nu}(\mechanical{k})\right]
\electric{G}^{(2)}_{\nu\alpha}(\mechanical{k})
=-i\delta^\mu_\alpha.
$$

> [!note] Fourier-kernel sign
> The supplied solution prints $+i\delta^\mu_\alpha$ on the right with the displayed positive-$\mechanical{k}^2$ kernel. For the stated time-ordered correlator and Fourier convention, the free Feynman-gauge propagator is $-ig_{\nu\alpha}/(\mechanical{k}^2+i0)$, so this kernel requires $-i\delta^\mu_\alpha$. The position-space operator in the exercise has the opposite overall Fourier sign.

#### Transversality

The matter-current Ward identity implies
$$
\mechanical{k}_\mu\electric{\Pi}^{\mu\nu}(\mechanical{k})=0,
\qquad
\electric{\Pi}^{\mu\nu}(\mechanical{k})
=(g^{\mu\nu}\mechanical{k}^2-\mechanical{k}^\mu\mechanical{k}^\nu)
\electric{\Pi}(\mechanical{k}^2),
$$
with a regulator that preserves this identity. Inverting the transverse and longitudinal kernels gives
$$
\electric{G}^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}(\mechanical{k})
=\frac{-i}{\mechanical{k}^2[1-\electric{\Pi}(\mechanical{k}^2)]}
\left(g_{\mu\nu}-\frac{\mechanical{k}_\mu\mechanical{k}_\nu}{\mechanical{k}^2}\right)
-\frac{i\xi\,\mechanical{k}_\mu\mechanical{k}_\nu}{(\mechanical{k}^2)^2},
$$
with causal prescriptions understood. The longitudinal term is not dressed by the transverse polarization.

> [!note] Massless pole
> The source states that transversality leaves the photon massless at every order. In perturbative QED, the scalar polarization has no mass-generating $1/\mechanical{k}^2$ pole, so the photon pole remains at $\mechanical{k}^2=0$. Transversality alone, without this condition, is not a general proof of masslessness.

### Solution 11.8 — Effective-action Hessian and exact propagator

The Legendre transform is
$$
\energy{\Gamma}[\signal{\phi}_{\mathrm{cl}}]
=\signal{W}[\signal{J}]
-\int d^4\mechanical{x}\,\signal{J}(\mechanical{x})\signal{\phi}_{\mathrm{cl}}(\mechanical{x}),
\qquad
\signal{\phi}_{\mathrm{cl}}(\mechanical{x})
=\frac{\delta\signal{W}}{\delta\signal{J}(\mechanical{x})}.
$$
It follows that
$$
\frac{\delta\energy{\Gamma}}{\delta\signal{\phi}_{\mathrm{cl}}(\mechanical{x})}
=-\signal{J}(\mechanical{x}).
$$
Differentiate with respect to $\signal{J}(\mechanical{y})$:
$$
\int d^4\mechanical{z}\,
\frac{\delta^2\energy{\Gamma}}
{\delta\signal{\phi}_{\mathrm{cl}}(\mechanical{x})
\delta\signal{\phi}_{\mathrm{cl}}(\mechanical{z})}
\frac{\delta\signal{\phi}_{\mathrm{cl}}(\mechanical{z})}
{\delta\signal{J}(\mechanical{y})}
=-\delta^{(4)}(\mechanical{x}-\mechanical{y}).
$$
With $\signal{G}^{(2)}_c=\delta^2\signal{W}/\delta\signal{J}^2$ and
$\signal{\Gamma}^{(2)}=\delta^2\energy{\Gamma}/\delta\signal{\phi}_{\mathrm{cl}}^2$,
$$
\boxed{\int d^4\mechanical{z}\,
\signal{\Gamma}^{(2)}(\mechanical{x},\mechanical{z})
\signal{G}^{(2)}_c(\mechanical{z},\mechanical{y})
=-\delta^{(4)}(\mechanical{x}-\mechanical{y}).}
$$
Translation invariance turns the convolution into
$$
\signal{\Gamma}^{(2)}(\mechanical{p})\signal{G}^{(2)}_c(\mechanical{p})=-1.
$$
At tree level, in the stated Hessian convention,
$$
\signal{\Gamma}^{(2)}(\mechanical{p})=\mechanical{p}^2-\mechanical{m}^2,
\qquad
\signal{G}^{(2)}_c(\mechanical{p})=-\frac1{\mechanical{p}^2-\mechanical{m}^2+i0}.
$$
More generally,
$$
\signal{\Gamma}^{(2)}(\mechanical{p})
=\mechanical{p}^2-\mechanical{m}^2-\energy{\Sigma}(\mechanical{p}^2),
\qquad
\signal{G}^{(2)}_c(\mechanical{p})
=-\frac1{\mechanical{p}^2-\mechanical{m}^2-\energy{\Sigma}(\mechanical{p}^2)+i0}.
$$
The connected time-ordered field correlator, denoted here by $\signal{D}_{\mathrm{exact}}$, is instead
$$
\signal{D}_{\mathrm{exact}}(\mechanical{p})
=\frac1i\signal{G}^{(2)}_c(\mechanical{p})
=\frac{i}{\mechanical{p}^2-\mechanical{m}^2-\energy{\Sigma}(\mechanical{p}^2)+i0}.
$$

> [!note] Factors of i in the supplied solution
> The source gives $i(\mechanical{p}^2-\mechanical{m}^2)$ for the proper two-point function and $i/(\mechanical{p}^2-\mechanical{m}^2)$ for the propagator. Their product is $-1$, but these are the proper vertex with its extra $i$ and the time-ordered correlator. They are not the two functional derivatives defined in the preceding Legendre-transform proof. With $\signal{W}=-i\ln\signal{Z}$, its second derivative is $i$ times the connected correlator.

### Solution 11.9 — Unphysical photon modes in electron–positron annihilation

At tree level,
$e^+(\mechanical{p}_1)e^-(\mechanical{p}_2)\to
\gamma^*\to\mu^+(\mechanical{p}_3)\mu^-(\mechanical{p}_4)$,
and $\mechanical{q}=\mechanical{p}_1+\mechanical{p}_2$. The amplitude is
$$
i\signal{\mathcal M}
=\bar v(\mechanical{p}_1)(-i\electric{e}\gamma^\mu)u(\mechanical{p}_2)
\frac{-ig_{\mu\nu}}{\mechanical{q}^2+i0}
\bar u(\mechanical{p}_4)(-i\electric{e}\gamma^\nu)v(\mechanical{p}_3).
$$
The Feynman-gauge propagator contains time and longitudinal components, but the electromagnetic current is conserved:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{q}_\mu\bar v(\mechanical{p}_1)\gamma^\mu u(\mechanical{p}_2)
&=\bar v(\mechanical{p}_1)
(\not{\mechanical{p}}_1+\not{\mechanical{p}}_2)u(\mechanical{p}_2)\\
&=-\mechanical{m}_e\bar v u+\mechanical{m}_e\bar v u=0.
\end{aligned}
$$
The outgoing current obeys the same identity. Terms proportional to
$\mechanical{q}_\mu\mechanical{q}_\nu$ therefore vanish between these currents. The Born cross section, neglecting the electron mass in the kinematics, is
$$
\energy{\sigma}(e^+e^-\to\mu^+\mu^-)
=\frac{4\pi\electric{\alpha}^2}{3\mechanical{s}}
\sqrt{1-\frac{4\mechanical{m}_\mu^2}{\mechanical{s}}}
\left(1+\frac{2\mechanical{m}_\mu^2}{\mechanical{s}}\right).
$$
It agrees in different consistent gauge choices.

> [!note] Real and virtual polarizations
> The source concludes that only two transverse polarizations contribute. That count applies to an on-shell real photon. Here the exchanged photon is off shell with timelike $\mechanical{q}^2$, and the subspace orthogonal to $\mechanical{q}$ has three components. Current conservation removes gauge-dependent terms; it does not replace the virtual propagator by a universal sum over only two real-photon helicities.

### Solution 11.10 — Translation Ward identity in quartic theory

The action is invariant under constant translations
$\signal{\phi}(\mechanical{x})\mapsto\signal{\phi}(\mechanical{x}+\mechanical{a})$.
The associated Noether current is the energy–momentum tensor,
$$
\energy{T}^{\mu\nu}
=\partial^\mu\signal{\phi}\,\partial^\nu\signal{\phi}
-g^{\mu\nu}\energy{\mathcal L}.
$$

Conservation of the translation current gives the time-ordered Ward identity
$$
\partial_\mu^x
\left\langle T\,\energy{T}^{\mu\nu}(\mechanical{x})
\signal{\phi}(\mechanical{x}_1)\cdots\signal{\phi}(\mechanical{x}_n)\right\rangle
=-i\sum_{k=1}^n
\delta^{(4)}(\mechanical{x}-\mechanical{x}_k)
\partial_{x_k}^{\nu}
\left\langle T\,\signal{\phi}(\mechanical{x}_1)\cdots\signal{\phi}(\mechanical{x}_n)\right\rangle.
$$
For one scalar insertion, the right side vanishes in a translation-invariant vacuum because $\langle\signal{\phi}\rangle$ is constant. With two insertions, the contact terms contain derivatives of the scalar two-point function. They express momentum conservation at an energy–momentum-tensor insertion.

Near an isolated stable one-particle pole, the time-ordered propagator has the form
$$
\signal{D}_{\mathrm{exact}}(\mechanical{p})
=\frac{i\signal{Z}_{\mathrm{pole}}}
{\mechanical{p}^2-\mechanical{m}_{\mathrm{phys}}^2+i0}
+\text{terms regular at the pole}.
$$

> [!note] What translation invariance protects
> The source describes the physical pole mass as protected by translation invariance. Translation symmetry enforces momentum conservation; together with Lorentz symmetry it permits the relativistic one-particle dispersion relation. It does not prevent a radiative mass shift. The pole mass is independent of the choice of renormalization scheme when the same physical theory is held fixed, while the renormalized mass parameter and counterterm can change. The isolated-pole expression also assumes a stable particle.
