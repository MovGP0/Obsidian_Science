---
title: "Functional Determinants and Zeta Regularization"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Gaussian field integrals produce functional determinants. Zeta regularization defines them through analytic continuation of the operator spectrum.

The determinant measures the contribution of all independent quadratic fluctuation modes. With finitely many positive Euclidean eigenvalues, each real mode integral supplies an inverse square root of its eigenvalue. Multiplying those factors gives the determinant power. The difficulty in field theory is the unbounded number of high-frequency modes, not a new finite-dimensional Gaussian rule.

A Gaussian functional integral gives a factor
$[\det\mathcal{O}]^{-1/2}$.
For an operator $\mathcal{O}$ with eigenvalues $\{\lambda_n\}$,
$$
\det\mathcal{O}=\prod_n\lambda_n.
$$
This product generally diverges and needs regularization.
**Zeta regularization** defines
$$
\begin{aligned}
\ln\det\mathcal{O}&=-\zeta'_{\mathcal{O}}(0),\\
\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}(s)
&=\sum_n\lambda_n^{-s}
=\frac1{\Gamma(s)}
\int_0^\infty\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\,
t^s\operatorname{Tr}(e^{-t\mathcal{O}}).
\end{aligned}
\tag{11.10}
$$
The source considers $\mathcal{O}=-\square-\mechanical{m}^2$ on a four-dimensional torus.
It states that the spectrum is discrete, that the zeta function has an analytic continuation in the complex plane, and that its derivative is well defined at $s=0$.
In infinite space, $\zeta'_{\mathcal{O}}(0)$ contains the same ultraviolet structure as vacuum diagrams, illustrated in Fig. 11.1.
Zeta regularization provides a systematic treatment.

> [!note]
> The heat-kernel integral requires an appropriate elliptic operator and spectral prescription. For the scalar theory, one normally Wick rotates to the Euclidean operator $-\partial_E^2+\mechanical{m}^2$, treats zero modes separately, and uses analytic continuation where needed. The continued spectral zeta function is generally meromorphic, rather than entire. Dimensionful eigenvalues also require a reference scale when defining their logarithmic determinant. In infinite volume, the separate volume divergence must be handled as well.

For a positive finite spectrum, differentiating the spectral sum at zero gives minus the sum of the eigenvalue logarithms. Thus (11.10) reproduces the ordinary finite determinant before analytic continuation is needed. A zero eigenvalue instead makes its mode integral undamped and its inverse undefined; a zeta prescription does not remove the need to separate that mode.

At small positive heat-kernel parameter, high eigenvalues determine the ultraviolet behavior. Analytic continuation assigns a regulated determinant with a chosen reference scale. Physical parameter conditions are still needed to fix finite counterterms; a finite regularized expression alone is not a renormalization condition. This Euclidean spectral construction and its scale dependence are developed in [Hawking, 1977, §§2–4](https://projecteuclid.org/journals/communications-in-mathematical-physics/volume-55/issue-2/Zeta-function-regularization-of-path-integrals-in-curved-spacetime/cmp/1103900982.pdf).
