## Exercise 6.6 — Leading scalar cross section

In quartic scalar theory, the first-order two-to-two amplitude is the constant $\signal{\mathcal M}=-\lambda$. Integrate (6.9) over the full solid angle. Include $1/2!$ for the two identical final particles.

The supplied result is
$$
\mechanical{\sigma}_{\mathrm{tot}}
=\frac{\lambda^2}{32\pi\energy{s}},
\qquad \energy{s}\ge4\mechanical{m}^2.
$$
Check the dimensions.

## Solution 6.6 — Leading total cross section in quartic scalar theory

The first-order amplitude is constant, $\signal{\mathcal M}=-\lambda$. Before the identical-final-state factor, the CM differential cross section is
$$
\left(\frac{d\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\mechanical{\Omega}}\right)_{\mathrm{CM}}
=\frac1{64\pi^2\energy{s}}
\frac{|\mechanical{\mathbf p}_f|}{|\mechanical{\mathbf p}_i|}
|\signal{\mathcal M}|^2
=\frac{\lambda^2}{64\pi^2\energy{s}},
$$
where elastic scattering gives $|\mechanical{\mathbf p}_f|=|\mechanical{\mathbf p}_i|$.
Integrate over the full solid angle, $\int d\mechanical{\Omega}=4\pi$, and include $1/2!$ for the two identical final particles:
$$
\mechanical{\sigma}_{\mathrm{tot}}
=\frac12\,4\pi\,\frac{\lambda^2}{64\pi^2\energy{s}}
=\frac{\lambda^2}{32\pi\energy{s}}.
$$

In four dimensions $\lambda$ is dimensionless and $\energy{s}$ has the dimension of energy squared. Thus $\mechanical{\sigma}_{\mathrm{tot}}$ has unit $\unit{MeV^{-2}}$, the natural-unit dimension of an area.

The amplitude is constant, so the angular distribution in this CM frame is isotropic. Isotropy does not remove the identical-particle count: integrating one labeled outgoing direction over the whole sphere counts each unordered pair twice. Equivalently, integrate over one hemisphere without the extra factor one half, but do not use both prescriptions.

For a massive scalar, the displayed threshold value is the limit from $\energy{s}>4\mechanical{m}^2$. Exactly at zero incoming CM momentum, both the incident flux and the available two-body phase space vanish. Their equal-mass ratio has a finite limiting cross section; it does not describe a finite collision rate between particles with no relative motion. At large $\energy{s}$, the leading area scales as $1/\energy{s}$ while the coupling remains perturbative.
