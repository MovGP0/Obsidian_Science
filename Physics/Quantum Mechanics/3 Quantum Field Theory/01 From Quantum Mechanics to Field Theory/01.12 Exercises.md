### Solution 1.1 — Natural units and conversions

**(a)** With $\energy{\hbar}=\mechanical{c}=1$, the reduced Compton wavelength is
$$
\bar{\signal{\lambda}}_{\mathrm C}
=\frac{\energy{\hbar}}{\mechanical{m}_e\mechanical{c}}
=\frac1{\mechanical{m}_e}.
$$
Using $\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}\approx197.3\,\unit{MeV\,fm}$ and
$\mechanical{m}_e=0.511\,\unit{MeV}$ in natural units,
$$
\bar{\signal{\lambda}}_{\mathrm C}
=\frac{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}{\mechanical{m}_e\mechanical{c}^2}
=\frac{197.3\,\unit{MeV\,fm}}{0.511\,\unit{MeV}}
\approx386\,\unit{fm}.
$$

**(b)** The classical electron radius is
$$
\mechanical{r}_e=\electric{\alpha}\bar{\signal{\lambda}}_{\mathrm C}
=\frac{\electric{\alpha}}{\mechanical{m}_e}.
$$
With $\electric{\alpha}\approx1/137$,
$$
\mechanical{r}_e=\frac1{137}\times386\,\unit{fm}
\approx2.82\,\unit{fm}.
$$

**(c)** Use the source's rounded SI values
$$
\energy{\hbar}=1.0546\times10^{-34}\,\unit{J\,s},
\qquad
\mechanical{c}=2.998\times10^8\,\unit{m\,s^{-1}},
$$
and the conversions
$$
1\,\unit{J}=\frac1{1.602\times10^{-13}}\,\unit{MeV},
\qquad
1\,\unit{m}=10^{15}\,\unit{fm}.
$$
Then
$$
\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}
\approx
\frac{1.0546\times10^{-34}\times2.998\times10^8}
{1.602\times10^{-13}}\times10^{15}\,\unit{MeV\,fm}
\approx197.3\,\unit{MeV\,fm}.
$$

**(d)** A cosmic microwave background temperature
$\thermal{T}_{\mathrm{CMB}}=2.73\,\unit{K}$ gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}_{\mathrm{CMB}}
&=(8.617\times10^{-5}\,\unit{eV\,K^{-1}})
(2.73\,\unit{K})\\
&\approx2.35\times10^{-4}\,\unit{eV}
\approx0.235\,\unit{meV}.
\end{aligned}
$$
This is the typical microwave energy scale.

### Solution 1.2 — Gamma-matrix properties

**(a)** Start from
$$
\{\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu\}=2g^{\mu\nu}\mathbf1.
$$
The first contraction gives
$$
\gamma^\mu\gamma_\mu
=\frac12\{\gamma^\mu,\gamma_\mu\}
=\frac12(2g^\mu{}_\mu)\mathbf1
=4\mathbf1.
$$

For a one-slash contraction, write $\not{a}=a_\nu\gamma^\nu$ and use
$\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu=2g^{\mu\nu}\mathbf1-\gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma_\mu
&=(2g^{\mu\nu}\mathbf1-\gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu)\gamma_\mu\\
&=2\gamma^\nu-\gamma^\nu(4)
=-2\gamma^\nu.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus $\gamma^\mu\not{a}\gamma_\mu=-2\not{a}$.

For the two-slash contraction, take the matrix product
$A=\gamma^\nu\gamma^\rho$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma^\rho\gamma_\mu
&=(2g^{\mu\nu}\mathbf1-\gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu)
\gamma^\rho\gamma_\mu\\
&=2\gamma^\rho\gamma^\nu
-\gamma^\nu(\gamma^\mu\gamma^\rho\gamma_\mu)\\
&=2\gamma^\rho\gamma^\nu-\gamma^\nu(-2\gamma^\rho)\\
&=2(\gamma^\rho\gamma^\nu+\gamma^\nu\gamma^\rho)\\
&=2\{\gamma^\nu,\gamma^\rho\}
=4g^{\nu\rho}\mathbf1.
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore
$$
\gamma^\mu\not{a}\not{b}\gamma_\mu
=4(a\cdot b)\mathbf1.
$$

Repeating the preceding steps gives
$$
\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma^\rho\gamma^\sigma\gamma_\mu
=-2\gamma^\sigma\gamma^\rho\gamma^\nu,
$$
or
$$
\gamma^\mu\not{a}\not{b}\not{c}\gamma_\mu
=-2\not{c}\not{b}\not{a}.
$$

**(b)** The trace of one gamma matrix vanishes. Use cyclicity and
$\{\gamma^5,\gamma^\mu\}=0$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^\mu]
&=\operatorname{Tr}[(\gamma^5)^2\gamma^\mu]
=\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^5\gamma^\mu\gamma^5]\\
&=-\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^\mu(\gamma^5)^2]
=-\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^\mu].
\end{aligned}
$$
Hence $\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^\mu]=0$.

For two matrices,
$$
\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu]
=\frac12\operatorname{Tr}[\{\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu\}]
=\frac12(2g^{\mu\nu})\operatorname{Tr}[\mathbf1]
=4g^{\mu\nu}.
$$

**(c)** For an odd product of $2n+1$ gamma matrices, insert
$(\gamma^5)^2=\mathbf1$ and use cyclicity:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^{\mu_1}\cdots\gamma^{\mu_{2n+1}}]
&=\operatorname{Tr}[(\gamma^5)^2\gamma^{\mu_1}\cdots\gamma^{\mu_{2n+1}}]\\
&=\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^5\gamma^{\mu_1}\cdots
\gamma^{\mu_{2n+1}}\gamma^5].
\end{aligned}
$$

Anticommuting the final $\gamma^5$ through the odd product gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^{\mu_1}\cdots\gamma^{\mu_{2n+1}}]
&=(-1)^{2n+1}
\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^{\mu_1}\cdots
\gamma^{\mu_{2n+1}}(\gamma^5)^2]\\
&=-\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^{\mu_1}\cdots\gamma^{\mu_{2n+1}}].
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the trace vanishes.

**(d)** Apply
$\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu=2g^{\mu\nu}\mathbf1-\gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu$
repeatedly:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma^\rho\gamma^\sigma]
={}&2g^{\mu\nu}\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^\rho\gamma^\sigma]
-\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu\gamma^\rho\gamma^\sigma]\\
={}&8g^{\mu\nu}g^{\rho\sigma}
-2g^{\mu\rho}\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^\nu\gamma^\sigma]
+\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^\nu\gamma^\rho\gamma^\mu\gamma^\sigma]\\
={}&8g^{\mu\nu}g^{\rho\sigma}
-8g^{\mu\rho}g^{\nu\sigma}
+2g^{\mu\sigma}\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^\nu\gamma^\rho]\\
&-\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^\nu\gamma^\rho\gamma^\sigma\gamma^\mu].
\end{aligned}
$$
By cyclicity, the last trace equals the initial trace.
Therefore
$$
2\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma^\rho\gamma^\sigma]
=8\bigl(
g^{\mu\nu}g^{\rho\sigma}
-g^{\mu\rho}g^{\nu\sigma}
+g^{\mu\sigma}g^{\nu\rho}
\bigr),
$$
and hence
$$
\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma^\rho\gamma^\sigma]
=4\bigl(
g^{\mu\nu}g^{\rho\sigma}
-g^{\mu\rho}g^{\nu\sigma}
+g^{\mu\sigma}g^{\nu\rho}
\bigr).
$$

### Solution 1.3 — Negative mass squared and vacuum instability

**(a)** The equation is
$$
(\Box-\mechanical{\lambda}^2)\signal{\phi}=0.
$$
For plane waves $\signal{\phi}\propto e^{-i\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{x}}$, with
$\mechanical{p}^\mu=(\energy{E},\mechanical{\mathbf p})$,
$$
-(\energy{E}^2-\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p}\rvert^2)-\mechanical{\lambda}^2=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\energy{E}^2=\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p}\rvert^2-\mechanical{\lambda}^2.
$$
If $\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p}\rvert^2<\mechanical{\lambda}^2$, then $\energy{E}^2<0$ and
$$
\energy{E}=\pm i\signal{\Omega},
\qquad
\signal{\Omega}=\sqrt{\mechanical{\lambda}^2-\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p}\rvert^2}.
$$
This is the tachyonic dispersion relation. There is no real-frequency mass shell for $\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p}\rvert<\mechanical{\lambda}$ about this unstable vacuum.

**(b)** In that momentum range, a mode has the form
$$
\signal{\phi}\propto
e^{\signal{\Omega}\mechanical{t}}
e^{i\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf x}}.
$$
It grows exponentially with time.
The configuration $\signal{\phi}=0$ is a local maximum, or an unstable stationary point, of
$$
\energy{V}(\signal{\phi})=-\frac12\mechanical{\lambda}^2\signal{\phi}^2.
$$
Small fluctuations grow.

**(c)** Add a stabilizing term, for example
$$
\energy{V}(\signal{\phi})
=-\frac12\mechanical{\lambda}^2\signal{\phi}^2
+\frac{\signal{\lambda}_4}{4!}\signal{\phi}^4,
\qquad
\signal{\lambda}_4>0.
$$
The true minima then occur at $\signal{\phi}=\pm\signal{v}$.

The vacuum magnitude is
$$
\signal{v}^2=\frac{6\mechanical{\lambda}^2}{\signal{\lambda}_4}.
$$
Choosing one minimum spontaneously breaks the $\mathbb Z_2$ symmetry.
For fluctuations $\signal{\phi}=\signal{v}+\signal{\eta}$,
$$
\mechanical{m}_\eta^2
=\energy{V}''(\signal{v})
=-\mechanical{\lambda}^2+\frac{\signal{\lambda}_4}{2}\signal{v}^2
=2\mechanical{\lambda}^2>0.
$$
The instability disappears about the true minimum.

> [!note]
> The source calls this the Higgs mechanism in the global case. Here the calculation shows discrete spontaneous symmetry breaking and a stable massive scalar fluctuation. The gauge-field Higgs mechanism additionally gives a vector boson its longitudinal mode and mass.

### Solution 1.4 — Dirac Noether current

**(a)** For the infinitesimal phase transformation,
$$
\delta\signal{\psi}=i\signal{\alpha}\signal{\psi},
\qquad
\delta\bar{\signal{\psi}}=-i\signal{\alpha}\bar{\signal{\psi}},
\qquad
\delta\mechanical{x}^\mu=0.
$$
The asymmetric Dirac Lagrangian density
$$
\energy{\mathcal L}
=\bar{\signal{\psi}}(i\not{\partial}-\mechanical{m})\signal{\psi}
$$
depends on $\signal{\psi}$, its adjoint, and $\partial_\mu\signal{\psi}$, but not on derivatives of the adjoint.
Apart from the transformation parameter, the direct Noether expression is
$$
\electric{j}_{\mathrm{can}}^\mu
=\frac{\partial\energy{\mathcal L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu\signal{\psi})}
(i\signal{\psi})
=\bar{\signal{\psi}}(i\gamma^\mu)(i\signal{\psi})
=-\bar{\signal{\psi}}\gamma^\mu\signal{\psi}.
$$
Choose the overall current and generator sign convention so that
$$
\electric{j}^\mu=\bar{\signal{\psi}}\gamma^\mu\signal{\psi}.
$$

**(b)** The Dirac equation gives
$$
\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\signal{\psi}=-i\mechanical{m}\signal{\psi},
$$
and the adjoint equation gives
$$
(\partial_\mu\bar{\signal{\psi}})\gamma^\mu
=i\mechanical{m}\bar{\signal{\psi}}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_\mu\electric{j}^\mu
&=(\partial_\mu\bar{\signal{\psi}})\gamma^\mu\signal{\psi}
+\bar{\signal{\psi}}\gamma^\mu(\partial_\mu\signal{\psi})\\
&=(i\mechanical{m}\bar{\signal{\psi}})\signal{\psi}
+\bar{\signal{\psi}}(-i\mechanical{m}\signal{\psi})
=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

**(c)** With delta-normalized momentum-mode operators, the normal-ordered charge is
$$
\electric{Q}
=\int\frac{d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p}}{(2\pi)^3}
\sum_s\left[
a^{(s)\dagger}(\mechanical{\mathbf p})a^{(s)}(\mechanical{\mathbf p})
-b^{(s)\dagger}(\mechanical{\mathbf p})b^{(s)}(\mechanical{\mathbf p})
\right].
$$
For the one-particle state
$\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p},s\rangle_f=a^{(s)\dagger}(\mechanical{\mathbf p})\lvert0\rangle$,
$$
\electric{Q}\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p},s\rangle_f
=+\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p},s\rangle_f.
$$
For the one-antiparticle state,
$$
\electric{Q}\,b^{(s)\dagger}(\mechanical{\mathbf p})\lvert0\rangle
=-b^{(s)\dagger}(\mechanical{\mathbf p})\lvert0\rangle.
$$
Particles and antiparticles thus have opposite unit charges, $+1$ and $-1$.

> [!note]
> The source writes a $d^3k$ measure while using $\mathbf p$ in the operators; one consistent dummy momentum is used here. Its displayed charge formula assumes delta-normalized mode operators. With the covariant mode normalization of (A.48), the measure instead contains $1/(2\energy{E}_{\mathbf p})$. The charge eigenvalues are unchanged when the operator normalization and measure are used consistently.

### Solution 1.5 — Klein paradox

**(a)** For $\energy{E}=1.5\mechanical{m}$:

- If $\energy{V}_0<\energy{E}-\mechanical{m}=0.5\mechanical{m}$, the classical propagation regime has $0<\signal{T}<1$ and $\signal{R}=1-\signal{T}$.
- If $\energy{E}-\mechanical{m}<\energy{V}_0<\energy{E}+\mechanical{m}$, that is, $0.5\mechanical{m}<\energy{V}_0<2.5\mechanical{m}$, the step gives total reflection: $\signal{R}=1$, $\signal{T}=0$, with an evanescent wave in the second region.
- If $\energy{V}_0>\energy{E}+\mechanical{m}=2.5\mechanical{m}$, the Klein regime has $\signal{R}>1$, $\signal{T}<0$, and $\signal{R}+\signal{T}=1$.

**(b)** For $\energy{V}_0\gg\energy{E},\mechanical{m}$,
$$
\mechanical{k}_2\approx\energy{V}_0,
\qquad
\energy{E}-\energy{V}_0+\mechanical{m}\approx-\energy{V}_0,
$$
so
$$
\signal{q}_2\approx
\frac{\mechanical{k}_2}{\energy{E}-\energy{V}_0+\mechanical{m}}
\approx-1.
$$
Meanwhile $\signal{q}_1=\mechanical{k}_1/(\energy{E}+\mechanical{m})$ remains fixed.
Thus
$$
\signal{R}\approx
\left(\frac{\signal{q}_1+1}{\signal{q}_1-1}\right)^2,
\qquad
\signal{T}\approx-\frac{4\signal{q}_1}{(\signal{q}_1-1)^2}.
$$
For $\energy{E}=1.5\mechanical{m}$, $\signal{q}_1\approx0.447$:
$$
\signal{R}\longrightarrow
\left(\frac{1.447}{-0.553}\right)^2
\approx6.85,
\qquad
\signal{T}\approx-5.85,
\qquad
\signal{R}+\signal{T}=1.
$$

**(c)** The threshold is
$$
\energy{V}_0^{\mathrm{threshold}}=\energy{E}+\mechanical{m}.
$$
It is the lower boundary of the regime in which the second-region momentum becomes real again.
Just above this threshold, $\mechanical{k}_2\to0$.

**(d)** For $\energy{V}_0>\energy{E}+\mechanical{m}$, the effective energy
$\energy{E}-\energy{V}_0$ lies below $-\mechanical{m}$ in the negative-energy continuum of the source's Dirac-sea description.
The barrier can create particle–antiparticle pairs.

The negative current in Region II is interpreted as antiparticle flux, rather than as a single backward-moving particle.
Because an antiparticle has the opposite charge, current direction and propagation direction need not agree.
Equivalently, antiparticles can emerge from the barrier while particles accumulate on the left.
The source calls this Klein scattering and states that a consistent interpretation requires the many-particle formalism of field theory.

> [!note]
> In the Klein regime, $R$ and $T$ are signed current ratios in the stated one-particle matching calculation. They cannot both be interpreted as ordinary probabilities. For the semi-infinite step in the intermediate regime, an evanescent amplitude does not give a nonzero asymptotic transmitted flux.

### Solution 1.6 — Scalar energy–momentum tensor

**(a)** For
$$
\energy{\mathcal L}
=\frac12(\partial_\mu\signal{\phi})(\partial^\mu\signal{\phi})
-\frac12\mechanical{m}^2\signal{\phi}^2,
$$
the canonical tensor is
$$
\energy{T}^{\mu\nu}
=\frac{\partial\energy{\mathcal L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu\signal{\phi})}
\partial^\nu\signal{\phi}-g^{\mu\nu}\energy{\mathcal L}
=\partial^\mu\signal{\phi}\,\partial^\nu\signal{\phi}
-g^{\mu\nu}\energy{\mathcal L}.
$$

**(b)** The explicit tensor components are
$$
\energy{T}^{00}
=\dot{\signal{\phi}}^{\,2}-\energy{\mathcal L}
=\frac12\dot{\signal{\phi}}^{\,2}
+\frac12\lvert\boldsymbol\nabla\signal{\phi}\rvert^2
+\frac12\mechanical{m}^2\signal{\phi}^2,
$$
$$
\energy{T}^{0i}
=\dot{\signal{\phi}}\,\partial^i\signal{\phi}
=-\dot{\signal{\phi}}\,\partial_i\signal{\phi},
$$
$$
\energy{T}^{ij}
=\partial^i\signal{\phi}\,\partial^j\signal{\phi}
-g^{ij}\energy{\mathcal L}
=\partial^i\signal{\phi}\,\partial^j\signal{\phi}
+\delta^{ij}\energy{\mathcal L}.
$$

**(c)** Since the canonical momentum density is
$\mechanical{\pi}=\dot{\signal{\phi}}$,
$$
\energy{T}^{00}
=\frac12\mechanical{\pi}^2
+\frac12\lvert\boldsymbol\nabla\signal{\phi}\rvert^2
+\frac12\mechanical{m}^2\signal{\phi}^2
=\energy{\mathcal H}.
$$

**(d)** For the real plane-wave solution
$$
\signal{\phi}(\mechanical{x})
=\signal{a}e^{-i\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{x}}
+\signal{a}^*e^{+i\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{x}},
\qquad
\mechanical{p}^2=\mechanical{m}^2,
$$
the derivative is
$$
\partial_\mu\signal{\phi}
=-i\mechanical{p}_\mu\signal{a}e^{-i\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{x}}
+i\mechanical{p}_\mu\signal{a}^*e^{+i\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{x}}.
$$
Integrate over a large volume $\mechanical{V}$ and average over time so that oscillating terms vanish. The result is
$$
\mechanical{P}^\mu
=\int d^3\mechanical{\mathbf x}\,\energy{T}^{0\mu}
=\mechanical{p}^\mu\lvert\signal{a}\rvert^2\,2\signal{\omega}_{\mathbf p}\mechanical{V}.
$$
The normalization depends on the chosen plane-wave amplitude.
The total four-momentum is proportional to the mode four-momentum, with $\mechanical{P}^0>0$ for positive mode energy $\signal{\omega}_{\mathbf p}$.

Local conservation follows from the Klein–Gordon equation:
$$
\partial_\mu\energy{T}^{\mu\nu}
=(\Box\signal{\phi}+\mechanical{m}^2\signal{\phi})\partial^\nu\signal{\phi}
=0.
$$

> [!note]
> Unlike the momentum-space measure printed in the exercise statement, the supplied solution correctly integrates the local tensor over spatial coordinates, $d^3\mechanical{\mathbf x}$.

### Solution 1.7 — Weyl spinors and chirality

**(a)** In the Weyl representation, write
$$
\signal{\psi}=
\begin{pmatrix}\signal{\psi}_L\\\signal{\psi}_R\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The Dirac equation $(i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu-\mechanical{m})\signal{\psi}=0$ becomes
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
-\mechanical{m}&i(\partial_t+\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\boldsymbol\nabla)\\
i(\partial_t-\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\boldsymbol\nabla)&-\mechanical{m}
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}\signal{\psi}_L\\\signal{\psi}_R\end{pmatrix}
=0.
$$
For $\mechanical{m}=0$, the equations decouple:
$$
i(\partial_t+\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\boldsymbol\nabla)\signal{\psi}_R=0,
\qquad
i(\partial_t-\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\boldsymbol\nabla)\signal{\psi}_L=0.
$$

**(b)** In the standard Weyl convention,
$$
\gamma^5=
\operatorname{diag}(-\mathbf1_2,+\mathbf1_2).
$$
Hence
$$
\gamma^5\signal{\psi}_L=-\signal{\psi}_L,
\qquad
\gamma^5\signal{\psi}_R=+\signal{\psi}_R.
$$
The two chiral components have eigenvalues $-1$ and $+1$.

**(c)** Take a positive-energy plane wave
$$
\signal{\psi}_R\propto e^{-i(\energy{E}\mechanical{t}-\mechanical{p}\mechanical{z})},
\qquad
\mechanical{\mathbf p}=\mechanical{p}\,\hat{\mathbf z}.
$$
The right-chiral Weyl equation becomes
$$
(\partial_t+\sigma^3\partial_z)\signal{\psi}_R=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
(\energy{E}-\mechanical{p}\sigma^3)\signal{\psi}_R=0.
$$
For $\energy{E}=\mechanical{p}>0$, the spinor is a $\sigma^3$ eigenstate with eigenvalue $+1$.
The helicity operator is
$$
\mechanical{h}=\frac12\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mathbf p}
=\frac12\sigma^3.
$$
Thus the positive-energy massless right-chiral mode has helicity $+1/2$, with spin parallel to its motion.
The left-chiral mode has helicity $-1/2$.

**(d)** Charged weak currents couple to left-chiral fermion fields.
The corresponding massless antineutrinos have right helicity.
Parity exchanges $\signal{\psi}_L$ and $\signal{\psi}_R$, so an interaction coupled to only one chirality is not parity invariant.

The source identifies this with maximal parity violation, observed by Wu in the 1957 beta-decay experiment with cobalt-60.
The $V-A$ interaction structure makes this asymmetry explicit in the Lagrangian density.

> [!note]
> The source speaks broadly of weak interactions coupling only to the left component. This statement applies to the charged weak current. The neutral Z current also couples to right-chiral charged fermions. For nonzero mass, chirality and helicity are not identical.

### Solution 1.8 — Proca equation and the massless limit

**(a)** For
$$
\energy{\mathcal L}
=-\frac14\signal{F}_{\mu\nu}\signal{F}^{\mu\nu}
+\frac12\mechanical{m}^2\signal{A}_\mu\signal{A}^\mu,
$$
the derivatives are
$$
\frac{\partial\energy{\mathcal L}}{\partial\signal{A}_\nu}
=\mechanical{m}^2\signal{A}^\nu,
\qquad
\frac{\partial\energy{\mathcal L}}
{\partial(\partial_\mu\signal{A}_\nu)}
=-\signal{F}^{\mu\nu}.
$$
The Euler–Lagrange equation gives
$$
\partial_\mu\signal{F}^{\mu\nu}+\mechanical{m}^2\signal{A}^\nu=0.
$$

**(b)** Take the divergence and use the antisymmetry of the field tensor:
$$
\partial_\nu\partial_\mu\signal{F}^{\mu\nu}=0.
$$
Then
$$
\mechanical{m}^2\partial_\nu\signal{A}^\nu=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\partial_\nu\signal{A}^\nu=0
\qquad(\mechanical{m}\ne0).
$$
For $\mechanical{m}=0$, the divergence equation is an identity and does not impose the Lorenz condition.
The Maxwell potential retains gauge freedom.

**(c)** With $\partial_\mu\signal{A}^\mu=0$,
$$
\partial_\mu\signal{F}^{\mu\nu}
=\Box\signal{A}^\nu-\partial^\nu(\partial_\mu\signal{A}^\mu)
=\Box\signal{A}^\nu.
$$
Therefore every component satisfies
$$
(\Box+\mechanical{m}^2)\signal{A}^\nu=0.
$$

**(d)** The vector field has four components.
For $\mechanical{m}>0$, the constraint $\partial_\mu\signal{A}^\mu=0$ leaves three physical degrees of freedom: the three polarizations of a massive spin-one particle.

For $\mechanical{m}=0$, gauge invariance removes another unphysical mode.
For example, in temporal gauge take $\signal{A}^0=0$ and use the remaining gauge freedom to impose
$\boldsymbol\nabla\cdot\signal{\mathbf A}=0$.
Two transverse photon polarizations remain.
The extra freedom is the massless gauge symmetry
$$
\signal{A}_\mu\longrightarrow\signal{A}_\mu+\partial_\mu\alpha.
$$
