---
title: "Dirac's Strategy"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Requiring a first-order relativistic equation whose components satisfy the Klein–Gordon equation determines algebraic relations for Dirac's coefficients.

In 1928, Paul Adrien Maurice Dirac approached the Klein–Gordon problems with a new idea. The source connects the negative-energy solutions and the density that is not positive definite to the second-order time dependence. In the first-order Schrödinger equation, the probability density $\signal{\rho}=|\signal{\psi}|^{2}$ is automatically nonnegative. Dirac therefore asked whether an equation could be both first order in time and relativistically covariant.

Lorentz covariance requires a first-order time equation also to be first order in space. Seek an equation of the form

$$
i\frac{\partial\signal{\psi}}{\partial\mechanical{t}}
=\energy{\hat H}\signal{\psi}
=\left(-i\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\nabla+\beta\mechanical{m}\right)\signal{\psi}, \tag{1.26}
$$

where $\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha^{1},\alpha^{2},\alpha^{3})$ and $\beta$ must be determined. Require each component of $\signal{\psi}$ to satisfy the Klein–Gordon equation, and therefore the relativistic dispersion relation.

Apply the Hamiltonian twice:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\energy{\hat H}^{2}\signal{\psi}
&=\left(-i\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\nabla+\beta\mechanical{m}\right)^{2}\signal{\psi}\\
&=\left[-\alpha^{i}\alpha^{j}\partial_i\partial_j
-i\mechanical{m}(\alpha^{i}\beta+\beta\alpha^{i})\partial_i
+\beta^{2}\mechanical{m}^{2}\right]\signal{\psi}.
\end{aligned} \tag{1.27}
$$

The Klein–Gordon equation requires $\energy{\hat H}^{2}\signal{\psi}=(-\nabla^{2}+\mechanical{m}^{2})\signal{\psi}$. Thus,

$$
\frac{1}{2}\{\alpha^{i},\alpha^{j}\}=\delta^{ij}, \tag{1.28}
$$

$$
\{\alpha^{i},\beta\}=0. \tag{1.29}
$$

$$
\beta^{2}=1. \tag{1.30}
$$

Here $\{A,B\}=AB+BA$ is the anticommutator. Ordinary numbers cannot satisfy relations (1.28)–(1.30). Dirac recognized that $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ and $\beta$ must be matrices. The smallest matrices that satisfy these relations have dimension $4\times4$.
