title: "Physical Motivations"

> [!TLDR]
> Special relativity and changes in particle number require a theory that extends nonrelativistic quantum mechanics.

Nonrelativistic quantum mechanics, as formulated by Schrödinger, Heisenberg, and Dirac, is a central part of modern physics. Its predictions have been tested with high precision in many experiments, from the hydrogen atom to condensed matter. However, the theory has a basic limitation: it is not compatible with Einstein's special theory of relativity.

This is a problem of principle, rather than a problem of technical approximations. Schrödinger quantum mechanics is built around the equation

$$
i\energy{\hbar}\frac{\partial\signal{\psi}}{\partial\mechanical{t}}
=\energy{\hat H}\signal{\psi}, \tag{1.1}
$$

which treats time as an external parameter. The question is whether the dynamics and its predictions transform consistently between inertial observers.

> [!note]
> Treating time as a parameter does not by itself conflict with relativity. Relativistic quantum field theory also uses a time parameter in Hamiltonian evolution. The problem here is the nonrelativistic energy–momentum relation and the resulting failure of Lorentz covariance, not a requirement to make time a position-like operator. Relativistic Hamiltonian evolution is developed explicitly in [Tong, section 2.6](https://www.damtp.cam.ac.uk/user/tong/qft/qft.pdf).

A second difficulty is that standard quantum mechanics describes a fixed number of particles.

For $N$ particles, the Schrödinger equation describes a wavefunction $\signal{\psi}(\mechanical{\mathbf x}_1,\mechanical{\mathbf x}_2,\ldots,\mechanical{\mathbf x}_N,\mechanical{t})$ with $N$ spatial arguments. Rest energy sets the scale at which a fixed-particle description must be checked. Pair creation also requires an interaction and enough invariant energy to satisfy energy, momentum, and charge conservation; a large particle energy in one chosen frame is not sufficient. Particle number is then no longer conserved, and the fixed-particle-number formalism is no longer sufficient.

This chapter follows the development from ordinary quantum mechanics to quantum field theory. Free relativistic one-particle sectors are consistent, but a general fixed-particle description does not cover local interactions with pair creation. The source's broad claim of contradictions must be read with this scope. This limitation motivates a change in the basic description: quantized fields describe states with different particle numbers.

A useful low-energy check is that in an appropriate fixed-particle sector, a field theory should recover ordinary quantum mechanics when velocities are small and real particle creation is energetically excluded. Thus the new theory must explain both why the older description works and where its fixed-particle approximation fails.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\signal{\psi}$ | — | Wavefunction | The quantum state in the chosen representation. |
| $\mechanical{t}$ | $\unit{s}$ | Time | The external time parameter in the Schrödinger equation. |
| $\energy{\hbar}$ | $\unit{J\,s}$ | Reduced Planck constant | The constant that relates energy to angular frequency. |
| $\energy{\hat H}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Hamiltonian | The observable for the energy of the system. |
| $N$ | $1$ | Particle number | The fixed number of particles in standard quantum mechanics. |
| $\mechanical{\mathbf x}_i$ | $\unit{m}$ | Position | The position of particle i. |
