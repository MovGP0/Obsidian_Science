---
title: "Symmetries and Gauge Theories (1954–1973)"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Yang and Mills extended electromagnetic gauge invariance to non-Abelian groups, creating the framework used by the Standard Model.

In 1954, Yang and Mills generalized electromagnetic gauge invariance from the Abelian group $U(1)$ to non-Abelian symmetry groups. Yang–Mills theories based on groups such as $SU(2)$ and $SU(3)$ form the basis of the Standard Model.

These theories initially appeared to describe massless gauge bosons, in apparent conflict with the short range of the weak interaction. The strong interaction is different: gluons remain massless, while color confinement accounts for its finite effective range.

Spontaneous symmetry breaking supplied the electroweak solution. Contributions came from Nambu (1960), Goldstone (1961), Anderson (1963), and Higgs, Brout–Englert, and Guralnik–Hagen–Kibble (1964). The mechanism allows gauge bosons to acquire mass without destroying renormalizability.

Building on Glashow's work from 1961, Weinberg (1967) and Salam (1968) developed the unified electroweak theory. Its gauge group $SU(2)_L\times U(1)_Y$ is spontaneously broken to $U(1)_{\mathrm{em}}$. The predicted $W^{\pm}$ and $Z^{0}$ boson masses were about $80\,\unit{GeV}$ and $91\,\unit{GeV}$, respectively. The source associates their 1983 discovery at CERN with Rubbia and van der Meer.

In 1973, Fritzsch, Gell-Mann, and Leutwyler formulated quantum chromodynamics (QCD), the strong-interaction theory based on $SU(3)_C$, with gluons as gauge bosons. Gross, Politzer, and Wilczek discovered asymptotic freedom, work recognized by the 2004 Nobel Prize. It explains why quarks behave almost as free particles at high energies but remain confined inside hadrons at low energies.
