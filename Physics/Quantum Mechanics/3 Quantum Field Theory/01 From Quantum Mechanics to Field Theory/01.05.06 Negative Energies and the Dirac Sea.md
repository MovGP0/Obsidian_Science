---
title: "Negative Energies and the Dirac Sea"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Dirac proposed that all negative-energy electron states are occupied, so the Pauli exclusion principle prevents positive-energy electrons from falling into them.

Negative-energy solutions remain a problem. The Dirac solutions $\signal{v}^{(s)}(\mechanical{\mathbf p})e^{+i\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{x}}$ represent states with energy $-\energy{\omega}_{\mathbf p}<0$. For every momentum $\mechanical{\mathbf p}$, there are states with energies $\pm\energy{\omega}_{\mathbf p}$, and the negative branch extends to $-\infty$.

This spectrum has no lower bound. A positive-energy electron could emit a photon and fall into a negative-energy state, then emit another photon and continue without limit. Unlimited energy would be released, and no atom could be stable.

Dirac proposed a description now called the Dirac sea. Electrons are fermions and obey the Pauli exclusion principle. Suppose all negative-energy states are already occupied in the vacuum. The vacuum is then a sea of electrons filling every state with $\energy{E}<0$. A physical positive-energy electron cannot fall into these occupied states.

In this description, a sufficiently energetic photon, with $\energy{E}_{\gamma}>2\mechanical{m}_e\mechanical{c}^{2}\approx1.022\,\unit{MeV}$, can lift an electron from a negative-energy state to a positive-energy state and leave a hole in the sea. The hole behaves as a particle with charge opposite to the electron: the positron. Dirac predicted it in 1931, and Anderson discovered it experimentally in 1932.

The positron was the first antiparticle predicted by theory. Its discovery confirmed a prediction of relativistic quantum theory. However, the Dirac-sea description has several conceptual problems:

1. It works only for fermions because the Pauli exclusion principle is essential. Bosons have no equivalent mechanism.
2. The sea has infinite energy and charge, which must be removed by ad hoc procedures.
3. It cannot describe charged bosons such as charged pions, $\pi^{\pm}$. The Klein–Gordon theory has no Dirac sea.
4. It has no useful meaning for self-conjugate neutral particles such as the photon.

Quantum field theory gives the correct resolution. The negative-energy Dirac solutions are reinterpreted through antiparticle creation operators, rather than as physical states with negative energy.

## Example 1.2 — Nonrelativistic Limit of the Dirac Equation

Consider an electron in a weak external electromagnetic field described by the potential $\electric{A}^{\mu}=(\electric{\phi},\magnetic{\mathbf A})$.

With minimal coupling, the Dirac equation is

$$
\left[i\gamma^{\mu}(\partial_{\mu}-i\electric{e}\electric{A}_{\mu})-\mechanical{m}\right]\signal{\psi}=0. \tag{1.51}
$$

In the Dirac representation, write

$$
\signal{\psi}=e^{-i\mechanical{m}\mechanical{t}}
\begin{pmatrix}\signal{\varphi}\\\signal{\chi}\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The factor $e^{-i\mechanical{m}\mechanical{t}}$ removes the rapidly oscillating part. When the potentials and kinetic energy are much smaller than $\mechanical{m}$,

$$
\signal{\chi}\approx
\frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot(\mechanical{\mathbf p}-\electric{e}\magnetic{\mathbf A})}{2\mechanical{m}}
\signal{\varphi}\ll\signal{\varphi}.
$$

To first order in $\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$, the equations reduce to the Pauli equation:

$$
i\frac{\partial\signal{\varphi}}{\partial\mechanical{t}}
=\left[\frac{(\mechanical{\mathbf p}-\electric{e}\magnetic{\mathbf A})^{2}}{2\mechanical{m}}
+\electric{e}\electric{\phi}
-\frac{\electric{e}}{2\mechanical{m}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\magnetic{\mathbf B}\right]\signal{\varphi}. \tag{1.52}
$$

The term $-\electric{e}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\magnetic{\mathbf B}/(2\mechanical{m})$ describes the interaction of the magnetic dipole moment with $\magnetic{\mathbf B}=\nabla\times\magnetic{\mathbf A}$. The electron magnetic moment is

$$
\magnetic{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=\frac{\electric{e}}{2\mechanical{m}}\,2\mechanical{\mathbf S}
=\magnetic{g}_e\frac{\electric{e}}{2\mechanical{m}}\mechanical{\mathbf S}, \tag{1.53}
$$

with $\magnetic{g}_e=2$. This is the gyromagnetic factor predicted by Dirac theory. The source gives the experimental value as $\magnetic{g}_e\approx2.002319\ldots$. The small difference from two is explained by radiative corrections in quantum electrodynamics, treated in Chapter 8.
