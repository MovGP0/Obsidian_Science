---
title: "Covariance of the Schrödinger Equation"
---

> [!TLDR]
> The different orders of the time and space derivatives make the Schrödinger equation incompatible with Lorentz covariance.

Equation (1.1) treats space and time asymmetrically. For a free nonrelativistic particle, the Hamiltonian is $\energy{\hat H}=\mechanical{\hat{\mathbf p}}^{2}/(2\mechanical{m})$. In natural units, the Schrödinger equation becomes

$$
i\frac{\partial\signal{\psi}}{\partial\mechanical{t}}
=-\frac{\nabla^{2}}{2\mechanical{m}}\signal{\psi}. \tag{1.6}
$$

This equation is first order in time and second order in space. A Lorentz transformation mixes space and time. For this scalar Schrödinger wavefunction, a Lorentz boost introduces mixed derivatives that do not reproduce this free equation. Thus it is not Lorentz covariant. The order of derivatives is a warning in this particular equation, not a general test for all wave equations: the Dirac equation will be first order in both time and space.

The natural starting point for a relativistically covariant wave equation is the relativistic dispersion relation. In natural units,

$$
\energy{E}^{2}=\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{2}+\mechanical{m}^{2}, \tag{1.7}
$$

or $\mechanical{p}^{\mu}\mechanical{p}_{\mu}=\mechanical{m}^{2}$. Canonical quantization replaces $\energy{E}\to i\partial_0$ and $\mechanical{\mathbf p}\to-i\nabla$, or $\mechanical{p}^{\mu}\to i\partial^{\mu}$.

Equation (1.7) can be used in at least two ways. Each leads to a theory with advantages and serious problems. For a low-momentum check, expand the positive root of (1.7): the leading term is the rest energy, and the next term is the kinetic energy used in (1.6). Removing the uniform rest-energy phase recovers the nonrelativistic equation. This explains the approximation without making (1.6) an exact relativistic equation.

Before these relativistic equations are introduced, recall probability conservation in nonrelativistic quantum mechanics.

For a wavefunction $\signal{\psi}$, the probability density is $\signal{\rho}=|\signal{\psi}|^{2}=\signal{\psi}^{*}\signal{\psi}$. The probability current is $\signal{\mathbf j}=(\signal{\psi}^{*}\nabla\signal{\psi}-\signal{\psi}\nabla\signal{\psi}^{*})/(2\mechanical{m}i)$. The Schrödinger equation gives the continuity equation

$$
\frac{\partial\signal{\rho}}{\partial\mechanical{t}}+\nabla\cdot\signal{\mathbf j}=0, \tag{1.8}
$$

which keeps an initially normalized $\int d^{3}\mechanical{x}\,\signal{\rho}=1$ constant in time if the net probability flux through the boundary vanishes. Local continuity alone does not exclude probability leaving a finite region. The relativistic equations must be checked for an analogous structure and its physical meaning.
