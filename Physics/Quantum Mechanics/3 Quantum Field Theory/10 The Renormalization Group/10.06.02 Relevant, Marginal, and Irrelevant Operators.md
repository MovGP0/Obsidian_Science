---
title: "Relevant, Marginal, and Irrelevant Operators"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Operator dimensions classify couplings as relevant, marginal, or irrelevant. Irrelevant interactions become less important at low energy.

The Wilsonian analysis classifies operators in the Lagrangian.
Consider an operator $\signal{\mathcal{O}_n}$ with canonical mass dimension
$[\signal{\mathcal{O}_n}]=n$ in $d$ dimensions.
Its coupling $\signal{g_n}$ has dimension
$[\signal{g_n}]=d-n$.

Define the dimensionless coupling
$\signal{\tilde g_n}=\signal{g_n}\energy{\mu}^{n-d}$.
Its flow has a classical contribution from dimensional analysis and quantum contributions from loops:
$$
\energy{\mu}\frac{\partial\signal{\tilde g_n}}{\partial\energy{\mu}}
=(n-d)\signal{\tilde g_n}+\text{loop contributions}.
$$
First hold the dimensionful coefficient fixed and vary the energy used to form the dimensionless coupling. For a dimension-six operator in four dimensions, the dimensionless coefficient contains two powers of energy and therefore shrinks as energy decreases. For a mass-squared deformation, the dimensionless coefficient instead contains inverse energy squared and grows. These checks fix the infrared meaning of the signs.

Canonical dimensions classify perturbations near the Gaussian fixed point. At an interacting fixed point, anomalous dimensions and mixing between operators can change the eigenperturbations and their scaling exponents. A classically marginal direction needs its loop terms even at weak coupling, because its classical term is zero.

At weak coupling, the classical term dominates.
The sign of $n-d$ gives the classification:

| Type | Condition | Infrared behavior | Examples in d = 4 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Relevant | $n<d$ | Grows toward the infrared. | Mass term $\mechanical{m}^2\signal{\phi}^2$, with operator $\signal{\phi}^2$ of dimension $n=2$. |
| Marginal | $n=d$ | Classically invariant. | $\signal{\lambda}\signal{\phi}^4$, with $n=4$. |
| Irrelevant | $n>d$ | Decreases toward the infrared. | $\signal{\phi}^6$, with $n=6$. |

Irrelevant operators become less important at low energy.
An effective field theory can therefore describe low-energy behavior to a chosen accuracy with a finite number of operators, while effects of ultraviolet details are suppressed.
This is the conceptual basis of the **effective field theory (EFT)** approach.

> [!note]
> The source calls $d$ the number of spatial dimensions. For relativistic Lagrangian mass-dimension counting, $d$ is the spacetime dimension; a classical Euclidean statistical field theory can instead use a spatial integration dimension. The dimensions $n$ in the examples refer to the field operators, excluding their coupling coefficients. An EFT retains higher-dimension operators when the required accuracy makes their suppressed effects relevant.

## Example 10.5: Universality of phase transitions

The Wilsonian approach explains universality.
Microscopically different systems—iron, carbon dioxide near its critical point, and binary liquid mixtures—can show the same critical exponents when they belong to the same universality class.
Near the critical point, the long-distance behavior controls these exponents.

In this description, the relevant operators control the approach to critical behavior, while microscopic differences carried by irrelevant operators flow toward zero.
The RG fixed point determines the critical exponents.

> [!note]
> Marginal directions can require additional analysis. Universality concerns the shared long-distance scaling properties, rather than equality of every microscopic parameter or observable normalization.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\signal{\mathcal{O}_n}$ | — | Local field operator | The operator has canonical mass dimension n. |
| $\signal{g_n}$ | — | Dimensionful operator coupling | Its mass dimension is d minus n and therefore varies with the operator. |
| $\signal{\tilde g_n}$ | $1$ | Dimensionless operator coupling | The coupling multiplied by the appropriate power of the renormalization scale. |
| $\energy{\mu}$ | $\unit{MeV}$ | Renormalization scale | The scale used to define the dimensionless coupling. |
| $\mechanical{m}$ | $\unit{MeV}$ | Mass parameter | The mass in the relevant scalar mass term. |
| $\signal{\phi}$ | $\unit{MeV}$ | Scalar field | The scalar field in the four-dimensional examples. |
| $\signal{\lambda}$ | $1$ | Quartic scalar coupling | The coefficient of the classically marginal quartic interaction. |
