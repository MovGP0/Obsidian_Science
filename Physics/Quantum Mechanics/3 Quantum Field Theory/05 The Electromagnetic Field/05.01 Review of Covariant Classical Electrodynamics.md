## Chapter introduction

> “From a long view of the history of mankind, there can be little doubt that the most significant event of the 19th century will be judged as Maxwell's discovery of the laws of electrodynamics.”
>
> — Richard P. Feynman

The electromagnetic field is the historical and conceptual prototype of a gauge theory. Its covariant formulation goes back to Maxwell (1865) and provides a reference for the quantization of vector fields.

The question for quantization is why four potential components do not require four independent photon oscillators. The classical field equations already contain the answer: some equations constrain initial data, and some changes of potential leave the fields unchanged. Work in flat spacetime with smooth fields and specify boundary conditions when solving for a potential.

Gauge symmetry introduces redundant degrees of freedom and makes the standard canonical procedure more difficult. This chapter develops two complementary approaches:

- **Coulomb gauge**, or transverse quantization, removes the redundant variables at the cost of manifest covariance.
- **Covariant Gupta–Bleuler quantization** keeps covariance but introduces states with negative norm.

The negative-norm states in the covariant construction belong to an auxiliary state space. They are not interpreted as observable photons; the physical-state condition and the removal of null states are essential parts of that construction.

## Covariant classical electrodynamics

First use the field tensor to fix the electric and magnetic sign conventions. Then read Maxwell's equations to see how current conservation follows. Finally, use gauge invariance to distinguish a new physical field from a new description of the same field. A useful reconstruction check is to change the potential by a gradient and confirm that both tensor equations remain unchanged.
