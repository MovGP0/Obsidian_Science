---
title: "Gauge Orbits and Gauge Fixing"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Gauge-equivalent Yang–Mills fields describe the same physics. A naive functional integral counts each gauge orbit repeatedly.

The Yang–Mills Lagrangian density (12.5) has local gauge symmetry.
For every function $U(\mechanical{x})\in SU(N)$, the configurations
$\signal{A}_\mu$ and its gauge transform $\signal{A}_\mu^U$ describe the same physics.
A naive functional integral is
$$
\signal{Z}=\int\mathcal{D}\signal{A}_\mu^a\,
e^{i\energy{S}_{\mathrm{YM}}[\signal{A}]}.
\tag{12.9}
$$

The naive integral counts the same physical configuration infinitely many times, once for each gauge transformation.
The equivalent configurations form **gauge orbits** in functional space.

The repeated directions also explain a practical failure: the quadratic action cannot distinguish a small pure-gauge change from no change, so its kernel has zero modes and has no ordinary inverse propagator. A gauge condition removes these redundant directions locally; it does not add a physical restoring force.

Here gauge equivalence means transformations compatible with the stated boundary conditions and with the redundancy being quotiented. Transformations that act on boundary charges require separate treatment. The normalized expectation value of a gauge-invariant insertion should not depend on the local representative. A gauge-field component at a fixed point is different: it is representative dependent and need not have a gauge-independent expectation value.

Geometrically, the configuration space $\mathcal{A}$ lies over the orbit space, or **moduli space**, $\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{G}$, where $\mathcal{G}$ is the gauge group.
The integral (12.9) must select one representative from each orbit.
This is the purpose of **gauge fixing**.

> [!note]
> This geometric description is local. Gauge conditions can have multiple representatives on an orbit, known as Gribov copies, and some fields have stabilizers. A single global representative is not automatically available.
