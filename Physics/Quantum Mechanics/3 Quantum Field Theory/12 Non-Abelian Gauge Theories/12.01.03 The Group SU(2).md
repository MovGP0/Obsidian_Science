---
title: "The Group SU(2)"
---

> [!TLDR]
> SU(2) has three generators given by half the Pauli matrices. It is the simply connected double cover of the rotation group SO(3).

$SU(2)$ is the group of unitary $2\times2$ matrices with determinant one.
Its three generators are $T^a=\sigma^a/2$, where the Pauli matrices are
$$
\sigma^1=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\sigma^2=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\sigma^3=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The structure constants are $f^{abc}=\varepsilon^{abc}$, the three-dimensional Levi-Civita tensor:
$$
\left[\frac{\sigma^a}{2},\frac{\sigma^b}{2}\right]
=i\varepsilon^{abc}\frac{\sigma^c}{2}.
$$
$SU(2)$ is locally isomorphic to $SO(3)$, the group of rotations in three dimensions, but their topologies differ:
$$
SU(2)\cong S^3
$$
is simply connected, whereas
$$
SO(3)\cong\mathbb{RP}^3,
\qquad
\pi_1(SO(3))=\mathbb{Z}_2.
$$

The Pauli matrices give a direct local check: the commutator of the first two half-Pauli matrices is $i$ times the third. They therefore fix both the sign and scale of the structure constants used later. Three generators mean three connection components in the internal space, not three components of a fundamental doublet.

Local agreement with rotations does not identify the two groups globally. Exponentiate the third generator: a full turn gives minus the identity on the doublet, while two full turns give the identity. The adjoint action is unchanged by this minus sign. This explains how the same Lie algebra can act on doublets and on ordinary three-component vectors, and why the representation matters when specifying matter fields.
