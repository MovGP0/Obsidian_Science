---
title: "Helicity and Chirality"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Helicity measures spin along the direction of motion; it agrees with chirality in the massless limit.

The helicity operator is the spin projection along the direction of motion:
$$
\mechanical{h}=\frac{\boldsymbol\Sigma\cdot\hat{\mechanical{\mathbf p}}}{2}
=\frac12
\begin{pmatrix}
\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mechanical{\mathbf p}}&0\\
0&\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mechanical{\mathbf p}}
\end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.69}
$$
Its eigenvalues are $\mechanical{h}=\pm\frac12$. Chirality and helicity agree as $\mechanical{m}\to0$, or in the ultrarelativistic regime $\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p}\rvert\gg\mechanical{m}$. For a right-chiral particle with $\gamma^5\signal{\psi}_R=+\signal{\psi}_R$, this gives $\mechanical{h}\signal{\psi}_R=+\frac12\signal{\psi}_R$.

For $\mechanical{m}\ne0$, chirality and helicity are different concepts. Chirality is the $\gamma^5$ eigenvalue and is invariant under proper Lorentz transformations. Helicity depends on the observer's frame of motion.

> [!note]
> Exact identification with a helicity eigenstate requires the massless limit. At finite mass, a chiral component is not by itself a solution for a free massive particle.

| Regime | Condition | Chirality and helicity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ultrarelativistic limit | $\mechanical{m}\ll\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p}\rvert$ | Approximately equal for particle states. |
| Nonrelativistic regime | $\mechanical{m}\gg\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p}\rvert$ | Different. |

The sketch gives a qualitative comparison, with no numerical threshold or vertical scale.

The massless identification compares the sign of twice the particle helicity with the chirality eigenvalue; the numerical eigenvalues themselves differ by a factor of two. For antiparticle states the chirality label of the creating field component has the opposite relation to physical helicity. Track whether “chirality” labels a field or an external spinor. See [Dreiner, Haber, and Martin, equation (G.4.22)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0812.1594#page=218).

> [!note] Scope of the massless identification
> The massless requirement above concerns a state that is both a pure chiral mode and a free energy eigenmode. A massive free particle can still have definite helicity, and each chiral projection of that helicity spinor retains its helicity. The projected component alone is not a full massive solution. Thus “not an exact identification” does not mean “no helicity eigenstates at finite mass.”

An observer can overtake a massive particle along its motion: the momentum reverses while its spin direction need not, so helicity changes. No finite boost overtakes a massless particle. At zero momentum the unit vector in (4.69) is undefined, so helicity has no rest-frame value.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\mechanical{h}$ | $1$ | Helicity | The spin projection along the momentum direction. |
| $\mechanical{\mathbf p}$ | $\unit{MeV}$ | Momentum | The particle momentum; its hat denotes a unit vector. |
| $\mechanical{m}$ | $\unit{MeV}$ | Mass | The particle mass. |
| $\signal{\psi}_R$ | $\unit{MeV^{3/2}}$ | Right-chiral field | The component with positive chirality. |
