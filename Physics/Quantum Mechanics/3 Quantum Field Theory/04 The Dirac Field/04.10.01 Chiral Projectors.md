---
title: "Chiral Projectors"
---

> [!TLDR]
> The right- and left-chiral projectors separate a Dirac spinor into two complementary components.

The chiral projectors are
$$
P_R=\frac{1+\gamma^5}{2},\qquad P_L=\frac{1-\gamma^5}{2}. \tag{4.63}
$$
They satisfy $P_R^2=P_R$, $P_L^2=P_L$, $P_RP_L=0$, and $P_R+P_L=1$. Every spinor decomposes as
$$
\signal{\psi}=P_L\signal{\psi}+P_R\signal{\psi}
\equiv\signal{\psi}_L+\signal{\psi}_R. \tag{4.64}
$$

The components $\signal{\psi}_L$ and $\signal{\psi}_R$ are the left-handed and right-handed Weyl spinors. In the chiral (Weyl) representation,
$$
\signal{\psi}=\begin{pmatrix}\signal{\psi}_L\\\signal{\psi}_R\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\gamma^5\signal{\psi}
=\begin{pmatrix}-\signal{\psi}_L\\+\signal{\psi}_R\end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.65}
$$

The projector identities follow from gamma five squared being the identity. Applying either projector twice must give the same component, and applying the opposite projector afterward must give zero. These are useful basis-independent tests before using the diagonal Weyl form.

In (4.64) the projected objects are four-component spinors with only two independent components. In the block notation (4.65), the same symbols denote their two-component nonzero blocks. Keeping that change of notation explicit prevents a dimension mismatch when multiplying matrices.

Chiral projection can be defined for a massive field, but a projected component alone does not obey an independent massive Dirac equation: the mass couples it to the other component. Chirality is therefore a representation label even where it is not a separately conserved free propagation label.
