> [!TLDR]
> The time-ordered vacuum correlation function gives the scalar propagation amplitude for either time ordering.

The Feynman propagator is interpreted as a propagation amplitude. For $\mechanical{x}^{0}>\mechanical{y}^{0}$, it equals $\langle0|\signal{\phi}(\mechanical{x})\signal{\phi}(\mechanical{y})|0\rangle$.

This is the amplitude for a particle created at the earlier event to be found at the later event. For reversed ordering, $\signal{\Delta}_{F}(\mechanical{x}-\mechanical{y})=\langle0|\signal{\phi}(\mechanical{y})\signal{\phi}(\mechanical{x})|0\rangle$, interpreted as an antiparticle propagating forward from the first event to the second. For a real scalar field, particle and antiparticle coincide.

The point-insertion language above is shorthand for a field correlation. A field acting at a point is not a normalizable position eigenstate of one particle, and this dimensionful propagator is not a probability bounded by one. A detection prediction also needs a preparation, detector coupling, and a normalized state or scattering setup. For the reverse time order, the earlier event is x and the later event is y; the antiparticle interpretation follows that order.

## Remark 3.12 — Feynman Propagator and Causality

The Feynman function need not vanish at spacelike separation, unlike the commutator function. This does not violate causality. It is an auxiliary correlation function in perturbation theory, not itself an observable. Physical observables, including scattering quantities, must obey causality.

To test the causality statement, compare the two vacuum orderings at a spacelike separation. They can be individually nonzero, yet their difference is zero. A controllable local response depends on that commutator, so the nonzero correlation alone does not transmit a message. The free real-field calculation supplies no detector data or measured travel time; its interpretation is a consequence of the model and operator algebra.
