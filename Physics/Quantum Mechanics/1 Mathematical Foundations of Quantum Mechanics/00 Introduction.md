title: Introduction

> [!TLDR]
> This book develops the mathematical structure of quantum mechanics step by step. It connects analysis, linear algebra, operator notation, Fourier analysis, and probability in one consistent framework.

To understand quantum mechanics, you must first understand its mathematical structure. This book was written with that aim. It is not a quantum physics textbook in the usual sense. It does not cover the hydrogen atom, the Pauli exclusion principle, or the band structure of crystals. Its aim is more fundamental: to explain the mathematics that supports the theory. Without this framework, quantum mechanics becomes a collection of rules that seem difficult to explain. A rigorous formulation of quantum mechanics needs tools that introductory first-year university courses in analysis and linear algebra often do not fully develop. These include infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, linear operators and their adjoints, eigenvalue theory, and Fourier transforms. These tools are parts of one consistent formalism, rather than separate techniques. As a result, many students start their first quantum mechanics course with gaps in their preparation. They can work with Pauli matrices or calculate Gaussian integrals. But they do not yet understand the structure that explains why these tools work. Without this structure, the theory seems to be a collection of methods chosen for each problem. This book was written to close that gap. It builds the required mathematical language step by step, so that quantum mechanics becomes a consistent and natural framework.

The book has five parts.

**Part I (Chapters 1–3)** establishes the foundations of analysis: complex numbers and Euler's formula, differential and integral calculus and their applications to operators, ordinary differential equations, and the eigenvalue problem.

**Part II (Chapters 4–7)** develops linear algebra in its complex form: vector spaces over $\mathbb{C}$, linear operators and matrices, eigenvalues and unitary diagonalization, and Hilbert space as an infinite-dimensional completion.

**Part III (Chapters 8–10)** introduces the notation and algebra specific to quantum mechanics. It covers Dirac notation (bras, kets, and inner products), commutators and the uncertainty principle, unitary operators, and the connection between symmetries and conserved quantities.

**Part IV (Chapters 11–12)** covers Fourier analysis: Fourier series as expansions in eigenstates, and the Fourier transform as a change of basis between position space and momentum space.

**Part V (Chapters 13–14)** completes the picture with probability theory and the calculation of expectation values and quantum uncertainties.

Each chapter ends with a “Common Errors” section. This section explains and corrects the errors that students make most often. Each chapter also has exercises at three levels: basic, intermediate, and challenge. The book is designed to be read in order, because each chapter builds on the previous material. Readers who already know some topics can also read the text in a different order and return to earlier chapters when they find gaps in their knowledge.

The “QM Box” sections have the heading “Quantum Mechanics”. They connect the mathematical tools to their meaning and use in the formalism of quantum mechanics. Readers who use the book to prepare for a quantum mechanics course can thus follow a clear link between mathematical development and physical interpretation.

The challenge exercises need more effort. Some can require tools that go slightly beyond the material presented in the text. It is not necessary to solve them all. They are for readers who want to study the subject in more depth and develop a sound, independent understanding of the material.

The text assumes a basic knowledge of elementary trigonometry, differential and integral calculus, and $2 \times 2$ systems of linear equations.
