> [!TLDR]
> A commutator records how the order of two operators changes their product. Commutator algebra connects compatibility, uncertainty, conservation laws, and quantum energy spectra.

The first task is algebraic: compare applying two maps in opposite orders. Only after choosing observables, states, and domains can that difference be used in a physical prediction. In particular, the commutator is not itself the probability of a sequence of measurement outcomes; ideal measurements involve projectors and conditional state updates.

Start with the product rules, then test them on the canonical position–momentum pair. Compatibility asks whether a complete common eigenbasis exists in the matrix setting. Uncertainty instead asks about spreads in a particular prepared state. The ladder section uses a special Hamiltonian to turn the algebra into a spectrum. Keep these questions separate: nonzero operator commutators need not have nonzero expectations in every state, so a state-dependent uncertainty bound may vanish even when the observables do not commute.

Multiplication of numbers is commutative: $ab=ba$. Matrix multiplication generally is not: $AB\ne BA$.

In quantum mechanics, noncommutativity is central to the uncertainty principle, energy quantization, and selection rules for optical transitions. The **commutator** describes the failure of two operators to commute.

> [!note]
> The source describes noncommutativity as the mechanism that produces all these effects. The commutator algebra is essential, but a specific spectrum or selection rule also depends on the Hamiltonian, its representation, and the physical system. The commutator alone does not determine every such result.
