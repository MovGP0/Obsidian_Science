title: "Common Errors"

> [!TLDR]
> Do not factor operator exponentials without checking commutators, or confuse relative phases with global phases. Unitary evolution preserves information in the complete closed-system description, while different observable probabilities can still change.

## Error 1 — Assume that operator exponentials always factor

In general, do not replace $e^{\hat A+\hat B}$ with $e^{\hat A}e^{\hat B}$. If $[\hat A,\hat B]=0$, the factorization is valid in the finite-matrix setting. More generally, the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff expansion gives

$$
e^{\hat A}e^{\hat B}
=e^{\hat A+\hat B+\frac12[\hat A,\hat B]+\cdots},
$$

where the omitted terms contain higher nested commutators. The noncommuting case is substantially more complicated, and convergence or domain conditions must be checked.

> [!note]
> The source says that the simple factorization holds “only” for commuting operators. Commutation is the standard sufficient condition. Equality for particular exponentials is not, by itself, a general test that their generators commute; exceptional equalities can occur.

## Error 2 — Confuse global phase with relative phase

A global phase transformation,

$$
|\psi\rangle\longmapsto e^{i\signal{\phi}}|\psi\rangle,
$$

does not change measurement probabilities. A relative-phase transformation,

$$
\alpha|1\rangle+\beta|2\rangle
\longmapsto\alpha|1\rangle+e^{i\signal{\phi}}\beta|2\rangle,
$$

can change interference terms and expectation values. Keep these cases separate.

## Error 3 — Infer that constant energy probabilities mean no evolution

For a time-independent Hamiltonian, $e^{-i\hat H\signal{t}/\mechanical{\hbar}}$ leaves energy probabilities unchanged. An energy eigenstate gains only a global phase. A superposition, however, can acquire changing relative phases, so probabilities for other observables, such as position, can change.

Time evolution changes the state in Hilbert space even when its energy-basis probability distribution remains constant.

## Error 4 — Use position as the generator of a spatial translation

The translation operator is

$$
\hat T(\mechanical{a})=e^{-i\mechanical{a}\hat p/\mechanical{\hbar}}.
$$

Its generator is momentum, not position. Similarly, angular momentum generates spatial rotations, and energy generates time evolution. The generators are the associated physical observables.

## Quantum mechanics — Unitarity and information

Unitary evolution is reversible. If the initial state is known, the evolved state is

$$
|\psi(\signal{t})\rangle=\hat U(\signal{t})|\psi(0)\rangle.
$$

Conversely,

$$
|\psi(0)\rangle=\hat U^\dagger(\signal{t})|\psi(\signal{t})\rangle.
$$

Thus quantum information is preserved in the complete closed-system description.

The source uses the **black-hole information paradox** to illustrate the importance of this statement. It describes Hawking radiation as apparently turning information into an irreversible mixture, in conflict with unitarity, and presents this as a problem whose resolution requires an understanding of quantum gravity.

On a practical level, ideal quantum gates are unitary operators. They transform qubit states reversibly. Measurements and open-system noise require a broader description; a subsystem can lose accessible information while the full system, including its environment, evolves unitarily.

A strongly continuous one-parameter unitary symmetry group has a self-adjoint generator:

$$
\hat U(\theta)=e^{i\theta\hat G}.
$$

When this group is a symmetry of the dynamics and the generator has no explicit time dependence, the generator is conserved. This is the continuous-symmetry correspondence in Dirac notation.

> [!note]
> The final source statement concerns continuous symmetry groups connected to the identity. Some discrete quantum symmetries, notably time reversal, are antiunitary instead. Exercise 7 addresses this distinction.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\signal{\phi}$ | $1$ | Phase angle | A global or relative phase in the stated transformation. |
| $\signal{t}$ | $\unit{s}$ | Time | The elapsed time in the unitary evolution. |
| $\mechanical{a}$ | $\unit{m}$ | Translation distance | The displacement generated by momentum. |
| $\mechanical{\hbar}$ | $\unit{J\,s}$ | Reduced Planck constant | The action scale in translation and evolution. |
| $\alpha$, $\beta$ | $1$ | State amplitude | The two basis coefficients in the relative-phase example. |
