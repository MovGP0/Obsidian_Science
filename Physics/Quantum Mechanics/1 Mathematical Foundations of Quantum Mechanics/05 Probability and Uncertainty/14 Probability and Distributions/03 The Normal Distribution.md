> [!TLDR]
> A normal distribution is determined by its mean and variance. Its Gaussian shape appears in the central limit theorem, maximum-entropy problems, and minimum-uncertainty quantum wave packets.

> [!definition] Definition 14.4 | Normal distribution N(μ, σ²)
> For $\mu\in\mathbb R$ and $\sigma>0$,
>
> $$
> \rho(x)=\frac1{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}
> e^{-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}.
> $$
>
> Its mean is $\langle X\rangle=\mu$ and its variance is $\operatorname{Var}(X)=\sigma^2$.

The normal distribution is especially important in physics:

1. **Central limit theorem:** The centered and standardized sum of independent, identically distributed random variables with finite nonzero variance converges in distribution to a normal law as the number of variables increases.
2. **Maximum entropy:** Among continuous distributions with a specified mean and variance, the normal distribution maximizes differential entropy.
3. **Gaussian wave packets:** Unchirped Gaussian wave packets saturate the quantum position–momentum uncertainty bound.

> [!note]
> A Gaussian position density alone does not guarantee a minimum uncertainty product. The wavefunction phase also matters; a quadratic phase can increase momentum uncertainty without changing the position density.

## Example — Verify normalization

Consider

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac1{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}
e^{-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}\,dx.
$$

Substitute $u=(x-\mu)/\sigma$:

$$
\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-u^2/2}\,du
=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{2\pi}=1.
$$

This uses the Gaussian integral with parameter $a=1/2$.
