> [!TLDR]
> The Bose–Einstein distribution permits many particles in one state as the chemical potential approaches the ground-state energy.

Consider identical, noninteracting bosons of mass ${\mechanical{m}}$ in volume ${\mechanical{V}}$ at temperature ${\thermal{T}}$. The chemical potential ${\energy{\mu}}$ controls the mean particle number in the grand canonical ensemble. The mean occupation of a state with energy ${\energy{\varepsilon}}_k$ is

$$
\langle {\mechanical{n}}_k\rangle=\frac{1}{e^{{\thermal{\beta}}({\energy{\varepsilon}}_k-{\energy{\mu}})}-1},\qquad {\thermal{\beta}}=\frac{1}{{\thermal{k}}_B{\thermal{T}}}.
$$

For a finite system at positive temperature, the sum over occupations converges only if ${\energy{\mu}}<{\energy{\varepsilon}}_{\min}$. In the bulk limit for a free gas, choose the ground-state energy as zero. Then ${\energy{\mu}}\le0$ and the fugacity is

$$
{\mechanical{z}}=e^{{\thermal{\beta}}{\energy{\mu}}},\qquad 0<{\mechanical{z}}\le1.
$$

**Remark 8.1.** Unlike the Fermi gas, this Bose gas has an upper bound on fugacity. The limit ${\mechanical{z}}\to1^-$, or ${\energy{\mu}}\to0^-$, marks saturation of the excited states and the onset of condensation.

> [!note] Finite-system endpoint
> The source writes ${\energy{\mu}}\le{\energy{\varepsilon}}_{\min}$. Equality is a thermodynamic-limit statement. At finite volume, putting ${\energy{\mu}}={\energy{\varepsilon}}_{\min}$ directly in the ground-state occupation makes it infinite. Treat the condensate separately when taking the bulk limit.

The formulas in this chapter use one internal component. If there are several independently occupied internal states, include their degeneracy in the state count.
