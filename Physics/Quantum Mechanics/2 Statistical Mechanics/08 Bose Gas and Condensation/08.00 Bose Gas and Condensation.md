> [!TLDR]
> Bose statistics explains macroscopic ground-state occupation, blackbody radiation, and the thermal response of lattice vibrations.

Bosons have integer spin and do not obey the Pauli exclusion principle. Any number of identical bosons can occupy the same complete quantum state. At low temperature this permits **Bose–Einstein condensation (BEC)**: a macroscopic number of particles occupies the ground state.

This chapter examines the ideal Bose gas, the condensation transition, and two important applications of Bose statistics: blackbody radiation from photons and the Debye model of phonons in crystals.

Start with [[08.01 Ideal Bose Gas - Grand Canonical Formalism]]. See [[08.08 Summary]] for the main results and [[08.99 Exercises]] for individual questions with worked solutions.

## Reading route and model choices

First ask which particle number is constrained. In an atomic gas, cooling at fixed atom number can force a large population into one orbital. In a photon gas or a thermal phonon system, the number of excitation quanta can change. The same occupation rule therefore leads to different thermodynamic problems.

Use Sections 8.1–8.3 for a uniform, noninteracting, three-dimensional atomic gas. Follow the state count before using a condensation formula: the number of low-energy states determines whether the excited population can saturate. Then read Sections 8.4–8.5 to compare linear photon and acoustic-phonon dispersion with quadratic atomic dispersion. Section 8.6 explains why a trapped, interacting cloud needs further assumptions.

A useful test of understanding is to explain why integer spin alone does not guarantee a positive condensation temperature. [[08.06 Condensation in Two Dimensions]] supplies a counterexample: geometry and the low-energy density of states matter as well as statistics.
