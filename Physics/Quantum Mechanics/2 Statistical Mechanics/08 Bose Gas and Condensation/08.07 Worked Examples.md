## Example 8.2: Critical temperature of rubidium-87 in a trap

Consider ${\mechanical{N}}=5\times10^5$ rubidium-87 atoms in an isotropic three-dimensional harmonic trap. Its angular frequency is

$$
{\signal{\omega}}_{\mathrm{trap}}=2\pi\times100\,\unit{s^{-1}}.
$$

Estimate the critical temperature.

### Calculation

The single-particle density of states in this trap is

$$
{\energy{g}}({\energy{\varepsilon}})=\frac{{\energy{\varepsilon}}^2}{2({\energy{\hbar}}{\signal{\omega}}_{\mathrm{trap}})^3}.
$$

At the transition, the excited states contain all the particles. Integration of the Bose–Einstein distribution gives

$$
{\mechanical{N}}=\left(\frac{{\thermal{k}}_B{\thermal{T}}_c}{{\energy{\hbar}}{\signal{\omega}}_{\mathrm{trap}}}\right)^3\zeta(3).
$$

We solve this equation for the required temperature:

$$
{\thermal{T}}_c=\frac{{\energy{\hbar}}{\signal{\omega}}_{\mathrm{trap}}}{{\thermal{k}}_B}
\left(\frac{{\mechanical{N}}}{\zeta(3)}\right)^{1/3},
\qquad \zeta(3)\approx1.202.
$$

The trap frequency sets the first temperature scale:

$$
\frac{{\energy{\hbar}}{\signal{\omega}}_{\mathrm{trap}}}{{\thermal{k}}_B}
=\frac{1.055\times10^{-34}\times2\pi\times100}{1.381\times10^{-23}}\,\unit{K}
\approx4.80\,\unit{nK}.
$$

The particle-number factor is

$$
\left(\frac{{\mechanical{N}}}{\zeta(3)}\right)^{1/3}
=\left(\frac{5\times10^5}{1.202}\right)^{1/3}
\approx(4.16\times10^5)^{1/3}
\approx74.6.
$$

Thus

$$
{\thermal{T}}_c\approx4.80\,\unit{nK}\times74.6
\approx358\,\unit{nK}.
$$

The source compares this result with typical experimental critical temperatures of 100–500 nK.

## Example 8.3: Power emitted by the Sun

Treat the Sun as a black body with temperature ${\thermal{T}}_\odot=5778\,\unit{K}$ and radius ${\mechanical{R}}_\odot=6.96\times10^8\,\unit{m}$. Calculate its total radiant power.

### Calculation

The Stefan–Boltzmann law gives the power per surface area:

$$
{\energy{j^*}}={\energy{\sigma}} {\thermal{T}}_\odot^4,
\qquad
{\energy{\sigma}}=5.67\times10^{-8}\,\unit{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-4}}.
$$

We first calculate the emitting area:

$$
{\mechanical{A}}_\odot=4\pi {\mechanical{R}}_\odot^2
=4\pi(6.96\times10^8\,\unit{m})^2
\approx6.087\times10^{18}\,\unit{m^2}.
$$

The total luminosity is the radiant exitance multiplied by this area:

$$
{\energy{L}}_\odot={\energy{\sigma}} {\thermal{T}}_\odot^4 {\mechanical{A}}_\odot.
$$

Since

$$
(5778\,\unit{K})^4\approx1.115\times10^{15}\,\unit{K^4},
$$

we obtain

$$
{\energy{L}}_\odot\approx5.67\times10^{-8}\times1.115\times10^{15}
\times6.087\times10^{18}\,\unit{W}
\approx3.85\times10^{26}\,\unit{W}.
$$

The source compares this result with ${\energy{L}}_\odot=3.828\times10^{26}\,\unit{W}$. The simple black-body model gives close agreement.

## Example 8.4: Heat capacity of copper at low temperature

For copper, use the following source data:

$$
{\thermal{\Theta}}_D=343\,\unit{K},\qquad
{\mechanical{n}}=\frac{{\mechanical{N}}}{{\mechanical{V}}}=8.49\times10^{28}\,\unit{m^{-3}},\qquad
{\mechanical{\rho}}=8960\,\unit{kg\,m^{-3}}.
$$

The atomic mass is 63.5 atomic mass units. Calculate the heat capacity at ${\thermal{T}}=10\,\unit{K}$ and check the ${\thermal{T}}^3$ law.

### Debye calculation

Since $10\,\unit{K}\ll343\,\unit{K}$, we use the low-temperature limit:

$$
{\thermal{C}}_V=\frac{12\pi^4}{5}{\mechanical{N}}{\thermal{k}}_B\left(\frac{{\thermal{T}}}{{\thermal{\Theta}}_D}\right)^3.
$$

For one mole, ${\mechanical{N}}={\mechanical{N}}_A\times1\,\unit{mol}$. We therefore use the molar gas constant ${\thermal{R}}_{\mathrm{gas}}={\mechanical{N}}_A{\thermal{k}}_B$ and write the molar heat capacity as

$$
{\thermal{C}}_{V,\mathrm{mol}}=\frac{12\pi^4}{5}{\thermal{R}}_{\mathrm{gas}}
\left(\frac{{\thermal{T}}}{{\thermal{\Theta}}_D}\right)^3.
$$

The two numerical factors are

$$
\frac{12\pi^4}{5}\approx233.8,
$$

$$
\left(\frac{10}{343}\right)^3
\approx(2.915\times10^{-2})^3
\approx2.478\times10^{-5}.
$$

Thus

$$
{\thermal{C}}_{V,\mathrm{mol}}
\approx233.8\times8.314\times2.478\times10^{-5}\,
\unit{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}
\approx4.82\times10^{-2}\,\unit{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}.
$$

The source gives an experimental lattice contribution of approximately $0.047\,\unit{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ at 10 K. It excludes the electronic contribution, which is proportional to temperature and becomes dominant at still lower temperature. The Debye result agrees closely with the stated lattice value.

> [!note] Numerical and unit clarification
> The source rounds $(10/343)^3$ to $2.481\times10^{-5}$. Direct evaluation gives approximately $2.478\times10^{-5}$; the final value $0.0482\,\unit{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ is unchanged at the shown precision. The result is a molar heat capacity. For a one-mole sample, the numerical value of its total heat capacity is the same, with unit $\unit{J\,K^{-1}}$.

### Comparison with the Einstein model

Use the source estimate ${\thermal{\Theta}}_E\approx0.83{\thermal{\Theta}}_D\approx285\,\unit{K}$. At 10 K, ${\thermal{\Theta}}_E/{\thermal{T}}=28.5$. The Einstein molar heat capacity is

$$
{\thermal{C}}_{V,\mathrm{mol}}^{E}
=3{\thermal{R}}_{\mathrm{gas}}\left(\frac{{\thermal{\Theta}}_E}{{\thermal{T}}}\right)^2
\frac{e^{{\thermal{\Theta}}_E/{\thermal{T}}}}{(e^{{\thermal{\Theta}}_E/{\thermal{T}}}-1)^2}.
$$

At this large value of ${\thermal{\Theta}}_E/{\thermal{T}}$, the fraction is approximately $e^{-{\thermal{\Theta}}_E/{\thermal{T}}}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
{\thermal{C}}_{V,\mathrm{mol}}^{E}
&\approx3{\thermal{R}}_{\mathrm{gas}}\times812\times e^{-28.5}\\
&\approx24.9\times812\times4.19\times10^{-13}\,
\unit{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}\\
&\approx8.5\times10^{-9}\,\unit{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This numerical result is consistent with the source. It is about seven orders of magnitude below the stated experimental value. The acoustic modes in the Debye model are essential for the low-temperature heat capacity.

## Example 8.5: Energy and pressure in the condensed phase

Consider ${\mechanical{N}}$ bosons of mass ${\mechanical{m}}$ in volume ${\mechanical{V}}$ at ${\thermal{T}}<{\thermal{T}}_c$. Show that ${\mechanical{P}}{\mechanical{V}}=2{\energy{U}}/3$ and that the pressure does not depend on volume within this phase.

### Calculation

In the bulk condensed-phase limit, ${\mechanical{z}}=1$. The pressure and energy are

$$
{\mechanical{P}}{\mechanical{V}}=\frac{{\mechanical{V}}{\thermal{k}}_B{\thermal{T}}}{{\signal{\lambda}}^3}\zeta(5/2),
$$

$$
{\energy{U}}=\frac32{\mechanical{P}}{\mechanical{V}}
=\frac32\frac{{\mechanical{V}}{\thermal{k}}_B{\thermal{T}}}{{\signal{\lambda}}^3}\zeta(5/2).
$$

The required identity follows directly:

$$
{\mechanical{P}}{\mechanical{V}}=\frac23{\energy{U}}.
$$

Divide the pressure expression by ${\mechanical{V}}$:

$$
{\mechanical{P}}=\frac{{\thermal{k}}_B{\thermal{T}}}{{\signal{\lambda}}^3}\zeta(5/2)\propto {\thermal{T}}^{5/2},
\qquad
{\signal{\lambda}}=\frac{{\energy{h}}}{\sqrt{2\pi {\mechanical{m}}{\thermal{k}}_B{\thermal{T}}}}.
$$

At a fixed temperature, this pressure does not depend on ${\mechanical{V}}$ or ${\mechanical{N}}$, as long as the gas remains in the condensed phase. The ideal bulk gas therefore has divergent isothermal compressibility:

$$
{\mechanical{\kappa}}_T\longrightarrow\infty.
$$

With the ground-state energy set to ${\energy{\varepsilon}}_0=0$, condensed particles do not contribute to the pressure. This idealization neglects the finite-size kinetic zero-point energy. Only excited particles contribute. Their number satisfies

$$
{\mechanical{N}}_{\mathrm{ex}}=\frac{{\mechanical{V}}}{{\signal{\lambda}}^3}\zeta(3/2)
\propto {\mechanical{V}}{\thermal{T}}^{3/2}.
$$

Thus ${\mechanical{N}}_{\mathrm{ex}}\propto {\thermal{T}}^{3/2}$ at fixed volume. Adding particles to the condensate at fixed temperature and volume does not increase the pressure.
