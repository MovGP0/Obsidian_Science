> [!TLDR]
> The peak wavelength is inversely proportional to temperature, but the peak depends on the spectral variable.

Find the maximum of the angular-frequency spectrum by differentiating its frequency-dependent part:

$$
\frac{d}{d{\signal{\omega}}}
\left[\frac{{\signal{\omega}}^3}{e^{{\energy{\hbar}}{\signal{\omega}}/({\thermal{k}}_B{\thermal{T}})}-1}\right]=0.
$$

With $x={\energy{\hbar}}{\signal{\omega}}/({\thermal{k}}_B{\thermal{T}})$, this gives

$$
3(1-e^{-x})=x.
$$

The positive nonzero solution is $x_{\max}\approx2.821$. Thus,

$$
{\signal{\omega}}_{\max}=\frac{2.821\,{\thermal{k}}_B{\thermal{T}}}{{\energy{\hbar}}}.
$$

## Remark 8.4 — The maximum depends on the spectral variable

The maximum of the angular-frequency spectrum is not at the same wavelength as the maximum of the wavelength spectrum. The change of variable is nonlinear:

$$
{\signal{\omega}}=\frac{2\pi {\signal{c}}}{{\signal{\lambda}}_{\mathrm{rad}}}.
$$

The spectra must describe the same positive energy interval. Therefore, the transformation uses the absolute value of the Jacobian:

$$
{\energy{u}}({\signal{\lambda}}_{\mathrm{rad}},{\thermal{T}})
={\energy{u}}({\signal{\omega}},{\thermal{T}})\left|\frac{d{\signal{\omega}}}{d{\signal{\lambda}}_{\mathrm{rad}}}\right|.
$$

This gives

$$
{\energy{u}}({\signal{\lambda}}_{\mathrm{rad}},{\thermal{T}})
=\frac{8\pi {\energy{h}}{\signal{c}}}{{\signal{\lambda}}_{\mathrm{rad}}^5}
\frac{1}{e^{{\energy{h}}{\signal{c}}/({\signal{\lambda}}_{\mathrm{rad}}{\thermal{k}}_B{\thermal{T}})}-1}.
$$

The wavelength spectrum is energy per volume and wavelength interval. To locate its maximum, set its derivative with respect to wavelength to zero. With $x={\energy{h}}{\signal{c}}/({\signal{\lambda}}_{\mathrm{rad}}{\thermal{k}}_B{\thermal{T}})$, the positive nonzero root satisfies

$$
5(1-e^{-x})=x,\qquad x_{\max}\approx4.965.
$$

The result is the usual **Wien displacement law**:

$$
{\signal{\lambda}}_{\mathrm{rad},\max}{\thermal{T}}
=\frac{{\energy{h}}{\signal{c}}}{4.965\,{\thermal{k}}_B}
\equiv {\signal{b}}
\approx2.898\times10^{-3}\,\unit{m\,K}.
$$

> [!note] Source clarification — change of variable
> The source writes equality between the two spectral differentials without an absolute value. Frequency decreases as wavelength increases. Use the absolute Jacobian when both spectra are defined as positive densities. The subscript “rad” distinguishes radiation wavelength from the thermal de Broglie wavelength used elsewhere in this chapter.

## Example 8.1 — Solar temperature from the Wien law

The wavelength maximum of the solar spectrum is approximately

$$
{\signal{\lambda}}_{\mathrm{rad},\max}\approx500\,\unit{nm}.
$$

Apply the Wien displacement law:

$$
{\thermal{T}}_\odot=\frac{2.898\times10^{-3}\,\unit{m\,K}}
{500\times10^{-9}\,\unit{m}}
\approx5796\,\unit{K}.
$$

This agrees with the effective solar surface temperature quoted in the source, ${\thermal{T}}_\odot\approx5778\,\unit{K}$. The relative difference is approximately $0.3\%$.

## Physical quantities

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ${\signal{\omega}}$ | $\unit{s^{-1}}$ | Angular frequency | Angular frequency of a mode or trap. |
| ${\energy{u}}({\signal{\omega}},{\thermal{T}})$ | $\unit{J\,s\,m^{-3}}$ | Angular-frequency spectrum | Energy per volume and angular-frequency interval. |
| ${\energy{u}}({\signal{\lambda}}_{\mathrm{rad}},{\thermal{T}})$ | $\unit{J\,m^{-4}}$ | Wavelength spectrum | Energy per volume and wavelength interval. |
| ${\signal{\lambda}}_{\mathrm{rad}}$ | $\unit{m}$ | Radiation wavelength | Wavelength used for the wavelength spectrum. |
| ${\thermal{T}}$ | $\unit{K}$ | Temperature | Absolute temperature of the system. |
| ${\thermal{k}}_B$ | $\unit{J\,K^{-1}}$ | Boltzmann constant | Converts temperature to energy. |
| ${\energy{\hbar}}$ | $\unit{J\,s}$ | Reduced Planck constant | Equal to ${\energy{h}}/(2\pi)$. |
| ${\energy{h}}$ | $\unit{J\,s}$ | Planck constant | Relates frequency to quantum energy. |
| ${\signal{c}}$ | $\unit{m\,s^{-1}}$ | Speed of light | Propagation speed of light in vacuum. |
| ${\signal{b}}$ | $\unit{m\,K}$ | Wien displacement constant | Product of the peak wavelength and temperature. |
