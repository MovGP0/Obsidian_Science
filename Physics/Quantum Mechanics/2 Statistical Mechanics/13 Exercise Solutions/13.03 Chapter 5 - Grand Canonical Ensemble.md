## Exercise 5.1: Grand potential of a monatomic ideal gas

**Task.** Calculate $\energy{\Phi}(\thermal{T},\mechanical{V},\energy{\mu})$, verify $\energy{\Phi}=-\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}$, and compare its entropy with Sackur–Tetrode.

**Solution.** The canonical partition function is

$$
Z_N=\frac1{N!}\left(\frac{\mechanical{V}}{\signal{\lambda}_T^3}\right)^N,
\qquad
\signal{\lambda}_T=\frac{\signal{h}}
{\sqrt{2\pi\mechanical{m}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}}.
$$

Equation (5.6) sums the exponential series:

$$
\Xi(\thermal{T},\mechanical{V},\energy{\mu})
=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}
\frac{(z\mechanical{V}/\signal{\lambda}_T^3)^N}{N!}
=\exp\!\left(\frac{z\mechanical{V}}{\signal{\lambda}_T^3}\right),
\qquad z=e^{\thermal{\beta}\energy{\mu}}.
$$

By definition,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\energy{\Phi}
&=-\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}\ln\Xi
=-\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
\frac{z\mechanical{V}}{\signal{\lambda}_T^3}\\
&=-\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}\mechanical{V}
\left(\frac{2\pi\mechanical{m}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}{\signal{h}^2}\right)^{3/2}
e^{\energy{\mu}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})}.
\end{aligned}
$$

### Verification of Φ = −PV

Equation (5.8) gives $\mechanical{P}=\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}z/\signal{\lambda}_T^3$, hence $\energy{\Phi}=-\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}$. Also,

$$
\langle N\rangle
=-\left(\frac{\partial\energy{\Phi}}{\partial\energy{\mu}}\right)_{T,V}
=\frac{z\mechanical{V}}{\signal{\lambda}_T^3},
$$

so $\energy{\Phi}=-\langle N\rangle\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}$ and $\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}=\langle N\rangle\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}$.

### Entropy

To calculate $\thermal{S}=-(\partial\energy{\Phi}/\partial\thermal{T})_{V,\mu}$, include both the $\thermal{T}^{5/2}$ prefactor and the temperature dependence of $e^{\energy{\mu}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})}$.

Since $\langle N\rangle=\mechanical{V}\signal{\lambda}_T^{-3} e^{\energy{\mu}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})}$,

$$
\frac1{\langle N\rangle}
\frac{\partial\langle N\rangle}{\partial\thermal{T}}
=\frac{3}{2\thermal{T}}
-\frac{\energy{\mu}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}^2}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
\thermal{S}
&=-\left.\frac{\partial\energy{\Phi}}{\partial\thermal{T}}\right|_{V,\mu}\\
&=\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\langle N\rangle
+\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
\frac{\partial\langle N\rangle}{\partial\thermal{T}}\\
&=\langle N\rangle\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}
\left(\frac52-\frac{\energy{\mu}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

Eliminate the chemical potential with $z=\langle N\rangle\signal{\lambda}_T^3/\mechanical{V}$:

$$
\thermal{S}
=\langle N\rangle\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}
\left[\ln\frac{\mechanical{V}}
{\langle N\rangle\signal{\lambda}_T^3}+\frac52\right].
$$

This is the canonical Sackur–Tetrode formula (4.12).

**Dimensional and limiting checks.** The logarithm argument is dimensionless: it counts available quantum cells per particle. The grand potential has energy units. For $\energy{\mu}\to-\infty$, $z\to0$, the vacuum term dominates $\Xi$, and $\langle N\rangle\to0$. No extra Gibbs correction is needed; $1/N!$ was already included in the canonical partition functions.

## Exercise 5.2: Heat capacity of a Langmuir surface

**Task.** At fixed chemical potential, calculate the surface heat capacity. Show its equivalence to an effective two-level system with parameter

$$
\energy{\Delta}=|\energy{\varepsilon}_0|+\energy{\mu},
$$

and show that the Schottky-peak condition is

$$
x=2\coth(x/2),
\qquad
x=\frac{\energy{\Delta}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}.
$$

**Solution.** Each of the $M$ adsorption sites is empty, with particle count zero and energy zero, or occupied, with particle count one and energy $-|\energy{\varepsilon}_0|$.

The grand-canonical weight is $e^{-\thermal{\beta}(\varepsilon-\mu N)}$. Thus the empty-state weight is one and the occupied-state weight is $e^{\thermal{\beta}\energy{\Delta}}$. The shifted level energies are $\widetilde{\energy{\varepsilon}}=\energy{\varepsilon}-\energy{\mu}N\in\{0,-\energy{\Delta}\}$.

The mean occupation, as in Equation (5.12), is

$$
\theta=\frac{e^{\thermal{\beta}\energy{\Delta}}}
{1+e^{\thermal{\beta}\energy{\Delta}}}
=\frac1{1+e^{-\thermal{\beta}\energy{\Delta}}}.
$$

The mean internal energy is $\langle\energy{E}\rangle=-M|\energy{\varepsilon}_0|\theta$. The source defines

$$
\thermal{C}_{\mu}
=\left.\frac{\partial\langle\energy{E}\rangle}{\partial\thermal{T}}\right|_{V,\mu}
=-M|\energy{\varepsilon}_0|\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial\thermal{T}}.
$$

Use

$$
\theta(1-\theta)=
\frac{e^{-\thermal{\beta}\energy{\Delta}}}
{(1+e^{-\thermal{\beta}\energy{\Delta}})^2},
\qquad
\frac{\partial(\thermal{\beta}\energy{\Delta})}{\partial\thermal{T}}
=-\frac{\energy{\Delta}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}^2}.
$$

Then $\partial\theta/\partial\thermal{T} =-\theta(1-\theta)\energy{\Delta}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}^2)$, so

$$
\thermal{C}_{\mu}
=M\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\frac{|\energy{\varepsilon}_0|}{\energy{\Delta}}
x^2\frac{e^x}{(e^x+1)^2},
\qquad
x=\frac{\energy{\Delta}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}.
$$

Apart from the constant factor, this has the two-level Schottky form. For the positive-splitting branch, its maximum satisfies

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{x^2e^x}{(e^x+1)^2}\right]=0,
$$

or

$$
2(1+e^{-x})=x(1-e^{-x})
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
x=2\coth(x/2).
$$

The positive root is $x^*\approx2.399$, giving

$$
\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}^*
\approx\frac{\energy{\Delta}}{2.399}
\approx0.417\,\energy{\Delta}
=0.417(|\energy{\varepsilon}_0|+\energy{\mu}).
$$

For zero chemical potential, $\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}^*\approx0.417|\energy{\varepsilon}_0|$.

**Limits.** For $\energy{\Delta}>0$, or $\energy{\mu}>-|\energy{\varepsilon}_0|$, the source response is positive and has the usual Schottky maximum. For negative splitting, the occupied state has higher grand-canonical energy. Its occupation rises from zero toward one half as temperature increases, so internal energy decreases and the defined $\thermal{C}_\mu$ is negative. Its magnitude still peaks at $\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}^*\approx0.417|\energy{\Delta}|$.

For nonzero splitting, the low-temperature response is exponentially suppressed. At high temperature it decays as $1/\thermal{T}^2$.

> [!note] Energy derivative versus heat capacity
> In an open system, the derivative used by the source is not generally the reversible heat capacity. At fixed chemical potential and volume,
> $\thermal{T}(\partial\thermal{S}/\partial\thermal{T})_\mu =(\partial\langle\energy{E}\rangle/\partial\thermal{T})_\mu -\energy{\mu}(\partial\langle N\rangle/\partial\thermal{T})_\mu$.
> This distinction explains why the source's internal-energy response can be negative. The shifted two-level spectrum uses the positive gap magnitude $|\energy{\Delta}|$.

## Exercise 5.3: van der Waals particle-number fluctuations

**Task.** When the repulsive correction is negligible, $\mechanical{b}n\ll1$, show

$$
\frac{\Delta N}{\langle N\rangle}
=\frac{\langle N\rangle^{-1/2}}
{\sqrt{1-2\energy{a}n^2\mechanical{\kappa}_T^{(0)}}},
\qquad
\mechanical{\kappa}_T^{(0)}
=\frac1{n\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}},
$$

and discuss critical behaviour.

**Solution.** With $n=N/\mechanical{V}$, the van der Waals equation is

$$
\mechanical{P}(\thermal{T},n)
=\frac{n\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}{1-\mechanical{b}n}
-\energy{a}n^2.
$$

The isothermal compressibility satisfies

$$
\mechanical{\kappa}_T^{-1}
=-\mechanical{V}
\left(\frac{\partial\mechanical{P}}{\partial\mechanical{V}}\right)_{T,N}.
$$

Since $(\partial n/\partial\mechanical{V})_N=-n/\mechanical{V}$,

$$
\mechanical{\kappa}_T^{-1}
=n\frac{\partial\mechanical{P}}{\partial n}
=n\left[
\frac{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}{(1-\mechanical{b}n)^2}
-2\energy{a}n
\right].
$$

For $\mechanical{b}n\ll1$, use $(1-\mechanical{b}n)^{-2}\approx1$:

$$
\mechanical{\kappa}_T^{-1}
\approx n\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
\left(1-\frac{2\energy{a}n}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}\right)
=\frac1{\mechanical{\kappa}_T^{(0)}}
\left(1-2\energy{a}n^2\mechanical{\kappa}_T^{(0)}\right).
$$

Equations (5.9)–(5.10), relating fluctuations to susceptibility, give

$$
\frac{(\Delta N)^2}{\langle N\rangle^2}
=\frac{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}\mechanical{\kappa}_T}{\mechanical{V}}
=\frac1{\langle N\rangle}
\frac{\mechanical{\kappa}_T}{\mechanical{\kappa}_T^{(0)}}.
$$

Use $\mechanical{V}=\langle N\rangle/n$ and $\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}=1/(n\mechanical{\kappa}_T^{(0)})$. The dilute approximation gives

$$
\frac{\mechanical{\kappa}_T}{\mechanical{\kappa}_T^{(0)}}
=\frac1{1-2\energy{a}n^2\mechanical{\kappa}_T^{(0)}},
$$

hence

$$
\frac{\Delta N}{\langle N\rangle}
=\frac1{\sqrt{\langle N\rangle}}
\frac1{\sqrt{1-2\energy{a}n^2\mechanical{\kappa}_T^{(0)}}}.
$$

**Limits.** For zero attraction, the correction is one and $\Delta N=\sqrt{\langle N\rangle}$, the ideal-gas result (5.11). Positive attraction enhances fluctuations on the stable branch: local density increases are favoured by attractive interactions.

### Critical behaviour

On the critical isotherm at critical density,

$$
\left(\frac{\partial\mechanical{P}}{\partial n}\right)_T=0,
\qquad
\mechanical{\kappa}_T^{-1}=0,
\qquad
\mechanical{\kappa}_T\to\infty.
$$

In the dilute approximation, the factor diverges as $2\energy{a}n^2\mechanical{\kappa}_T^{(0)}\to1^-$. This condition, $\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}=2\energy{a}n$, is the **spinodal of the reduced model**, not the full van der Waals critical point.

The full model has

$$
\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}
=\frac{8\energy{a}}{27\mechanical{b}},
\qquad
n_{\mathrm c}=\frac1{3\mechanical{b}}.
$$

Here $\mechanical{b}n_{\mathrm c}=1/3$, so the dilute approximation is not valid. The approximate divergence is qualitatively similar, but not quantitatively the same critical point.

Near criticality, long-range correlations invalidate the simple independent-volume fluctuation argument and its usual inverse-square-root scaling. The naive ensemble-equivalence reasoning of Proposition 5.3 must then be examined more carefully.

The associated large density fluctuations underlie **critical opalescence**, a statistical effect. This limits the simple concentration-based argument behind the canonical thermodynamic result in Theorem 4.2.

## Exercise 5.4: BET multilayer adsorption

**Task.** Extend the Langmuir model to multilayer adsorption, with binding energies $|\energy{\varepsilon}_0|$ in the first layer and $|\energy{\varepsilon}_L|$ in subsequent layers. Derive the BET isotherm.

**Solution.** Each site can hold a column with $k=0,1,2,\ldots$ molecules. A nonempty column has energy $-|\energy{\varepsilon}_0|-(k-1)|\energy{\varepsilon}_L|$: one surface bond and $k-1$ liquid-like bonds.

Its grand partition function is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\xi
&=1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}
z^k e^{\thermal{\beta}[|\energy{\varepsilon}_0|+(k-1)|\energy{\varepsilon}_L|]}\\
&=1+ze^{\thermal{\beta}|\energy{\varepsilon}_0|}
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}
(ze^{\thermal{\beta}|\energy{\varepsilon}_L|})^{k-1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

For $y=ze^{\thermal{\beta}|\energy{\varepsilon}_L|}<1$, the geometric series gives

$$
\xi=1+\frac{cy}{1-y}
=\frac{1+(c-1)y}{1-y},
\qquad
c=e^{\thermal{\beta}(|\energy{\varepsilon}_0|-|\energy{\varepsilon}_L|)}.
$$

The weight ratio $c$ is large when first-layer binding is much stronger.

The mean occupation follows from $\langle n\rangle=z\,\partial_z\ln\xi=y\,\partial_y\ln\xi$:

$$
\langle n\rangle
=\frac{(c-1)y}{1+(c-1)y}+\frac{y}{1-y}
=\frac{cy}{(1-y)[1+(c-1)y]}.
$$

To identify $y$ with pressure, consider saturated vapour in equilibrium with the liquid. In this model its liquid chemical potential is $\energy{\mu}_L=-|\energy{\varepsilon}_L|$, the molecular liquid binding energy.

The saturation fugacity is $z_s=e^{-\thermal{\beta}|\energy{\varepsilon}_L|}$. For a classical ideal exterior gas, Equation (5.3) gives $z=\mechanical{P}\signal{\lambda}_T^3/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})$. Therefore

$$
y=\frac z{z_s}=\frac{\mechanical{P}}{\mechanical{P}_s}.
$$

The **BET isotherm** is

$$
\theta=\langle n\rangle
=\frac{c(\mechanical{P}/\mechanical{P}_s)}
{(1-\mechanical{P}/\mechanical{P}_s)
[1+(c-1)\mechanical{P}/\mechanical{P}_s]}.
$$

### Limits and physical interpretation

Convergence requires $\mechanical{P}<\mechanical{P}_s$. As pressure approaches saturation from below, the mean column height diverges. The source interprets this as surface condensation.

For strong first-layer binding, $c\gg1$, and low relative pressure,

$$
\theta\approx
\frac{c(\mechanical{P}/\mechanical{P}_s)}
{1+c(\mechanical{P}/\mechanical{P}_s)}.
$$

This is the Langmuir form, with pressure scale

$$
\frac{\mechanical{P}_s}{c}
=\frac{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}{\signal{\lambda}_T^3}
e^{-\thermal{\beta}|\energy{\varepsilon}_0|}
\equiv\mechanical{P}_0(\thermal{T}).
$$

For $c=1$, all layers have equal binding energy and

$$
\theta=\frac{\mechanical{P}/\mechanical{P}_s}
{1-\mechanical{P}/\mechanical{P}_s},
$$

the mean of a geometric column-height distribution.

**Dimensional check.** The quantities $c$, $y$, and $\theta$ are dimensionless. Unlike the Langmuir occupation probability, BET coverage can exceed one: $\theta=\mechanical{V}_{\mathrm{ads}}/\mechanical{V}_m$, where $\mechanical{V}_m$ is the monolayer volume. BET analysis is used to determine the specific surface area of porous materials.
