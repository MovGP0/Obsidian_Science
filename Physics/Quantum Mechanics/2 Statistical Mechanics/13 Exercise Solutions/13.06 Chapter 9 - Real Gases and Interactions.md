## Exercise 9.1: Isothermal compressibility near criticality

**Task.** In the van der Waals model, show that

$$
\mechanical{\kappa}_T
=-\frac1{\mechanical{V}}
\left(\frac{\partial\mechanical{V}}{\partial\mechanical{P}}\right)_T
$$

diverges near the critical point as $|\thermal{T}-\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}|^{-\gamma}$, and calculate the exponent.

**Solution.** Rewrite the reduced van der Waals equation (9.8) as

$$
\mechanical{p}(\mechanical{v},\thermal{t})
=\frac{8\thermal{t}}{3\mechanical{v}-1}
-\frac3{\mechanical{v}^2}.
\tag{13.27}
$$

Here reduced temperature is temperature divided by its critical value, rather than the shifted reduced temperature used in Landau theory.

The definitions are $\mechanical{p}=\mechanical{P}/\mechanical{P}_{\mathrm c}$, $\mechanical{v}=\mechanical{V}/\mechanical{V}_{\mathrm c}$, and $\thermal{t}=\thermal{T}/\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}$. In these variables,

$$
\mechanical{\kappa}_T
=-\frac1{\mechanical{P}_{\mathrm c}\mechanical{v}}
\left(\frac{\partial\mechanical{v}}{\partial\mechanical{p}}\right)_{\!t}
=-\frac1{\mechanical{v}}
\left(\frac{\partial\mechanical{p}}{\partial\mechanical{v}}\right)_{\!t}^{-1}
\frac1{\mechanical{P}_{\mathrm c}}.
$$

It is enough to study the slope near $(\mechanical{p},\mechanical{v},\thermal{t})=(1,1,1)$.

### Expansion near the critical point

Set $\mechanical{v}=1+\phi$ and $\thermal{t}=1+\thermal{\tau}$, with small offsets. From Equation (13.27),

$$
\left.\frac{\partial\mechanical{p}}{\partial\mechanical{v}}\right|_{(1,1)}=0,
\qquad
\left.\frac{\partial^2\mechanical{p}}{\partial\mechanical{v}^2}\right|_{(1,1)}=0,
$$

$$
\left.\frac{\partial^3\mechanical{p}}{\partial\mechanical{v}^3}\right|_{(1,1)}=-9,
\qquad
\left.\frac{\partial^2\mechanical{p}}
{\partial\mechanical{v}\,\partial\thermal{t}}\right|_{(1,1)}=-6.
$$

The first two slopes vanish at the horizontal inflection point. Taylor expansion gives

$$
\mechanical{p}(\mechanical{v},\thermal{t})
\approx1+4\thermal{\tau}-6\thermal{\tau}\phi-\frac32\phi^3
+O(\thermal{\tau}\phi^2,\phi^4).
\tag{13.28}
$$

Hence

$$
\left(\frac{\partial\mechanical{p}}{\partial\mechanical{v}}\right)_t
\approx-6\thermal{\tau}-\frac92\phi^2.
$$

### Critical isochore

At $\mechanical{v}=1$, or physical volume $\mechanical{V}=\mechanical{V}_{\mathrm c}$, $\phi=0$ and the slope is

$$
-6\thermal{\tau}=-6\frac{\thermal{T}-\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}}{\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}}.
$$

Above the transition,

$$
\mechanical{\kappa}_T
=\frac1{\mechanical{P}_{\mathrm c}}\frac1{6\thermal{\tau}}
=\frac{\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}}{6\mechanical{P}_{\mathrm c}}
(\thermal{T}-\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c})^{-1}.
\tag{13.29}
$$

> [!note] Reduced-variable corrections
> The source's first reduced-derivative expression omits the factor $1/\mechanical{P}_{\mathrm c}$, which is required for physical compressibility units. It also writes the critical isochore as reduced volume equal to physical critical volume; the reduced value is one.

Comparison with $\mechanical{\kappa}_T\sim|\thermal{T}-\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}|^{-\gamma}$ gives

$$
\gamma_{\mathrm{vdW}}=1.
$$

**Units and physics.** Compressibility has inverse-pressure units and is positive on the stable branch above the transition. Near the critical point a small pressure change produces a large volume response. The value one is characteristic of mean-field descriptions such as van der Waals, Curie–Weiss, and Landau theories. The source compares it with approximately $1.24$ for the three-dimensional Ising universality class. Critical fluctuations account for the difference.

## Exercise 9.2: Square-well second virial coefficient

**Task.** Calculate $\mechanical{B}_2(\thermal{T})$ for a square-well potential with hard-core separation $\mechanical{\sigma}$, depth $\energy{\varepsilon}$, and range ratio $\lambda=1.5$. Determine the Boyle temperature as a function of well depth.

**Solution.** The Mayer function is $f(\mechanical{r})=e^{-\energy{u}(\mechanical{r})/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})}-1$:

$$
f(\mechanical{r})=
\begin{cases}
-1,&\mechanical{r}<\mechanical{\sigma},\\
e^{\energy{\varepsilon}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})}-1,
&\mechanical{\sigma}\le\mechanical{r}<\lambda\mechanical{\sigma},\\
0,&\mechanical{r}\ge\lambda\mechanical{\sigma}.
\end{cases}
\tag{13.30}
$$

### Second virial coefficient

Apply Equation (9.11), splitting the regions where the Mayer function is nonzero:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{B}_2(\thermal{T})
&=-2\pi\int_0^\infty
f(\mechanical{r})\mechanical{r}^2\,d\mechanical{r}\\
&=-2\pi\left[
\int_0^{\mechanical{\sigma}}(-1)\mechanical{r}^2\,d\mechanical{r}
+\int_{\mechanical{\sigma}}^{\lambda\mechanical{\sigma}}
(e^{\energy{\varepsilon}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})}-1)
\mechanical{r}^2\,d\mechanical{r}\right]\\
&=\frac{2\pi\mechanical{\sigma}^3}{3}
-2\pi(e^{\energy{\varepsilon}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})}-1)
\frac{(\lambda\mechanical{\sigma})^3-\mechanical{\sigma}^3}{3}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Define the hard-sphere coefficient $\mechanical{b}_0=2\pi\mechanical{\sigma}^3/3$. Then

$$
\mechanical{B}_2(\thermal{T})
=\mechanical{b}_0
\left[1-(\lambda^3-1)
(e^{\energy{\varepsilon}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})}-1)\right].
\tag{13.31}
$$

**Limits.** At high temperature, $\mechanical{B}_2\to\mechanical{b}_0$: thermal energy overwhelms the attraction. As temperature tends to zero from above, the attractive contribution grows without bound and $\mechanical{B}_2\to-\infty$.

> [!note] Limit of a second-virial argument
> The source associates the low-temperature divergence with condensation and lost analyticity of the equation of state. The coefficient is analytic for every positive temperature. Its growth signals the failure of a low-order dilute expansion; that coefficient alone does not establish a phase boundary or instability.

### Boyle temperature

Set $\mechanical{B}_2(\thermal{T}_B)=0$:

$$
1=(\lambda^3-1)
(e^{\energy{\varepsilon}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}_B)}-1),
$$

so

$$
e^{\energy{\varepsilon}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}_B)}
=\frac{\lambda^3}{\lambda^3-1}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\thermal{T}_B
=\frac{\energy{\varepsilon}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}
\left[\ln\frac{\lambda^3}{\lambda^3-1}\right]^{-1}.
\tag{13.32}
$$

For $\lambda=1.5$,

$$
\frac{\energy{\varepsilon}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}_B}
=\ln\frac{3.375}{2.375}
\approx0.3514,
\qquad
\thermal{T}_B\approx2.846\,\frac{\energy{\varepsilon}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}.
$$

**Units and comparison.** The second virial coefficient has volume units, and the Boyle temperature has temperature units. For the source's argon scale $\energy{\varepsilon}/\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\approx120\,\unit{K}$, the square-well estimate is about $342\,\unit{K}$. The source compares this with about $411\,\unit{K}$ experimentally and $408\,\unit{K}$ from a Lennard–Jones calculation.

The square well is qualitatively useful but replaces the long attractive tail by a finite-range well. Above the Boyle temperature, repulsion gives a positive second virial coefficient; below it, attraction makes the coefficient negative.

## Exercise 9.3: Slope of the liquid–vapour coexistence curve

**Task.** Use the reduced van der Waals equation and Maxwell construction to find $d\mechanical{p}_0/d\thermal{t}$ near the critical point from below. Compare with a dimensional estimate.

**Solution.** With $\phi=\mechanical{v}-1$ and $\thermal{\tau}=\thermal{t}-1$, Equation (13.28) gives

$$
\mechanical{p}(\mechanical{v},\thermal{t})-1
\approx4\thermal{\tau}
-6\thermal{\tau}\phi-\frac32\phi^3.
\tag{13.33}
$$

### Liquid and vapour volumes

Below the critical temperature the cubic isotherm has three intersections with the coexistence pressure. To leading order its odd dependence on volume offset gives symmetric phase volumes:

$$
\phi_{\mathrm g}=-\phi_{\ell}\equiv\phi_*>0.
$$

Equal pressures at the two endpoints require the odd part to vanish:

$$
-6\thermal{\tau}\phi_*-\frac32\phi_*^3=0.
$$

Apply the Maxwell condition,

$$
\int_{-\phi_*}^{\phi_*}
[\mechanical{p}(\mechanical{v},\thermal{t})-\mechanical{p}_0]\,d\phi=0.
$$

The odd terms integrate to zero. The constant term gives
$2\phi_*[1+4\thermal{\tau}-\mechanical{p}_0]=0$, hence

$$
\mechanical{p}_0-1=4\thermal{\tau},
\qquad
\mechanical{v}_{\mathrm g}-\mechanical{v}_{\ell}=2\phi_*.
$$

The nonzero root satisfies

$$
\phi_*^2=-4\thermal{\tau},
\qquad
\phi_*=2\sqrt{-\thermal{\tau}}.
\tag{13.34}
$$

Thus the phase-volume difference is $4\sqrt{-\thermal{\tau}}$, with the mean-field order-parameter exponent $1/2$.

### Coexistence slope

The reduced coexistence pressure is

$$
\mechanical{p}_0(\thermal{t})
=1+4(\thermal{t}-1)+O((\thermal{t}-1)^2).
\tag{13.35}
$$

Therefore

$$
\left.\frac{d\mechanical{p}_0}{d\thermal{t}}\right|_{t\to1^-}=4.
\tag{13.36}
$$

In physical units,

$$
\left.\frac{d\mechanical{P}_0}{d\thermal{T}}\right|_{T_{\mathrm c}^-}
=\frac{4\mechanical{P}_{\mathrm c}}{\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}}.
$$

### Dimensional estimate

The natural pressure–temperature slope scale is $\mechanical{P}_{\mathrm c}/\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}$. The model fixes the dimensionless factor at four.

For the source's nitrogen values, $\mechanical{P}_{\mathrm c}=33.5\,\unit{atm}$ and $\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}=126\,\unit{K}$, the prediction is about $1.06\,\unit{atm\,K^{-1}}$. The source quotes an experimental comparison of about $1.5\,\unit{atm\,K^{-1}}$.

### Consistency with Clausius–Clapeyron

The coexistence slope obeys $d\mechanical{P}_0/d\thermal{T} =\thermal{L}/[\thermal{T}(\mechanical{V}_{\mathrm g}-\mechanical{V}_{\ell})]$. A finite slope requires latent heat to vanish with the volume difference, proportional to $\sqrt{\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}-\thermal{T}}$ in mean-field theory.

## Exercise 9.4: Latent vaporization heat

**Task.** Use Clausius–Clapeyron for the van der Waals gas to calculate latent heat and show that it vanishes at the critical temperature.

**Solution.**

$$
\frac{d\mechanical{P}_0}{d\thermal{T}}
=\frac{\thermal{L}}
{\thermal{T}(\mechanical{V}_{\mathrm g}-\mechanical{V}_{\ell})}.
\tag{13.37}
$$

Hence

$$
\thermal{L}(\thermal{T})
=\thermal{T}(\mechanical{V}_{\mathrm g}-\mechanical{V}_{\ell})
\frac{d\mechanical{P}_0}{d\thermal{T}}.
\tag{13.38}
$$

The model critical parameters are

$$
\mechanical{V}_{\mathrm c}=3N\mechanical{b},
\qquad
\mechanical{P}_{\mathrm c}=\frac{\energy{a}}{27\mechanical{b}^2},
\qquad
\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}
=\frac{8\energy{a}}{27\mechanical{b}},
$$

so $\mechanical{P}_{\mathrm c}\mechanical{V}_{\mathrm c}/(N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c})=3/8$.

### Near-critical limit

Exercise 9.3 gives

$$
\mechanical{v}_{\mathrm g}-\mechanical{v}_{\ell}
=4\sqrt{1-\thermal{t}},
\qquad
\frac{d\mechanical{p}_0}{d\thermal{t}}=4.
$$

In physical units,

$$
\mechanical{V}_{\mathrm g}-\mechanical{V}_{\ell}
=4\mechanical{V}_{\mathrm c}
\sqrt{1-\frac{\thermal{T}}{\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}}},
\qquad
\frac{d\mechanical{P}_0}{d\thermal{T}}
=\frac{4\mechanical{P}_{\mathrm c}}{\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}}.
$$

Substitute into Equation (13.38):

$$
\thermal{L}(\thermal{T})
\approx16\mechanical{P}_{\mathrm c}\mechanical{V}_{\mathrm c}
\frac{\thermal{T}}{\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}}
\sqrt{1-\frac{\thermal{T}}{\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}}}.
$$

Using $\mechanical{P}_{\mathrm c}\mechanical{V}_{\mathrm c} =(3/8)N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}$ gives
$\thermal{L}\approx6N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T} \sqrt{1-\thermal{T}/\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}}$. To leading critical order,

$$
\thermal{L}(\thermal{T})
\approx6N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}
\sqrt{1-\frac{\thermal{T}}{\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}}},
\qquad
\thermal{T}\to\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}^-.
\tag{13.39}
$$

Latent heat vanishes with the same exponent as the phase-volume difference: $\beta=1/2$ in mean-field theory. The factor $\thermal{T}\,d\mechanical{P}_0/d\thermal{T}$ remains finite.

### Well below the critical temperature

For dilute vapour, the gas volume is much larger than the liquid volume and

$$
\mechanical{V}_{\mathrm g}
\approx\frac{N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}{\mechanical{P}_0}.
$$

If latent heat is treated as approximately constant, integrating Clausius–Clapeyron gives $\mechanical{P}_0(\thermal{T})\propto e^{-\thermal{L}/(N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})}$.

Using the van der Waals internal-energy difference,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\thermal{L}(\thermal{T})
&\approx\frac{\energy{a}N^2}{\mechanical{V}_{\ell}}
-\frac{\energy{a}N^2}{\mechanical{V}_{\mathrm g}}
+\mechanical{P}_0(\mechanical{V}_{\mathrm g}-\mechanical{V}_{\ell})\\
&\approx\frac{\energy{a}N}{\mechanical{b}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The formal low-temperature estimate per particle is

$$
\frac{\thermal{L}}N
\approx\frac{\energy{a}}{\mechanical{b}}
=\frac{27}{8}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}
\approx3.4\,\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}.
$$

It estimates a molecular binding-energy scale.

**Verification.** Equation (13.39) has energy units. The vanishing latent heat reflects the disappearance of the liquid–vapour distinction at criticality.

> [!note] Formal low-temperature extrapolation
> The last estimate extrapolates a classical fluid model. It does not account for quantum effects or possible freezing of an actual material near zero temperature.

## Exercise 9.5: Numerical nitrogen pressure comparison

**Task.** For $n=0.166\,\unit{mol}$ of nitrogen at $300\,\unit{K}$ in $1\,\unit{L}$, calculate pressure from:

1. The ideal-gas law.
2. The molar van der Waals equation.
3. The second-order virial approximation, with $\mechanical{B}_2^{\mathrm{vdW}}=\mechanical{b}-\energy{a}/(\thermal{R}\thermal{T})$.

Use $\energy{a}=0.137\,\unit{Pa\,m^6\,mol^{-2}}$ and $\mechanical{b}=3.87\times10^{-5}\,\unit{m^3\,mol^{-1}}$.

**Solution.** In SI units, $\mechanical{V}=10^{-3}\,\unit{m^3}$, $\thermal{R}=8.314\,\unit{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$, and

$$
n\thermal{R}\thermal{T}=(0.166)(8.314)(300)\,\unit{J}\approx414.0\,\unit{J}.
$$

### (a) Ideal gas

$$
\mechanical{P}_{\mathrm{id}}
=\frac{n\thermal{R}\thermal{T}}{\mechanical{V}}
\approx4.140\times10^5\,\unit{Pa}
\approx4.09\,\unit{atm}.
$$

### (b) van der Waals gas

In molar form,

$$
\mechanical{P}_{\mathrm{vdW}}
=\frac{n\thermal{R}\thermal{T}}{\mechanical{V}-n\mechanical{b}}
-\frac{\energy{a}n^2}{\mechanical{V}^2}.
$$

The source evaluates the terms with rounded values:

$$
\mechanical{V}-n\mechanical{b}
=10^{-3}-(0.166)(3.87\times10^{-5})
\approx9.936\times10^{-4}\,\unit{m^3},
$$

$$
\frac{n\thermal{R}\thermal{T}}{\mechanical{V}-n\mechanical{b}}
\approx4.166\times10^5\,\unit{Pa},
\qquad
\frac{\energy{a}n^2}{\mechanical{V}^2}
\approx3.78\times10^3\,\unit{Pa}.
$$

It reports

$$
\mechanical{P}_{\mathrm{vdW}}
\approx4.128\times10^5\,\unit{Pa}
\approx4.075\,\unit{atm}.
$$

> [!note] Molar convention
> Here $n$ is an amount in moles. The parameters $a$ and $b$ are molar coefficients, unlike the particle-based coefficients used above. Their units and numerical values must not be interchanged.

### (c) Second-virial approximation

The molar coefficient is

$$
\frac{\energy{a}}{\thermal{R}\thermal{T}}
\approx5.493\times10^{-5}\,\unit{m^3\,mol^{-1}},
\qquad
\mechanical{B}_2^{\mathrm{vdW}}
=\mechanical{b}-\frac{\energy{a}}{\thermal{R}\thermal{T}}
\approx-1.62\times10^{-5}\,\unit{m^3\,mol^{-1}}.
$$

It is negative at the stated temperature; attraction dominates. The model Boyle temperature is about $425\,\unit{K}$.

To first order in molar density,

$$
\mechanical{P}_{\mathrm{vir}}
=\frac{n\thermal{R}\thermal{T}}{\mechanical{V}}
\left(1+\frac{\mechanical{B}_2n}{\mechanical{V}}\right)
=\mechanical{P}_{\mathrm{id}}
\left(1+\frac{\mechanical{B}_2n}{\mechanical{V}}\right).
$$

The dimensionless correction is

$$
\frac{\mechanical{B}_2n}{\mechanical{V}}
\approx\frac{(-1.62\times10^{-5})(0.166)}{10^{-3}}
\approx-2.69\times10^{-3}.
$$

The source reports $\mechanical{P}_{\mathrm{vir}}\approx4.129\times10^5\,\unit{Pa}\approx4.077\,\unit{atm}$.

### Comparison

The printed pressure values, in units of $10^5\,\unit{Pa}$, are

$$
\mechanical{P}_{\mathrm{id}}:
\mechanical{P}_{\mathrm{vdW}}:
\mechanical{P}_{\mathrm{vir}}
\approx4.140:4.128:4.129.
$$

Both interaction models predict about 0.3% less pressure than the ideal law. They agree closely because the molecular number density, of order $10^{26}\,\unit{m^{-3}}$, is dilute compared with an inverse molecular interaction volume.

The source estimates a small interaction parameter
$\Gamma=\rho\mechanical{\sigma}^3\energy{\varepsilon}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})$ of order $10^{-3}$.

> [!note] Density convention and numerical precision
> The source reuses $n$ for particle density in the final comment, after using it for moles. Here $\rho$ denotes that particle density; conversion from $n/\mechanical{V}$ requires Avogadro constant.
>
> Without the printed intermediate rounding, the supplied inputs give approximately $4.12939\times10^5\,\unit{Pa}$ for van der Waals and $4.12922\times10^5\,\unit{Pa}$ for the virial result. Their small difference is below the accuracy of the source's rounded comparison.

The second-virial approximation is the low-density Taylor expansion of the van der Waals equation in $n\mechanical{b}/\mechanical{V}$, explaining the close agreement.

## Exercise 9.6: Joule–Thomson coefficient

**Task.** For

$$
\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}
=N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
\left[1+\mechanical{B}_2(\thermal{T})\frac N{\mechanical{V}}\right],
$$

show, to leading dilute-gas order,

$$
\thermal{\mu}_{\mathrm{JT}}
=\frac{\thermal{T}\,d\mechanical{B}_2/d\thermal{T}-\mechanical{B}_2}
{\thermal{c}_P}.
$$

Calculate this for van der Waals and derive the inversion temperature, comparing with Equation (9.15).

### General identity

By definition $\thermal{\mu}_{\mathrm{JT}}=(\partial\thermal{T}/\partial\mechanical{P})_H$. Thermodynamics gives

$$
\thermal{\mu}_{\mathrm{JT}}
=\frac1{\thermal{C}_P}
\left[
\thermal{T}\left(\frac{\partial\mechanical{V}}{\partial\thermal{T}}\right)_P
-\mechanical{V}
\right].
\tag{13.40}
$$

This follows from $d\energy{H}=\thermal{T}\,d\thermal{S}+\mechanical{V}\,d\mechanical{P}$ and the Maxwell relation $(\partial\thermal{S}/\partial\mechanical{P})_T=-(\partial\mechanical{V}/\partial\thermal{T})_P$ at fixed particle count.

For an ideal gas, $\thermal{T}(\partial\mechanical{V}/\partial\thermal{T})_P=\mechanical{V}$, so the Joule–Thomson coefficient vanishes.

### Perturbative inversion of the equation of state

Write

$$
\mechanical{P}
=\frac{N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}{\mechanical{V}}
\left(1+\mechanical{B}_2\frac N{\mechanical{V}}\right).
$$

Seek volume as the ideal value plus a first-order correction. With $\mechanical{V}_0=N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}/\mechanical{P}$,

$$
\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}
\approx N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
+\frac{N^2\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}\mechanical{B}_2}{\mechanical{V}_0},
$$

so

$$
\mechanical{V}
\approx\frac{N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}{\mechanical{P}}
+N\mechanical{B}_2(\thermal{T}).
$$

To first order,

$$
\mechanical{V}(\mechanical{P},\thermal{T})
=\frac{N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}{\mechanical{P}}
+N\mechanical{B}_2(\thermal{T})
+O(\mechanical{B}_2^2/\mechanical{V}_0).
\tag{13.41}
$$

The source's remainder notation treats particle count as fixed.

### Temperature derivative at fixed pressure

$$
\left(\frac{\partial\mechanical{V}}{\partial\thermal{T}}\right)_P
=\frac{N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}{\mechanical{P}}
+N\frac{d\mechanical{B}_2}{d\thermal{T}},
$$

so

$$
\begin{aligned}
\thermal{T}\left(\frac{\partial\mechanical{V}}{\partial\thermal{T}}\right)_P
&=\frac{N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}{\mechanical{P}}
+N\thermal{T}\frac{d\mechanical{B}_2}{d\thermal{T}}\\
&=\mechanical{V}-N\mechanical{B}_2
+N\thermal{T}\frac{d\mechanical{B}_2}{d\thermal{T}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore the bracket in Equation (13.40) is
$N[\thermal{T}\,d\mechanical{B}_2/d\thermal{T}-\mechanical{B}_2]$.
With $\thermal{C}_P=N\thermal{c}_P$,

$$
\thermal{\mu}_{\mathrm{JT}}
=\frac1{\thermal{c}_P}
\left(\thermal{T}\frac{d\mechanical{B}_2}{d\thermal{T}}-\mechanical{B}_2\right).
\tag{13.42}
$$

This is Equation (9.14).

### van der Waals case

For the particle-based coefficient
$\mechanical{B}_2=\mechanical{b}-\energy{a}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})$,

$$
\frac{d\mechanical{B}_2}{d\thermal{T}}
=\frac{\energy{a}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}^2},
\qquad
\thermal{T}\frac{d\mechanical{B}_2}{d\thermal{T}}
=\frac{\energy{a}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}.
$$

Thus

$$
\thermal{T}\frac{d\mechanical{B}_2}{d\thermal{T}}-\mechanical{B}_2
=\frac{\energy{a}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}
-\left(\mechanical{b}-\frac{\energy{a}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}\right)
=\frac{2\energy{a}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}-\mechanical{b}.
\tag{13.43}
$$

Consequently,

$$
\thermal{\mu}_{\mathrm{JT}}^{\mathrm{vdW}}
=\frac1{\thermal{c}_P}
\left(\frac{2\energy{a}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}-\mechanical{b}\right).
$$

### Inversion temperature

Set the Joule–Thomson coefficient to zero:

$$
\thermal{T}_{\mathrm{inv}}
=\frac{2\energy{a}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\mechanical{b}}
=2\thermal{T}_B
=\frac{27}{4}\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}.
$$

This agrees with Equation (9.15).

**Physical discussion.** Below the inversion temperature the coefficient is positive. An isenthalpic pressure decrease then lowers temperature. Above it the coefficient is negative and expansion heats the gas.

The formula is a leading low-density result; at high pressure the inversion temperature depends on pressure. At low density its equality to twice the Boyle temperature follows from the van der Waals second virial coefficient.

The source gives about $205\,\unit{K}$ for hydrogen's maximum inversion temperature. Hydrogen at room temperature therefore warms under a Joule–Thomson expansion and must be precooled, for example with liquid nitrogen, before a Linde–Hampson liquefaction cycle.
