## Exercise 3.1: Density of states and cumulative state count

**Task.** For the ideal gas, show that

$$
\omega(\energy{E})=\frac{3N}{2\energy{E}}\Phi(\energy{E}).
$$

Use this identity to show that $\ln\omega(\energy{E})$ and $\ln\Phi(\energy{E})$ differ by a term of order $O(\ln N)$, negligible in the thermodynamic limit.

**Solution.** For a monatomic ideal gas, separation of the position and momentum integrals in Equation (3.4) gives the explicit result (3.5):

$$
\Phi(\energy{E})=
\frac{\mechanical{V}^{N}}{\signal{h}^{3N}}
\frac{\pi^{3N/2}}{\Gamma(3N/2+1)}
(2\mechanical{m}\energy{E})^{3N/2}.
$$

All energy dependence is in the power $\energy{E}^{3N/2}$. Collect the energy-independent factors into $C(N,\mechanical{V},\mechanical{m})$:

$$
\Phi(\energy{E})=C(N,\mechanical{V},\mechanical{m})\energy{E}^{3N/2}.
$$

By definition,

$$
\omega(\energy{E})=\frac{d\Phi}{d\energy{E}}
=C\frac{3N}{2}\energy{E}^{3N/2-1}
=\frac{3N}{2\energy{E}}C\energy{E}^{3N/2}.
$$

Recognizing $\Phi$ gives

$$
\omega(\energy{E})=\frac{3N}{2\energy{E}}\Phi(\energy{E}).
\tag{13.1}
$$

Direct comparison of Equations (3.5) and (3.6) gives the same result.

### Asymptotic logarithmic estimate

Taking the logarithm of Equation (13.1),

$$
\ln\omega(\energy{E})-\ln\Phi(\energy{E})
=\ln\frac{3N}{2\energy{E}}
=\ln(3N/2)-\ln\energy{E}.
$$

In thermal equilibrium, $\energy{E}=(3/2)N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}$, as in Equation (3.8). Thus energy is extensive, of order $O(N)$, and

$$
\ln\energy{E}=\ln N+\ln[(3/2)\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}].
$$

Substitution gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ln\omega(\energy{E})-\ln\Phi(\energy{E})
&=\ln(3N/2)-\ln N-\ln[(3/2)\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}]\\
&=-\ln(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})=O(1).
\end{aligned}
$$

The source also gives the looser estimate

$$
|\ln\omega(\energy{E})-\ln\Phi(\energy{E})|
\lesssim\ln N+O(1).
$$

Boltzmann entropy is $\thermal{S}=\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\ln\Omega\sim N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}$, and $\thermal{S}/(N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B})=O(1)$.

> [!note] Dimensionless logarithms and scaling
> The source writes logarithms of the dimensionful density of states and energy. A fixed reference energy, or a fixed energy-shell width, makes these logarithms dimensionless. At fixed temperature and extensive energy the difference is $O(1)$; the stated $O(\ln N)$ bound assumes a suitable polynomial scaling of energy with particle count.

The relative size of the logarithmic correction is

$$
\frac{\ln N}{N}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}0.
$$

Likewise, $\ln\Omega=\ln(\omega\delta)$ differs from $\ln\Phi$ by $\ln[3N\delta/(2\energy{E})]$, which is negligible compared with an extensive entropy.

**Physical comment.** Equation (13.1) reflects the geometry of high-dimensional spheres. Most volume lies in a thin shell of energy thickness $\Delta\energy{E}\sim\energy{E}/N$, so $\Phi(\energy{E})\sim\omega(\energy{E})\,\energy{E}/N$. In a phase space with $3N\sim10^{23}$ dimensions, the choices $\thermal{S}=\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\ln\Phi$, $\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\ln\omega$, or $\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\ln\Omega$ give the same leading thermodynamics after the required energy normalization. Their subextensive differences explain the identification of $\Omega$ with $\Phi$ in Section 3.4.

> [!note] Logarithmic form of the shell relation
> The source summarizes the relation as $\ln\Phi\sim\ln\omega-\ln N$. Equation (13.1) also contains $\ln\energy{E}$: precisely, $\ln\Phi-\ln\omega=\ln(2\energy{E}/3N)$ with an energy reference understood. That term must be retained when energy scales with particle count.

## Exercise 3.2: Pressure, chemical potential, and Euler relation

**Task.** Use the Sackur–Tetrode formula (3.13) to calculate ideal-gas pressure and chemical potential. Verify

$$
\energy{E}=\thermal{T}\thermal{S}-\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}+\energy{\mu}N.
$$

**Solution.** Make the energy dependence explicit with

$$
\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}^2=
\frac{\signal{h}^2}{2\pi\mechanical{m}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}},
\qquad
\energy{E}=\frac32N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}.
$$

Then

$$
\thermal{S}(\energy{E},\mechanical{V},N)=
N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}
\left[
\ln\frac{\mechanical{V}}{N}
+\frac32\ln\!\left(\frac{4\pi\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{3N\signal{h}^2}\right)
+\frac52
\right].
$$

This is Equation (3.11) rearranged for differentiation with respect to energy, volume, and particle number, with the other two variables held fixed.

### Temperature

The energy-dependent entropy term is $(3N/2)\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\ln\energy{E}$. Therefore

$$
\frac1{\thermal{T}}
=\left.\frac{\partial\thermal{S}}{\partial\energy{E}}\right|_{V,N}
=\frac{3N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}{2\energy{E}},
$$

in agreement with Equation (3.8).

### Pressure

The volume term is $N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\ln\mechanical{V}$:

$$
\frac{\mechanical{P}}{\thermal{T}}
=\left.\frac{\partial\thermal{S}}{\partial\mechanical{V}}\right|_{E,N}
=\frac{N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}{\mechanical{V}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}=N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}.
\tag{13.2}
$$

This recovers the ideal-gas equation of state independently of the Gibbs factor $1/N!$, since that factor has no volume dependence.

### Chemical potential

Rewrite the entropy as

$$
\frac{\thermal{S}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}
=N\ln\mechanical{V}-N\ln N
+\frac{3N}{2}\ln\frac{2\pi\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{\signal{h}^2}
-\frac{3N}{2}\ln\frac{3N}{2}
+\frac{5N}{2}.
$$

At fixed energy and volume, differentiate term by term:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial(N\ln\mechanical{V})}{\partial N}&=\ln\mechanical{V},\\
\frac{\partial(N\ln N)}{\partial N}&=\ln N+1,\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial N}
\left[\frac{3N}{2}\ln\frac{2\pi\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{\signal{h}^2}\right]
&=\frac32\ln\frac{2\pi\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{\signal{h}^2},\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial N}\left[\frac{3N}{2}\ln\frac{3N}{2}\right]
&=\frac32\ln\frac{3N}{2}+\frac32,\\
\frac{\partial(5N/2)}{\partial N}&=\frac52.
\end{aligned}
$$

Combining with the correct signs,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac1{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}
\left.\frac{\partial\thermal{S}}{\partial N}\right|_{E,V}
&=\ln\mechanical{V}-\ln N-1
+\frac32\ln\frac{2\pi\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{\signal{h}^2}
-\frac32\ln\frac{3N}{2}-\frac32+\frac52\\
&=\ln\frac{\mechanical{V}}N
+\frac32\ln\frac{4\pi\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{3N\signal{h}^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Use

$$
\frac{4\pi\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{3N\signal{h}^2}
=\frac{2\pi\mechanical{m}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}{\signal{h}^2}
=\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}^{-2},
\qquad n=\frac{N}{\mechanical{V}}.
$$

Then

$$
\left.\frac{\partial\thermal{S}}{\partial N}\right|_{E,V}
=\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\ln\frac{\mechanical{V}}{N\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}^3}
=-\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\ln(n\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}^3).
$$

The statistical definition in Section 3.3 gives

$$
\energy{\mu}
=-\thermal{T}\left.\frac{\partial\thermal{S}}{\partial N}\right|_{E,V}
=\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}\ln(n\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}^3).
\tag{13.3}
$$

In the classical limit, $n\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}^3\ll1$, so the chemical potential is negative.

### Euler relation

Use Equations (3.13), (13.2), and (13.3):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\thermal{T}\thermal{S}
&=N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
\left[\ln\frac{\mechanical{V}}{N\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}^3}+\frac52\right],\\
-\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}&=-N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T},\\
\energy{\mu}N
&=N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
\ln\frac{N\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}^3}{\mechanical{V}}
=-N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
\ln\frac{\mechanical{V}}{N\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}^3}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Adding the three terms,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\thermal{T}\thermal{S}-\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}+\energy{\mu}N
&=N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
\ln\frac{\mechanical{V}}{N\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}^3}
+\frac52N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}\\
&\quad-N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
-N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
\ln\frac{\mechanical{V}}{N\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}^3}\\
&=\frac32N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}=\energy{E}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus the Sackur–Tetrode formula satisfies the Euler relation

$$
\energy{E}=\thermal{T}\thermal{S}-\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}+\energy{\mu}N,
$$

and is consistent with the Gibbs–Duhem relation.

> [!note] Thermal-wavelength notation
> The source also writes the thermal wavelength as $\signal{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}$. Both forms denote the same quantity here.

**Physical comment.** Correct Gibbs counting, $\Phi\to\Phi/N!$, gives an entropy homogeneous of first degree in energy, volume, and particle number. It is extensive. In the classical dilute limit, the negative chemical potential reflects the entropy gained by adding a particle at fixed energy and volume.

## Exercise 3.3: One-dimensional ideal gas

**Task.** Consider $N$ particles of mass $\mechanical{m}$ in a free one-dimensional box of length $\mechanical{L}$. Calculate $\Phi(\energy{E})$, $\thermal{S}(\energy{E},\mechanical{L},N)$, the force

$$
\mechanical{\mathcal F}=\thermal{T}\frac{\partial\thermal{S}}{\partial\mechanical{L}},
$$

and the one-dimensional equation of state.

**Solution.** Phase space has dimension $2N$ with coordinates $(\mechanical{q}_1,\ldots,\mechanical{q}_N;\mechanical{p}_1,\ldots,\mechanical{p}_N)$ and Hamiltonian

$$
\energy{\mathcal H}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\mechanical{p}_i^2}{2\mechanical{m}}.
$$

The condition $\energy{\mathcal H}\le\energy{E}$ gives $\sum_i\mechanical{p}_i^2\le2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}$. The momentum domain is an N-dimensional ball of radius $\sqrt{2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}$. Independent position integrals over $[0,\mechanical{L}]$ give $\mechanical{L}^N$.

### (a) Cumulative phase volume and entropy

Include the Gibbs factor $1/N!$ and divide by $\signal{h}^N$, one factor for each position–momentum pair:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Phi(\energy{E},\mechanical{L},N)
&=\frac1{N!\signal{h}^N}
\int_{\mathcal H\le E}d^N\mechanical{q}\,d^N\mechanical{p}\\
&=\frac{\mechanical{L}^N}{N!\signal{h}^N}
V_N\!\left(\sqrt{2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}\right)\\
&=\frac{\mechanical{L}^N}{N!\signal{h}^N}
\frac{\pi^{N/2}}{\Gamma(N/2+1)}
(2\mechanical{m}\energy{E})^{N/2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This uses Equation (3.1) with dimension $n=N$. Here $V_N$ denotes the mathematical volume of the momentum ball.

The thermodynamic-limit entropy, $\thermal{S}\approx\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\ln\Phi$, is

$$
\frac{\thermal{S}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}
=N\ln\mechanical{L}-\ln N!-N\ln\signal{h}
+\frac N2\ln(2\mechanical{m}\energy{E})
+\frac N2\ln\pi-\ln\Gamma(N/2+1).
$$

Use $\ln N!\approx N\ln N-N$ and $\ln\Gamma(N/2+1)\approx(N/2)\ln(N/2)-N/2$:

$$
\frac{\thermal{S}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}
\approx N\ln\mechanical{L}-N\ln N+N
+\frac N2\ln(2\pi\mechanical{m}\energy{E})
-\frac N2\ln\frac N2+\frac N2-N\ln\signal{h}.
$$

Collecting logarithms gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\thermal{S}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}
&=N\ln\frac{\mechanical{L}}N
+\frac N2\ln\frac{2\pi\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{\signal{h}^2}
-\frac N2\ln N+\frac N2\ln2+\frac{3N}{2}\\
&=N\ln\frac{\mechanical{L}}N
+\frac N2\ln\frac{4\pi\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{N\signal{h}^2}
+\frac{3N}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

With the thermal wavelength (3.12) and the one-dimensional energy relation $\energy{E}=N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}/2$, this becomes

$$
\thermal{S}(\energy{E},\mechanical{L},N)=
N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}
\left[\ln\frac{\mechanical{L}}{N\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}}+\frac32\right].
\tag{13.4}
$$

It is extensive: under $(\energy{E},\mechanical{L},N)\to(\eta\energy{E},\eta\mechanical{L},\eta N)$, the thermal wavelength stays fixed and $\thermal{S}\to\eta\thermal{S}$.

### (b) Force

Only $N\ln\mechanical{L}$ depends on length:

$$
\left.\frac{\partial\thermal{S}}{\partial\mechanical{L}}\right|_{E,N}
=\frac{N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}{\mechanical{L}}.
$$

Thus

$$
\mechanical{\mathcal F}
=\thermal{T}\left.\frac{\partial\thermal{S}}{\partial\mechanical{L}}\right|_{E,N}
=\frac{N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}{\mechanical{L}}.
\tag{13.5}
$$

Energy divided by length has units of force. A one-dimensional gas exerts a force on its end walls, rather than a three-dimensional pressure.

### (c) One-dimensional equation of state

Equation (13.5) becomes

$$
\mechanical{\mathcal F}\mechanical{L}=N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}.
$$

It has the same form as $\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}=N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}$, with pressure replaced by force and volume replaced by length. Also,

$$
\frac1{\thermal{T}}
=\left.\frac{\partial\thermal{S}}{\partial\energy{E}}\right|_{L,N}
=\frac{N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}{2\energy{E}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\energy{E}=\frac12N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}.
$$

This agrees with equipartition: one quadratic kinetic degree of freedom per particle.

**Physical comment.** Replace three translational degrees of freedom by one. Then $\thermal{C}_V=N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}/2$ rather than $3N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}/2$, and the internal energy changes by the same factor. More generally,

$$
P_dV_d=N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T},
\qquad
\energy{E}=\frac d2N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T},
$$

where $P_d$ is conjugate to the d-dimensional volume $V_d$.

## Exercise 3.4: Spin-½ system in a magnetic field

**Task.** For the N-spin system of Example 3.1 in field $\magnetic{B}$:

1. Find the equilibrium energy $\energy{E}^*(\thermal{T},N,\magnetic{B})$.
2. Calculate $\thermal{C}=\partial\energy{E}^*/\partial\thermal{T}$ and show that it has a Schottky peak.
3. Show that $0\le\thermal{S}\le N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\ln2$ and discuss the limits.

### (a) Equilibrium energy

Equation (3.17) gives

$$
\frac{n_+}{n_-}
=e^{2\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{n_+-n_-}{n_++n_-}
=\tanh\!\left(\frac{\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}\right).
$$

Set $\thermal{\beta}=1/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})$ and $x=\thermal{\beta}\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}$. Then $n_+-n_-=N\tanh x$. From $\energy{E}=-(n_+-n_-)\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}$,

$$
\energy{E}^*(\thermal{T},N,\magnetic{B})
=-N\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}
\tanh\!\left(\frac{\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}\right).
\tag{13.6}
$$

**Limits.** As $\thermal{T}\to0$, all spins align with the field and $\energy{E}^*\to-N\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}$. As $\thermal{T}\to\infty$, the populations become equal and $\energy{E}^*\to0$.

### (b) Heat capacity

Let $y=\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})$. Use

$$
\frac{d}{d\thermal{T}}\tanh y
=(1-\tanh^2y)\frac{dy}{d\thermal{T}},
\qquad
\frac{dy}{d\thermal{T}}=-\frac y{\thermal{T}}.
$$

Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\thermal{C}
&=\left.\frac{\partial\energy{E}^*}{\partial\thermal{T}}\right|_{N,B}
=-N\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}(1-\tanh^2y)\left(-\frac y{\thermal{T}}\right)\\
&=\frac{N(\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B})^2}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}^2}
\operatorname{sech}^2\!\left(\frac{\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\thermal{C}=N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}y^2\operatorname{sech}^2y,
\qquad
y=\frac{\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}.
\tag{13.7}
$$

For the **Schottky peak**, consider $f(y)=y^2/\cosh^2y$. This positive function tends to zero at both $y=0$ and $y\to\infty$, since $\cosh^2y\sim e^{2y}/4$. Its maximum satisfies

$$
f'(y)=
\frac{2y\cosh^2y-2y^2\cosh y\sinh y}{\cosh^4y}
=\frac{2y(\cosh y-y\sinh y)}{\cosh^3y}=0.
$$

For positive $y$, this is $\coth y=y$. Numerically,

$$
y_*\approx1.1997,
\qquad
\thermal{T}_{\mathrm{peak}}
=\frac{\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}y_*}
\approx0.834\,\frac{\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}.
$$

The maximum heat capacity is

$$
\thermal{C}_{\max}\approx
N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\frac{y_*^2}{\cosh^2y_*}
\approx0.439\,N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}.
$$

**Limits:**

- $\thermal{T}\to0$, or $y\to\infty$: $\thermal{C}\sim4N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}y^2e^{-2y}\to0$. Spins are frozen into the ground state, with no thermal excitations.
- $\thermal{T}\to\infty$, or $y\to0$: $\thermal{C}\sim N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}y^2\to0$ as $1/\thermal{T}^2$. The populations approach equality and the energy saturates.

A finite maximum between these limits is characteristic of a bounded spectrum. Here two levels are separated by $\energy{\Delta}=2\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}$. This is the **Schottky peak**, characteristic of two-level or few-level systems, often observed in paramagnetic salts at low temperature.

### (c) Entropy bounds

The thermodynamic-limit binomial expression (3.16) gives

$$
\thermal{S}=-N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}
[f_+\ln f_++f_-\ln f_-],
\qquad
f_\pm=\frac{n_\pm}{N}\in[0,1],
\quad f_++f_-=1.
$$

The binary entropy $h(f)=-[f\ln f+(1-f)\ln(1-f)]$ is concave on $[0,1]$, vanishes at both endpoints, and has maximum $h(1/2)=\ln2$. Indeed, $h'(f)=\ln[(1-f)/f]=0$ at $f=1/2$. Therefore

$$
0\le\thermal{S}\le N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\ln2.
$$

For $\thermal{T}\to0$, Equation (13.6) gives $n_+=N$, $n_-=0$, hence $f_+=1$ and $f_-=0$. With $0\ln0=0$,

$$
\thermal{S}=0.
$$

All spins occupy the unique ground state. There is no statistical uncertainty, consistent with the third law of thermodynamics.

For $\thermal{T}\to\infty$ or $\magnetic{B}\to0$ at fixed nonzero temperature, the ratio $\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})\to0$. Both populations approach $N/2$, and

$$
\thermal{S}
=-N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}
\left[\frac12\ln\frac12+\frac12\ln\frac12\right]
=N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\ln2.
$$

The source relates this to $\Omega=\binom{N}{N/2}\sim2^N$ at exponential order. At infinite temperature, all spin configurations are equally probable.

**Physical comment.** A bounded spin spectrum has a finite maximum entropy, unlike the ideal gas. This bounded spectrum also allows negative temperatures.

> [!note] State counting and the entropy maximum
> The fixed-energy shell at equal populations contains $\binom{N}{N/2}\sim2^N\sqrt{2/(\pi N)}$ states, not all $2^N$ configurations. The distinction is subextensive in entropy. Also, the entropy is maximal at zero spin energy; it decreases again toward the upper energy bound. It does not stay at its maximum as energy increases through the whole bounded spectrum.

## Exercise 3.5: Negative temperature

**Task.** Show that the N-spin system permits

$$
\frac1{\thermal{T}}=\frac{\partial\thermal{S}}{\partial\energy{E}}<0
$$

for positive energy, and explain why a negative-temperature state is hotter than every positive-temperature state.

**Solution.** Equation (3.15) gives

$$
n_+=\frac N2\left(1-\frac{\energy{E}}{N\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}}\right),
\qquad
n_-=\frac N2\left(1+\frac{\energy{E}}{N\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}}\right).
$$

Define the dimensionless spin energy

$$
\energy{\varepsilon}=\frac{\energy{E}}{N\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}}\in[-1,1].
$$

Then

$$
f_+=\frac{1-\energy{\varepsilon}}2,
\qquad
f_-=\frac{1+\energy{\varepsilon}}2.
$$

Substitution into Equation (3.16) gives

$$
\thermal{S}(\energy{E},N,\magnetic{B})
=-N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}
\left[
\frac{1-\energy{\varepsilon}}2\ln\frac{1-\energy{\varepsilon}}2
+\frac{1+\energy{\varepsilon}}2\ln\frac{1+\energy{\varepsilon}}2
\right].
\tag{13.8}
$$

This function is even in reduced energy and concave. It has its maximum at $\energy{\varepsilon}=0$, where both populations are equal, and vanishes at $\energy{\varepsilon}=\pm1$. Reversal of the field or all spins interchanges the two orientations.

### Temperature as entropy slope

Since $\energy{E}=N\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}\energy{\varepsilon}$,

$$
\frac1{\thermal{T}}
=\left.\frac{\partial\thermal{S}}{\partial\energy{E}}\right|_{N,B}
=\frac1{N\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}}\frac{d\thermal{S}}{d\energy{\varepsilon}}
=\frac{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}{2\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}}
\ln\frac{1-\energy{\varepsilon}}{1+\energy{\varepsilon}}.
$$

Equivalently, this recovers Equation (3.17):

$$
\frac1{\thermal{T}}
=\frac{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}{2\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}}
\ln\frac{n_+}{n_-}.
$$

- For $-1<\energy{\varepsilon}<0$, the lower level is more occupied: $n_+>n_-$, so $\thermal{T}>0$.
- For $\energy{\varepsilon}=0$, both populations are equal and $1/\thermal{T}=0$. The positive- and negative-infinite temperature limits meet here.
- For $0<\energy{\varepsilon}<1$, the upper level is more occupied: **population inversion**, with $\thermal{T}<0$.
- For $\energy{\varepsilon}\to1$, all spins oppose the field, $1/\thermal{T}\to-\infty$, and $\thermal{T}\to0^-$.

As energy increases from its lower bound to its upper bound, temperature follows

$$
0^+\longrightarrow+\infty\equiv-\infty\longrightarrow0^-.
$$

Negative temperatures follow from the bounded spectrum: entropy has a maximum at zero energy, so its slope is negative above that energy.

### Hotter than any positive temperature

The second law gives the operational meaning of temperature. If two systems exchange a small heat amount,

$$
d\thermal{S}_{\mathrm{tot}}
=\left(\frac1{\thermal{T}_A}-\frac1{\thermal{T}_B}\right)d\thermal{Q}_A.
$$

Heat flows from the system with smaller inverse temperature toward the system with larger inverse temperature, increasing total entropy. Thus **hotter means smaller $1/\thermal{T}$**, or larger $-1/\thermal{T}$.

Every positive-temperature state has $1/\thermal{T}>0$, while every negative-temperature state has $1/\thermal{T}<0$. Consequently heat flows from a negative-temperature system to a positive-temperature system. The hotness order is

$$
0^+\prec_{\mathrm{hot}}
\text{finite positive temperatures}\prec_{\mathrm{hot}}
+\infty\equiv-\infty\prec_{\mathrm{hot}}
\text{finite negative temperatures}\prec_{\mathrm{hot}}0^-.
$$

This is an order of increasing hotness, not an ordinary numerical inequality between temperatures. The two infinite-temperature limits represent the same equal-population, maximum-entropy state.

**Physical comment.** Negative temperatures require a spectrum bounded above. Examples include spin systems, restricted atomic-level populations in laser systems, and optical-lattice systems with bounded bands. For an unbounded spectrum, such as an ideal gas with unlimited kinetic energy, entropy increases with energy and equilibrium temperature stays positive. The source credits Purcell and Pound (1951) with the historical observation of negative temperatures in nuclear-spin systems in salts.

> [!note] Degrees of freedom
> A negative temperature can describe bounded degrees of freedom in a prepared subsystem. It does not describe unbounded translational motion in equilibrium with that subsystem.

## Exercise 3.6: Electron thermal wavelength

**Task.** Calculate the electron thermal wavelength at $300\,\unit{K}$ and $10^4\,\unit{K}$. Compare it with the typical spacing in a metal with $n_{\mathrm e}\sim10^{28}\,\unit{m^{-3}}$, and explain why the electron gas is not classical.

**Solution.** Equation (3.12) defines

$$
\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}
=\frac{\signal{h}}{\sqrt{2\pi\mechanical{m}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}}.
$$

For an electron, $\mechanical{m}_{\mathrm e}=9.109\times10^{-31}\,\unit{kg}$.

### At 300 K

Using the source's rounded constants,

$$
2\pi\mechanical{m}_{\mathrm e}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
=2\pi(9.109\times10^{-31})(1.381\times10^{-23})(300)
\approx2.371\times10^{-50}\,\unit{J\,kg}.
$$

The square root is $1.540\times10^{-25}\,\unit{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$, so

$$
\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}(300\,\unit{K})
\approx\frac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{1.540\times10^{-25}}\,\unit{m}
\approx4.30\times10^{-9}\,\unit{m}
\approx43\,\unit{Å}.
$$

### At 10⁴ K

Since the wavelength scales as $\thermal{T}^{-1/2}$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}(10^4\,\unit{K})
&=\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}(300\,\unit{K})\sqrt{300/10^4}\\
&\approx(4.30\times10^{-9})(0.1732)\,\unit{m}\\
&\approx7.45\times10^{-10}\,\unit{m}\approx7.4\,\unit{Å}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Direct calculation gives $2\pi\mechanical{m}_{\mathrm e}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T} \approx7.903\times10^{-49}\,\unit{J\,kg}$, with square root $8.890\times10^{-25}\,\unit{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$, and the same wavelength.

### Electron spacing

For the typical metal density,

$$
\mechanical{d}\sim n_{\mathrm e}^{-1/3}
=(10^{28})^{-1/3}\,\unit{m}
\approx4.64\times10^{-10}\,\unit{m}
\approx4.6\,\unit{Å}.
$$

### Wavelength comparison and degeneracy parameter

| Temperature | Thermal wavelength | Wavelength / spacing | Degeneracy parameter |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $300\,\unit{K}$ | $43\,\unit{Å}$ | $\sim9$ | $n_{\mathrm e}\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}^3\sim8\times10^2$ |
| $10^4\,\unit{K}$ | $7.4\,\unit{Å}$ | $\sim1.6$ | $n_{\mathrm e}\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}^3\sim4$ |

Explicitly,

$$
n_{\mathrm e}\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}^3(300\,\unit{K})
=10^{28}(4.30\times10^{-9})^3\approx7.95\times10^2,
$$

and

$$
n_{\mathrm e}\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}^3(10^4\,\unit{K})
=10^{28}(7.45\times10^{-10})^3\approx4.1.
$$

In both cases,

$$
n_{\mathrm e}\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}^3
=\left(\frac{\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}}{\mechanical{d}}\right)^3>1.
$$

The classical limit instead requires a degeneracy parameter much smaller than one.

**Physical comment.** The condition $n\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}^3\ll1$ means that particle wave packets do not significantly overlap. Only then can particles be treated as localized classical objects and the Sackur–Tetrode formula applied.

For conduction electrons in a metal, one electron wave packet spans many interatomic distances. Wavefunctions overlap, and the Pauli exclusion principle is essential. Fermi–Dirac statistics is required. The gas is degenerate when $\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}\ll\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}$. A typical scale is

$$
\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}\sim5\,\unit{eV}
\sim\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}(6\times10^4\,\unit{K}).
$$

Even at $10^4\,\unit{K}$, above ordinary metal melting temperatures, the density estimate remains nonclassical. Classical behaviour would require much lower density or temperatures well above the Fermi-temperature scale, conditions not present in ordinary conductors.
