## Exercise 12.1: A sixth-order Landau expansion

*For the Landau functional $\energy{F}(\phi)=\energy{a}\thermal{t}\phi^2+\energy{b}\phi^4+\energy{c}\phi^6$, with $\energy{b}<0$ and $\energy{c}>0$, find (a) the reduced transition temperature, (b) the order-parameter jump, and (c) the first-order character of the transition.*

Write $\energy{b}=-\lvert\energy{b}\rvert$, where $\lvert\energy{b}\rvert>0$. Then

$$
\energy{F}(\phi)
=\energy{a}\thermal{t}\phi^2-\lvert\energy{b}\rvert\phi^4+\energy{c}\phi^6.
$$

This has the form of (12.3), with $\energy{a}_2=\energy{a}\thermal{t}$, $\lvert\energy{a}_4\rvert=\lvert\energy{b}\rvert$, and $\energy{a}_6=\energy{c}$. The free energy has $\mathbb Z_2$ symmetry, $\energy{F}(\phi)=\energy{F}(-\phi)$, so it is sufficient to examine $\phi\ge0$.

### (a) Transition temperature

The stationary condition is

$$
\frac{d\energy{F}}{d\phi}
=2\energy{a}\thermal{t}\phi-4\lvert\energy{b}\rvert\phi^3
+6\energy{c}\phi^5
=2\phi(\energy{a}\thermal{t}-2\lvert\energy{b}\rvert\phi^2
+3\energy{c}\phi^4)=0.
\tag{13.57}
$$

The solution $\phi=0$ is a local minimum for $\thermal{t}>0$. Nonzero stationary points obey

$$
3\energy{c}\phi^4-2\lvert\energy{b}\rvert\phi^2
+\energy{a}\thermal{t}=0,
\qquad
\phi_\pm^2
=\frac{\lvert\energy{b}\rvert
\pm\sqrt{\lvert\energy{b}\rvert^2-3\energy{a}\energy{c}\thermal{t}}}
{3\energy{c}}.
$$

For $0<\energy{a}\thermal{t}<\lvert\energy{b}\rvert^2/(3\energy{c})$, both squared roots are positive. The plus sign gives the nonzero minimum. The minus sign gives the intervening maximum. For $\thermal{t}<0$ with $\energy{a}>0$, the minus root is negative, and only the plus root gives a nonzero minimum.

The first-order transition occurs when $\energy{F}(\phi_+)=\energy{F}(0)=0$: the two minima have equal depths. The coexistence conditions are

$$
\begin{cases}
\energy{a}\thermal{t}\phi_+^2
-\lvert\energy{b}\rvert\phi_+^4+\energy{c}\phi_+^6=0,\\
\energy{a}\thermal{t}-2\lvert\energy{b}\rvert\phi_+^2
+3\energy{c}\phi_+^4=0.
\end{cases}
$$

The first gives $\energy{a}\thermal{t}=\lvert\energy{b}\rvert\phi_+^2-\energy{c}\phi_+^4$. Substitute into the second:

$$
-\lvert\energy{b}\rvert\phi_+^2+2\energy{c}\phi_+^4=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\left.\phi_+^2\right|_{\thermal{T}_c}
=\frac{\lvert\energy{b}\rvert}{2\energy{c}}.
\tag{13.58}
$$

Substitution back into the first condition gives

$$
\energy{a}\thermal{t}_c
=\frac{\lvert\energy{b}\rvert^2}{2\energy{c}}
-\frac{\lvert\energy{b}\rvert^2}{4\energy{c}}
=\frac{\lvert\energy{b}\rvert^2}{4\energy{c}}.
$$

Thus

$$
\thermal{t}_c
=\frac{\lvert\energy{b}\rvert^2}{4\energy{a}\energy{c}}>0.
\tag{13.59}
$$

### (b) Order-parameter jump

At coexistence, the nonzero minimum is

$$
\phi_c\equiv\phi_+(\thermal{t}_c)
=\sqrt{\frac{\lvert\energy{b}\rvert}{2\energy{c}}}.
$$

Above the transition, the global minimum is $\phi=0$. At the transition, the equilibrium order parameter jumps to $\phi_c$. Therefore

$$
\Delta\phi=\phi_c
=\sqrt{\frac{\lvert\energy{b}\rvert}{2\energy{c}}}.
\tag{13.60}
$$

> [!note] Source clarification
> The source calls $\phi=0$ the only minimum above $\thermal{t}_c$. It is the only global minimum there, but nonzero metastable minima persist up to $\energy{a}\thermal{t}=\lvert\energy{b}\rvert^2/(3\energy{c})$.

### (c) First-order character

The finite jump $\Delta\phi\ne0$ identifies a first-order transition. Equivalently, the equilibrium free energy has a discontinuous first temperature derivative. The entropy $\thermal{S}=-\partial\energy{F}/\partial\thermal{T}$ jumps, which gives nonzero latent heat $\energy{L}=\thermal{T}_c\Delta\thermal{S}$.

The continuous transition is recovered as $\energy{b}\to0^-$: both $\thermal{t}_c\to0$ and $\Delta\phi\to0$. The point $\energy{b}=0$ is the tricritical point.

### Dimensions and limiting cases

For dimensionless reduced temperature,

$$
[\energy{a}]=[\energy{F}]\phi^{-2},
\qquad
[\energy{b}]=[\energy{F}]\phi^{-4},
\qquad
[\energy{c}]=[\energy{F}]\phi^{-6},
$$

so

$$
[\thermal{t}_c]
=\frac{[\energy{F}]^2\phi^{-8}}
{([\energy{F}]\phi^{-2})([\energy{F}]\phi^{-6})}=1.
$$

The source takes $[\energy{F}]$ as energy per volume. For fixed $\energy{c}>0$, the limit $\lvert\energy{b}\rvert\to0$ recovers the tricritical exponent $\beta_{\mathrm{crit}}=1/4$, as in part 2.

> [!note] Temperature notation
> The source uses $\thermal{t}=(\thermal{T}-\thermal{T}_c)/\thermal{T}_c$ while also giving a nonzero transition value $\thermal{t}_c$. In that definition, the temperature denoted by $\thermal{T}_c$ must be the reference temperature where the quadratic coefficient vanishes. The actual first-order transition occurs at the positive shift (13.59).

## Exercise 12.2: Three decimation steps for the Ising chain

*Start with $K_0=1.0$. (a) Calculate $K_1$, $K_2$, and $K_3$. (b) Verify that $2^n\xi(K_n)$ is approximately constant, where $\xi=-1/\ln(\tanh K)$. (c) Discuss the limit $K\to\infty$.*

The renormalization transformation (12.7), with scale factor $b_{\mathrm{RG}}=2$, is

$$
K'=\frac12\ln\cosh(2K).
$$

### (a) Numerical iterations

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_0&=1.00000,\\
K_1&=\tfrac12\ln\cosh(2.0000)
=\tfrac12\ln(3.7622)=0.66250,\\
K_2&=\tfrac12\ln\cosh(1.3250)
=\tfrac12\ln(2.0140)=0.35006,\\
K_3&=\tfrac12\ln\cosh(0.7001)
=\tfrac12\ln(1.2553)=0.11367.
\end{aligned}
$$

The sequence flows towards the stable fixed point $K^*=0$.

### (b) Correlation-length scaling

The exact chain correlation length is

$$
\xi(K)=-\frac1{\ln(\tanh K)}.
\tag{13.61}
$$

At each decimation level, measure $\xi(K_n)$ in units of that level's lattice spacing.

| Step $n$ | $K_n$ | $\tanh K_n$ | $\xi(K_n)=-1/\ln(\tanh K_n)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 1.00000 | 0.76159 | 3.6720 |
| 1 | 0.66250 | 0.58003 | 1.8359 |
| 2 | 0.35006 | 0.33643 | 0.9180 |
| 3 | 0.11367 | 0.11318 | 0.4590 |

Convert back to units of the original lattice spacing:

$$
\xi_n^{(0)}=2^n\xi(K_n).
$$

The first two values are

$$
\xi_0^{(0)}=3.672,
\qquad
\xi_1^{(0)}=2(1.8359)=3.672.
$$

> [!note] Source correction
> One sentence on the source page states $\xi(K_n)/2^n$ for the conversion to original lattice units. The exercise statement and subsequent calculations correctly use $2^n\xi(K_n)$.

The remaining values are

$$
\xi_2^{(0)}=4(0.9180)=3.672,
\qquad
\xi_3^{(0)}=8(0.4590)=3.672.
$$

Thus $2^n\xi(K_n)=\xi(K_0)$ within rounding errors. The correlation length measured in original lattice units is invariant under renormalization.

### (c) The limit of infinite coupling

For $K\to\infty$, or $\thermal{T}\to0$, use $\cosh(2K)\approx e^{2K}/2$:

$$
R(K)\approx\frac12\ln\left(\frac{e^{2K}}2\right)
=K-\frac{\ln2}{2}.
\tag{13.62}
$$

Each iteration decreases the large coupling by approximately $\ln2/2\approx0.347$. Even from very large finite coupling, the system reaches $K\sim O(1)$ after approximately $n\sim2K/\ln2$ steps. It then approaches $K^*=0$ rapidly.

The fixed point $K^*=\infty$ is unstable: any small thermal perturbation is relevant. The correlation length has an essential singularity, $\xi\sim e^{2K}$, rather than a power-law singularity. A formal identification $y_t=1$ from $\xi(K_{n+1})=\xi(K_n)/2$ therefore does not have the usual meaning of $\xi\sim b_{\mathrm{RG}}^{y_t}$ near a finite-temperature critical point.

The chain has a transition only at zero temperature. This agrees with the lower critical dimension $d_c^{\mathrm{inf}}=1$ for $\mathbb Z_2$ symmetry discussed in Chapter 12.

## Exercise 12.3: The Ornstein–Zernike function in three dimensions

*Calculate the inverse three-dimensional Fourier transform of $\widetilde G(\signal{k})=1/[c_G(\signal{k}^2+\mechanical{\xi}^{-2})]$. Show that $G(\mechanical{r})=e^{-\mechanical{r}/\mechanical{\xi}}/(4\pi c_G\mechanical{r})$.*

By definition,

$$
G(\mechanical{\mathbf r})
=\int\frac{d^3\signal{k}}{(2\pi)^3}
\,\widetilde G(\signal{k})e^{i\signal{\mathbf k}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf r}}
=\frac1{c_G}\int\frac{d^3\signal{k}}{(2\pi)^3}
\frac{e^{i\signal{\mathbf k}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf r}}}
{\signal{k}^2+\mechanical{\xi}^{-2}}.
\tag{13.63}
$$

Here $c_G$ denotes the source's coefficient $c$, to distinguish it from the sixth-order Landau coefficient in Exercise 12.1.

### Angular integration

Choose spherical coordinates with polar angle $\theta$ between $\signal{\mathbf k}$ and $\mechanical{\mathbf r}$. Then $d^3\signal{k}=\signal{k}^2\sin\theta\,d\signal{k}\,d\theta\,d\varphi$ and $\signal{\mathbf k}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf r}=\signal{k}\mechanical{r}\cos\theta$. The azimuthal integral gives $2\pi$, while

$$
\int_0^\pi e^{i\signal{k}\mechanical{r}\cos\theta}\sin\theta\,d\theta
=\int_{-1}^1e^{i\signal{k}\mechanical{r}u}\,du
=\frac{2\sin(\signal{k}\mechanical{r})}{\signal{k}\mechanical{r}}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
G(\mechanical{r})
&=\frac{2\pi}{c_G(2\pi)^3}
\int_0^\infty
\frac{\signal{k}^2}{\signal{k}^2+\mechanical{\xi}^{-2}}
\frac{2\sin(\signal{k}\mechanical{r})}{\signal{k}\mechanical{r}}
\,d\signal{k}\\
&=\frac1{2\pi^2c_G\mechanical{r}}
\int_0^\infty
\frac{\signal{k}\sin(\signal{k}\mechanical{r})}
{\signal{k}^2+\mechanical{\xi}^{-2}}
\,d\signal{k}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{13.64}
$$

### Residue evaluation

The radial integrand is even when extended to the real line. Hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
I
&=\int_0^\infty
\frac{\signal{k}\sin(\signal{k}\mechanical{r})}
{\signal{k}^2+\mechanical{\xi}^{-2}}\,d\signal{k}\\
&=\frac12\int_{-\infty}^\infty
\frac{\signal{k}\sin(\signal{k}\mechanical{r})}
{\signal{k}^2+\mechanical{\xi}^{-2}}\,d\signal{k}\\
&=\frac12\operatorname{Im}
\int_{-\infty}^\infty
\frac{\signal{k}e^{i\signal{k}\mechanical{r}}}
{\signal{k}^2+\mechanical{\xi}^{-2}}\,d\signal{k}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The complex integrand has simple poles at $\signal{k}=\pm i/\mechanical{\xi}$. For $\mechanical{r}>0$, close the contour in the upper half-plane. Only the positive-imaginary pole is enclosed:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname*{Res}_{\signal{k}=i/\mechanical{\xi}}
\frac{\signal{k}e^{i\signal{k}\mechanical{r}}}
{\signal{k}^2+\mechanical{\xi}^{-2}}
&=\lim_{\signal{k}\to i/\mechanical{\xi}}
\frac{\signal{k}e^{i\signal{k}\mechanical{r}}}
{\signal{k}+i/\mechanical{\xi}}\\
&=\frac{(i/\mechanical{\xi})e^{-\mechanical{r}/\mechanical{\xi}}}
{2i/\mechanical{\xi}}
=\frac12e^{-\mechanical{r}/\mechanical{\xi}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The residue theorem gives

$$
\int_{-\infty}^\infty
\frac{\signal{k}e^{i\signal{k}\mechanical{r}}}
{\signal{k}^2+\mechanical{\xi}^{-2}}\,d\signal{k}
=i\pi e^{-\mechanical{r}/\mechanical{\xi}},
\qquad
I=\frac\pi2e^{-\mechanical{r}/\mechanical{\xi}}.
$$

Insert this into (13.64):

$$
G(\mechanical{r})
=\frac1{2\pi^2c_G\mechanical{r}}
\frac\pi2e^{-\mechanical{r}/\mechanical{\xi}}
=\frac{e^{-\mechanical{r}/\mechanical{\xi}}}
{4\pi c_G\mechanical{r}}.
\tag{13.65}
$$

### Limiting cases and dimensions

- At the critical point, $\mechanical{\xi}\to\infty$ and $G(\mechanical{r})\to1/(4\pi c_G\mechanical{r})$. The Coulomb-like decay $\mechanical{r}^{-1}=\mechanical{r}^{-(d-2)}$ in $d=3$ agrees with $\eta=0$ in Gaussian Landau–Ginzburg theory.
- For $\mechanical{r}\gg\mechanical{\xi}$, the exponential factor determines the decay scale. This gives an operational definition of the correlation length.
- If the functional (12.4) is expressed in units of $\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}$, its action is dimensionless. In three dimensions, $[c_G]=\mathrm{length}^{-1}[\phi]^{-2}$ and $[\widetilde G]=[\phi]^2\mathrm{length}^{d}$. The inverse transform has $[G]=[\phi]^2$, as required for the connected correlation $\langle\phi(\mechanical{\mathbf r})\phi(0)\rangle_c$.

### Physical meaning

Equation (13.65) is the three-dimensional Ornstein–Zernike function. It has the form of a Yukawa propagator: the inverse correlation length acts as the scalar-field mass scale. The decay $e^{-\mechanical{r}/\mechanical{\xi}}/\mechanical{r}$ is the statistical analogue of a Yukawa potential with range $\mechanical{\xi}$. At criticality, the system has no finite correlation-length scale and the propagator becomes a power law.

## Exercise 12.4: Scaling relations and mean-field exponents

*Check the Rushbrooke, Widom, and Fisher relations for $\alpha=0$, $\beta_{\mathrm{crit}}=1/2$, $\gamma_{\mathrm{crit}}=1$, $\delta=3$, $\nu=1/2$, and $\eta=0$. Check the Josephson relation $2-\alpha=d\nu$, and find the dimension where it holds.*

### (a) Rushbrooke relation

$$
\alpha+2\beta_{\mathrm{crit}}+\gamma_{\mathrm{crit}}=2,
\qquad
0+2\left(\frac12\right)+1=2.
$$

The mean-field values satisfy the relation.

### (b) Widom relation

$$
\gamma_{\mathrm{crit}}=\beta_{\mathrm{crit}}(\delta-1),
\qquad
\frac12(3-1)=1=\gamma_{\mathrm{crit}}.
$$

### (c) Fisher relation

$$
\gamma_{\mathrm{crit}}=\nu(2-\eta),
\qquad
\frac12(2-0)=1=\gamma_{\mathrm{crit}}.
$$

### (d) Josephson hyperscaling relation

$$
2-\alpha=d\nu,
\qquad
2-0=d\left(\frac12\right)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad d=4.
$$

Applied to the mean-field exponents, hyperscaling gives the upper critical dimension

$$
d_c^{\mathrm{sup}}=4.
\tag{13.66}
$$

This agrees with the Ginzburg criterion and renormalization-group analysis.

### Physical meaning

The Rushbrooke, Widom, and Fisher identities follow from scaling. They are satisfied by a theory with the corresponding homogeneous scaling form (12.11), independent of spatial dimension.

Hyperscaling is more restrictive. It connects the critical exponents to spatial dimension through the free-energy density. Above $d_c^{\mathrm{sup}}=4$, a dangerously irrelevant coupling, especially the quartic coefficient $\energy{a}_4$, changes the dependence of the singular free energy on relevant fields. Mean-field exponents then fail to satisfy hyperscaling for $d>4$. Below four dimensions, hyperscaling holds for the nontrivial critical fixed point, whose Wilson–Fisher exponents replace mean-field values.

> [!note] Source clarification
> The source calls the Gaussian fixed point exact in four dimensions and states that Landau exponents apply everywhere on the critical manifold. Four is the marginal dimension: the leading power exponents are mean-field values, but logarithmic corrections can remain. Pure mean-field power laws alone do not describe all critical behaviour at that dimension.

### Check against Ising exponents

For the two-dimensional Ising model, Table 12.1 gives $\alpha=0$ and $\nu=1$. Thus $2-\alpha=2=d\nu$. In three dimensions, $\alpha\approx0.11$ and $\nu\approx0.63$, so $2-\alpha\approx1.89=d\nu$.

## Exercise 12.5: Wilson–Fisher fixed point

*For the $n$-component $\phi^4$ theory in $d=4-\epsilon$, use the one-loop flow $du/d\ell=\epsilon u-A_nu^2+O(u^3)$, where $A_n=(n+8)K_d$. (a) Find $u^*$. (b) Calculate $y_u$ and show that $y_u=-\epsilon$. (c) Explain $y_u<0$ for $d<4$.*

### (a) Fixed point

At a nonzero fixed point, $du/d\ell=0$. To the stated order,

$$
\epsilon u^*-A_n(u^*)^2=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
u^*(\epsilon,n)=\frac{\epsilon}{A_n}
=\frac{\epsilon}{(n+8)K_d}.
\tag{13.67}
$$

There is also the Gaussian fixed point $u_G^*=0$. For $\epsilon>0$, or $d<4$, the Wilson–Fisher value is positive and physically acceptable. For $\epsilon<0$, the non-Gaussian root is negative and gives an unstable quartic free energy; the Gaussian fixed point controls the physical critical behaviour. At $\epsilon=0$, the two fixed points merge and the quartic interaction is marginal.

### (b) Linearized flow

Set $u=u^*+\delta u$. Then

$$
\frac{d(\delta u)}{d\ell}
=\left.\frac{d}{du}(\epsilon u-A_nu^2)\right|_{u=u^*}\delta u
=(\epsilon-2A_nu^*)\delta u.
$$

With $u^*=\epsilon/A_n$,

$$
\frac{d(\delta u)}{d\ell}
=(\epsilon-2\epsilon)\delta u
=-\epsilon\,\delta u
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y_u=-\epsilon.
\tag{13.68}
$$

The normalization coefficient cancels through $A_nu^*=\epsilon$, so the eigenvalue is independent of that normalization at this order.

### (c) Meaning of the negative eigenvalue

For $d<4$, $\epsilon=4-d>0$, so $y_u=-\epsilon<0$. The quartic-coupling perturbation is irrelevant at the Wilson–Fisher fixed point:

$$
\delta u\propto b_{\mathrm{RG}}^{y_u}
=b_{\mathrm{RG}}^{-\epsilon}.
$$

Near the fixed point on the critical manifold, coarse-graining reduces a small displacement of $u$ from $u^*$. The fixed point is attractive along this direction. The relevant temperature and field directions have $y_t>0$ and $y_h>0$ and determine the leading critical exponents.

At the Gaussian fixed point, $y_u^{(G)}=+\epsilon>0$ for $d<4$. The flow moves away from that fixed point towards the Wilson–Fisher fixed point. This exchange of stability underlies the $\epsilon$ expansion. Universal quantities are expanded about their Gaussian values, for example

$$
\nu=\frac12+O(\epsilon),
\qquad
\gamma_{\mathrm{crit}}=1+O(\epsilon),
$$

as in (12.12)–(12.13).

### Dimensions

The flow parameter $\ell=\ln b_{\mathrm{RG}}$ is dimensionless. The source defines the dimensionless coupling by division by $K_d\signal{\Lambda}^{4-d}$, where $\signal{\Lambda}$ is the momentum cutoff. Thus

$$
\left[\frac{du}{d\ell}\right]=[u],
\qquad
[\epsilon u]=[u]=[A_nu^2].
$$

The flow equation is dimensionally consistent.

## Exercise 12.6: The three-dimensional XY model

*For the XY model with $n=2$ and $d=3$, (a) use $\epsilon=1$ in (12.12)–(12.13) to estimate $\nu$ and $\gamma_{\mathrm{crit}}$ to first order; (b) compare with $\nu_{\mathrm{num}}\approx0.67$ and $\gamma_{\mathrm{num}}\approx1.32$; and (c) discuss the accuracy of the expansion.*

### (a) First-order estimates

Insert $n=2$ and $\epsilon=1$ into (12.12):

$$
\nu^{(1)}
=\frac12+\frac{n+2}{4(n+8)}\epsilon
=\frac12+\frac4{4(10)}
=\frac12+\frac1{10}
=0.600.
$$

Equation (12.13) gives

$$
\gamma_{\mathrm{crit}}^{(1)}
=1+\frac{n+2}{2(n+8)}\epsilon
=1+\frac4{2(10)}
=1+\frac15
=1.200.
$$

### (b) Comparison with numerical values

| Exponent | First order in $\epsilon$ | Numerical value, 3D XY | Relative error |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\nu$ | 0.600 | 0.67 | approximately 10% |
| $\gamma_{\mathrm{crit}}$ | 1.200 | 1.32 | approximately 9% |

The first-order estimates underestimate both exponents by similar percentages.

> [!note] Source clarification
> The source describes both deviations as slightly below 10%. With its rounded values, the error in $\nu$ is $(0.67-0.60)/0.67\approx10.4\%$, while the error in $\gamma_{\mathrm{crit}}$ is approximately $9.1\%$.

### (c) Accuracy and structure of the expansion

The difference is consistent with the asymptotic rather than convergent character of the $\epsilon$ expansion discussed in Chapter 12.

1. **Order in the expansion:** The first-order calculation omits $O(\epsilon^2)$ terms. For $\epsilon=1$, omitted terms can give appreciable corrections. As an example, the source gives

$$
\nu
=\frac12+\frac{n+2}{4(n+8)}\epsilon
+\frac{(n+2)(n^2+23n+60)}{8(n+8)^3}\epsilon^2+\cdots.
$$

For $n=2$ and $\epsilon=1$, this yields approximately $0.65$, closer to the numerical value $0.67$.

2. **Asymptotics and resummation:** The coefficients of $\sum_k a_k\epsilon^k$ grow factorially, schematically $\lvert a_k\rvert\sim k!\,c^k$. The series has zero radius of convergence, as is typical of perturbative path-integral expansions and their Feynman diagrams. However, it is Borel summable.

Padé–Borel and conformal Borel transformations can resum the asymptotic series to a finite, defined value. The source describes high-order expansions through sixth or seventh order as a basis for accurate estimates of the three-dimensional XY exponent.

3. **Comparison with other methods:** The source lists lattice Monte Carlo simulations, high-temperature expansions, renormalization in the Polchinski formulation, and the conformal bootstrap as independent methods. It quotes

$$
\nu=0.6717(1),
\qquad
\gamma_{\mathrm{crit}}=1.3178(2).
$$

The source attributes these particular values to the conformal bootstrap. The bootstrap uses conformal symmetry at criticality and unitarity constraints to restrict critical data.

> [!note] Source attribution
> These numerical values and their attribution are retained from the supplied notes. No bibliographic reference is supplied for the quoted bootstrap result.

4. **Universality:** The three-dimensional XY universality class describes the lambda transition of superfluid helium-4. The source describes the Lipa et al. microgravity experiment, flown on the Space Shuttle in 1992 with final analysis in 2003. Microgravity reduces transition broadening from hydrostatic pressure. Its quoted heat-capacity exponent is

$$
\alpha=-0.0127(3).
$$

Hyperscaling in $d=3$ then gives

$$
\nu=\frac{2-\alpha}{3}=0.6709(1).
$$

The source presents the close agreement with theoretical estimates as a strong experimental test of renormalization-group universality.

5. **Meaning of the slow convergence:** Even first order captures the qualitative structure. The mean-field values $\nu_{\mathrm{MF}}=0.5$ and $\gamma_{\mathrm{MF}}=1$ are roughly 25% below the three-dimensional values. The $\epsilon$ expansion moves both exponents towards the physical result through a calculation governed by component count, dimension, and symmetry. It shows how strongly critical behaviour depends on universal properties and how weakly it depends on microscopic details.

This is the central lesson of Wilson's renormalization-group approach; the source notes his 1982 Nobel Prize.

The first-order expansion is a useful qualitative tool and gives roughly 10% accuracy for these three-dimensional estimates. With higher orders and suitable resummation, the method can provide precision results comparable with other critical-phenomena methods.
