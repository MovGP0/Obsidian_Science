## Exercise 8.1: Ideal-gas condensation estimate for helium-4

**Task.** Calculate the BEC temperature for helium-4 with mass $4.003\,\unit{u}$ and density $n=2.18\times10^{28}\,\unit{m^{-3}}$. Compare it with the observed lambda-transition temperature $\thermal{T}_\lambda=2.17\,\unit{K}$.

**Solution.** Equation (8.4) gives the three-dimensional ideal-gas critical temperature,

$$
\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}
=\frac{2\pi\signal{\hbar}^2}{\mechanical{m}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}
\left(\frac{n}{\zeta(3/2)}\right)^{2/3}.
$$

The factor $\signal{\hbar}^2/(\mechanical{m}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B})$ has units $\unit{m^2\,K}$, while the density factor has units $\unit{m^{-2}}$, leaving temperature units.

### Numerical calculation

$$
\mechanical{m}
=4.003(1.6605\times10^{-27})\,\unit{kg}
\approx6.646\times10^{-27}\,\unit{kg},
\qquad \zeta(3/2)\approx2.612.
$$

The quantum scale is

$$
\frac{2\pi\signal{\hbar}^2}{\mechanical{m}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}
\approx
\frac{2\pi(1.055\times10^{-34})^2}
{(6.646\times10^{-27})(1.381\times10^{-23})}
\,\unit{m^2\,K}
\approx7.62\times10^{-19}\,\unit{m^2\,K}.
$$

The density factor is

$$
\left(\frac n{\zeta(3/2)}\right)^{2/3}
=\left(\frac{2.18\times10^{28}}{2.612}\right)^{2/3}\unit{m^{-2}}
\approx4.10\times10^{18}\,\unit{m^{-2}}.
$$

Thus $\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}\approx3.13\,\unit{K}$.

### Comparison with the lambda transition

The ideal-gas estimate is about 44% above the observed superfluid transition at $2.17\,\unit{K}$. Liquid helium is not an ideal gas. The source describes a Lennard–Jones-like interaction with strong short-range repulsion and a quoted core length of about $2.6\,\unit{Å}$.

The source attributes the lower transition temperature to interaction-induced changes in the low-energy states and discusses excluded-volume and Bogoliubov descriptions as qualitative models. It also emphasizes the much smaller condensate fraction: neutron measurements and quantum Monte Carlo calculations give $N_0/N\sim0.07$–$0.10$ near zero temperature, rather than the ideal-gas value of one.

The estimate has the correct order of magnitude, while liquid helium is a strongly correlated realization of Bose condensation.

> [!note] Scope of the interaction discussion
> The source calls the quoted core length a radius and gives a qualitative explanation of the temperature shift. The ideal-gas formula is not a quantitative theory of dense liquid helium, and the core-length description should not be taken as a precise hard-sphere model.

## Exercise 8.2: Entropy below condensation

**Task.** For $\thermal{T}<\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}$, show

$$
\thermal{S}
=\frac52\frac{\mechanical{V}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}{\signal{\lambda}^3}
\zeta(5/2),
$$

and verify the third law.

**Solution.** In the condensed phase $z=1$, so chemical potential is zero. Equation (8.6) gives

$$
\energy{U}
=\frac32\frac{\mechanical{V}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}
{\signal{\lambda}^3}\zeta(5/2),
\qquad
\mechanical{P}
=\frac{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}{\signal{\lambda}^3}\zeta(5/2),
$$

where $\signal{\lambda}=\signal{h}/\sqrt{2\pi\mechanical{m}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}$, so its inverse cube scales as $\thermal{T}^{3/2}$. The excited population is

$$
N_{\mathrm{ec}}(\thermal{T})
=\frac{\mechanical{V}}{\signal{\lambda}^3}\zeta(3/2).
$$

The condensate population is $N_0=N-N_{\mathrm{ec}}$. In the ideal model, these particles occupy one zero-energy state and do not contribute to energy, pressure, or entropy.

### Euler-relation method

At zero chemical potential, $\thermal{T}\thermal{S}=\energy{U}+\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}$, so

$$
\thermal{S}=\frac{\energy{U}+\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}}{\thermal{T}}.
$$

Since $\energy{U}=(3/2)\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}$,

$$
\thermal{S}(\thermal{T}<\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c})
=\frac52\frac{\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}}{\thermal{T}}
=\frac52\frac{\mechanical{V}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}
{\signal{\lambda}^3}\zeta(5/2).
\tag{13.20}
$$

### Verification by integrating heat capacity

Equation (8.8) gives

$$
\thermal{C}_V^{(<)}
=\frac{15}{4}N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}
\frac{\zeta(5/2)}{\zeta(3/2)}
\left(\frac{\thermal{T}}{\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}}\right)^{3/2}.
$$

Then

$$
\thermal{S}
=\int_0^{\thermal{T}}
\frac{\thermal{C}_V^{(<)}(\thermal{T}')}{\thermal{T}'}\,d\thermal{T}'
=\frac52N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}
\frac{\zeta(5/2)}{\zeta(3/2)}
\left(\frac{\thermal{T}}{\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}}\right)^{3/2}.
$$

Using $N=\mechanical{V}\zeta(3/2)/\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm c}^3$ and
$\signal{\lambda}^3=\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm c}^3 (\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}/\thermal{T})^{3/2}$ recovers Equation (13.20).

The first derivation uses a thermodynamic identity; the second integrates the calculated heat capacity. Both give the same result.

**Units.** The quantity $\mechanical{V}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}/\signal{\lambda}^3$ has entropy units, $\unit{J\,K^{-1}}$.

**Zero-temperature limit.** The thermal wavelength diverges as $\thermal{T}^{-1/2}$, so entropy vanishes as $\thermal{T}^{3/2}$, consistent with the third law.

At the transition from below,

$$
\thermal{S}(\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c})
=\frac52N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}
\frac{\zeta(5/2)}{\zeta(3/2)}
\approx1.284\,N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}.
$$

Entropy is continuous through the transition, with no latent heat.

**Physical interpretation.** Only excited particles carry entropy. Their entropy per particle is

$$
\frac{\thermal{S}}{N_{\mathrm{ec}}}
=\frac52\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}
\frac{\zeta(5/2)}{\zeta(3/2)}
\approx1.284\,\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B},
$$

independent of temperature below the transition. In the ideal fixed-particle-count description, the macroscopically occupied single condensate state has zero configurational entropy.

## Exercise 8.3: Wien displacement and Planck-law limits

**Task.** From the Planck spectrum, derive:

1. Wien's wavelength displacement law, $\signal{\lambda}_{\max}\thermal{T}=b$.
2. The Rayleigh–Jeans limit at low frequency.
3. The Wien limit at high frequency.

**Solution.** Equation (8.9) is

$$
\energy{u}(\signal{\nu},\thermal{T})
=\frac{8\pi\signal{h}}{\mechanical{c}^3}
\frac{\signal{\nu}^3}
{e^{\signal{h}\signal{\nu}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})}-1}.
$$

Change variables to radiation wavelength, $\signal{\lambda}=\mechanical{c}/\signal{\nu}$. Conservation of energy in corresponding intervals gives

$$
\energy{u}(\signal{\nu},\thermal{T})\,d\signal{\nu}
=-\energy{u}(\signal{\lambda},\thermal{T})\,d\signal{\lambda},
\qquad
\frac{d\signal{\nu}}{d\signal{\lambda}}
=-\frac{\mechanical{c}}{\signal{\lambda}^2}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\energy{u}(\signal{\lambda},\thermal{T})
=\frac{8\pi\signal{h}\mechanical{c}}{\signal{\lambda}^5}
\frac1{e^{\signal{h}\mechanical{c}/
(\signal{\lambda}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})}-1}.
\tag{13.21}
$$

### (a) Wien displacement law

At fixed temperature, set $x=\signal{h}\mechanical{c}/(\signal{\lambda}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})$. Then

$$
\energy{u}(\signal{\lambda},\thermal{T})
=\frac{8\pi(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})^5}
{\signal{h}^4\mechanical{c}^4}\frac{x^5}{e^x-1}.
$$

Maximizing the wavelength spectrum is equivalent to maximizing $f(x)=x^5/(e^x-1)$. Its stationary condition is

$$
5(1-e^{-x})=x.
\tag{13.22}
$$

The positive root, obtained numerically, is $x_{\max}\approx4.965$. Thus

$$
\begin{aligned}
\signal{\lambda}_{\max}\thermal{T}
&=\frac{\signal{h}\mechanical{c}}{x_{\max}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}\\
&\approx
\frac{(6.626\times10^{-34})(2.998\times10^8)}
{(4.965)(1.381\times10^{-23})}\,\unit{m\,K}\\
&\approx2.898\times10^{-3}\,\unit{m\,K}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the Wien constant $b$. The frequency spectrum instead peaks at $x'_{\max}\approx2.821$. The maxima do not transform into each other by simple inversion: spectral densities include the nonconstant interval Jacobian.

### (b) Rayleigh–Jeans limit

For $\signal{h}\signal{\nu}\ll\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\energy{u}(\signal{\nu},\thermal{T})
&\approx\frac{8\pi\signal{h}}{\mechanical{c}^3}
\frac{\signal{\nu}^3}
{\signal{h}\signal{\nu}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})}\\
&=\frac{8\pi\signal{\nu}^2}{\mechanical{c}^3}
\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Classically each electromagnetic mode has mean energy $\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}$: half electric and half magnetic. Both polarizations are included in the mode density $8\pi\signal{\nu}^2/\mechanical{c}^3$.

Extending this classical formula to arbitrarily high frequency gives the **ultraviolet catastrophe**. Planck's quantized mode energies remove the divergence.

### (c) Wien limit

At high frequency the exponential dominates the denominator:

$$
\energy{u}(\signal{\nu},\thermal{T})
\sim\frac{8\pi\signal{h}\signal{\nu}^3}{\mechanical{c}^3}
e^{-\signal{h}\signal{\nu}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})}.
$$

The Wien approximation predates Planck's law (1896) and fits the high-frequency spectrum. Planck's formula also recovers the correct low-frequency limit.

> [!note] Source correction: low-frequency Wien behaviour
> The source says the Wien approximation becomes exponentially small at low frequency. Its exponential tends to one there, so the approximation instead scales as the cube of frequency, rather than the Rayleigh–Jeans square.

**Units.** The frequency spectral density has units $\unit{J\,s\,m^{-3}}$. In the classical limit the factor of Planck constant cancels, leaving thermal energy as expected.

## Exercise 8.4: Accuracy of the Debye T³ law

**Task.** For a crystal with $\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm D}=300\,\unit{K}$, estimate the temperature below which the cubic Debye heat-capacity law is accurate within 5%.

**Solution.** The cubic law follows from Equation (8.11) by sending the upper integration limit to infinity. Separate the finite-cutoff correction:

$$
\thermal{C}_V=
9N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}
\left(\frac{\thermal{T}}{\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm D}}\right)^3
\left[
\int_0^\infty\frac{x^4e^x}{(e^x-1)^2}\,dx
-\int_{\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm D}/\thermal{T}}^\infty
\frac{x^4e^x}{(e^x-1)^2}\,dx
\right].
$$

Integration by parts gives

$$
\int_0^\infty\frac{x^4e^x}{(e^x-1)^2}\,dx
=4\int_0^\infty\frac{x^3}{e^x-1}\,dx
=4\Gamma(4)\zeta(4)=\frac{4\pi^4}{15}.
$$

Hence

$$
\thermal{C}_V^{(0)}
=\frac{12\pi^4}{5}N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}
\left(\frac{\thermal{T}}{\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm D}}\right)^3.
$$

For $y=\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm D}/\thermal{T}\gg1$, the omitted tail has the asymptotic estimate

$$
\int_y^\infty\frac{x^4e^x}{(e^x-1)^2}\,dx
\approx\int_y^\infty x^4e^{-x}\,dx
\sim y^4e^{-y}\left(1+\frac4y+\cdots\right).
$$

The relative correction to the cubic asymptote is

$$
\frac{\thermal{C}_V^{(0)}-\thermal{C}_V}
{\thermal{C}_V^{(0)}}
=\frac{15}{4\pi^4}y^4e^{-y}[1+O(1/y)]
\equiv\delta(y).
\tag{13.23}
$$

Require $\delta(y)\le0.05$. The source gives these rounded values from the full integral:

| Cutoff ratio $y=\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm D}/\thermal{T}$ | Relative correction |
| --- | --- |
| $9$ | $\sim5.1\%$ |
| $10$ | $\sim2.7\%$ |
| $15$ | $\sim0.08\%$ |
| $20$ | $\sim0.002\%$ |

The 5% threshold is near $y\approx9$, giving

$$
\thermal{T}\lesssim\frac{\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm D}}9
\approx33\,\unit{K}
\qquad(\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm D}=300\,\unit{K}).
$$

The common rule of thumb $\thermal{T}\lesssim\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm D}/10$ is conservative for this threshold. The stricter condition $\thermal{T}<\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm D}/20$ gives much better than 5% accuracy and is suitable for precision work.

**Limits and meaning.** At zero temperature the omitted tail vanishes exponentially and the cubic law becomes asymptotically exact. As temperature approaches the Debye temperature, the correction is no longer small. Use the full Debye function, which tends to the Dulong–Petit heat capacity $3N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}$ at high temperature.

## Exercise 8.5: Photon gas at 3000 K

**Task.** For a photon gas at $\thermal{T}=3000\,\unit{K}$ in volume $\mechanical{V}=1\,\unit{L}$, calculate total energy, radiation pressure, and mean photon count.

**Solution.** Equilibrium with the isothermal cavity gives zero chemical potential and unit fugacity. All quantities follow from Bose integrals at $z=1$.

### (a) Total radiation energy

Equation (8.10) gives $\energy{u}_{\mathrm{tot}}=a_R\thermal{T}^4$, with

$$
a_R=\frac{\pi^2\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}^4}
{15\signal{\hbar}^3\mechanical{c}^3}
\approx7.566\times10^{-16}\,\unit{J\,m^{-3}\,K^{-4}}.
$$

For $\mechanical{V}=10^{-3}\,\unit{m^3}$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\energy{U}
&=a_R\thermal{T}^4\mechanical{V}\\
&\approx(7.566\times10^{-16})(3000)^4(10^{-3})\,\unit{J}\\
&=(7.566\times10^{-16})(8.1\times10^{13})(10^{-3})\,\unit{J}\\
&\approx6.13\times10^{-5}\,\unit{J}
\approx61\,\unit{\mu J}.
\end{aligned}
$$

### (b) Radiation pressure

For ultrarelativistic particles,

$$
\mechanical{P}_{\mathrm{rad}}
=\frac{\energy{U}}{3\mechanical{V}}
=\frac{a_R\thermal{T}^4}{3}
\approx2.04\times10^{-2}\,\unit{Pa}.
$$

This is about $0.020\,\unit{Pa}$, or $2\times10^{-7}\,\unit{atm}$: negligible for many laboratory applications, but radiation pressure becomes important at stellar temperatures.

> [!note] Source correction: temperature scaling
> The source quotes a pressure increase of $10^{14}$ at $10^6\,\unit{K}$. Relative to the stated $3000\,\unit{K}$, the fourth-power law gives $(10^6/3000)^4\approx1.23\times10^{10}$.

### (c) Mean photon count

Use $g(\signal{\omega})=\mechanical{V}\signal{\omega}^2/(\pi^2\mechanical{c}^3)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle N_\gamma\rangle
&=\int_0^\infty
\frac{\mechanical{V}\signal{\omega}^2/(\pi^2\mechanical{c}^3)}
{e^{\signal{\hbar}\signal{\omega}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})}-1}
\,d\signal{\omega}\\
&=\frac{\mechanical{V}}{\pi^2}
\left(\frac{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}{\signal{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}\right)^3
\int_0^\infty\frac{x^2\,dx}{e^x-1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Since the integral is $\Gamma(3)\zeta(3)=2\zeta(3)$,

$$
\langle N_\gamma\rangle
=\frac{2\zeta(3)}{\pi^2}\mechanical{V}
\left(\frac{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}{\signal{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}\right)^3.
\tag{13.24}
$$

Numerically,

$$
\frac{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}{\signal{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}
\approx
\frac{(1.381\times10^{-23})(3000)}
{(1.055\times10^{-34})(2.998\times10^8)}\,\unit{m^{-1}}
\approx1.31\times10^6\,\unit{m^{-1}}.
$$

Its cube is about $2.25\times10^{18}\,\unit{m^{-3}}$. With $\zeta(3)\approx1.202$,

$$
\langle N_\gamma\rangle
\approx\frac{2(1.202)}{\pi^2}(10^{-3})(2.25\times10^{18})
\approx5.5\times10^{14}.
$$

The mean photon energy is

$$
\langle\energy{\varepsilon}\rangle_\gamma
=\frac{\energy{U}}{\langle N_\gamma\rangle}
\approx1.1\times10^{-19}\,\unit{J}
\approx0.7\,\unit{eV}.
$$

Exactly,

$$
\langle\energy{\varepsilon}\rangle_\gamma
=\frac{\pi^4}{30\zeta(3)}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
\approx2.701\,\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}.
$$

**Checks.** Energy density times volume gives energy. The ratio of thermal energy to $\signal{\hbar}\mechanical{c}$ is an inverse length, so volume times its cube is a dimensionless particle count.

## Exercise 8.6: Bose condensation in two dimensions

**Task.** For a free two-dimensional Bose gas, show that $g_1(z)=-\ln(1-z)$ diverges as fugacity tends to one and that no finite-temperature BEC occurs in the thermodynamic limit. Discuss a two-dimensional harmonic trap.

### Two-dimensional density of states

For quadratic dispersion $\energy{\varepsilon}=\signal{\hbar}^2\signal{k}^2/(2\mechanical{m})$, integration over circular momentum shells gives

$$
g_{2\mathrm D}(\energy{\varepsilon})
=\frac{\mechanical{A}\mechanical{m}}{2\pi\signal{\hbar}^2}.
$$

Unlike the three-dimensional case, this density of states is independent of energy.

### Integrated particle density

$$
\begin{aligned}
N_{\mathrm{ec}}
&=\int_0^\infty
\frac{g_{2\mathrm D}(\energy{\varepsilon})\,d\energy{\varepsilon}}
{z^{-1}e^{\thermal{\beta}\energy{\varepsilon}}-1}\\
&=\frac{\mechanical{A}\mechanical{m}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}
{2\pi\signal{\hbar}^2}
\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{z^{-1}e^x-1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The integral is $g_1(z)=-\ln(1-z)$, since $\Gamma(1)=1$ and $\sum_{\ell\ge1}z^\ell/\ell=-\ln(1-z)$. Define $\signal{\Lambda}^2=2\pi\signal{\hbar}^2/(\mechanical{m}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})$:

$$
n_{2\mathrm D}\signal{\Lambda}^2=-\ln(1-z).
\tag{13.25}
$$

### No finite-temperature ideal-gas condensation

The right-hand side grows without bound as $z\to1^-$. For any finite positive temperature and areal density, Equation (13.25) has a solution with $0<z<1$:

$$
z=1-e^{-n_{2\mathrm D}\signal{\Lambda}^2}.
$$

The excited states never saturate at a finite particle density. Thus the homogeneous ideal Bose gas has no macroscopic ground-state condensate at positive temperature in the two-dimensional thermodynamic limit.

### Mermin–Wagner–Hohenberg context

In low-dimensional systems, thermal phase fluctuations prevent the usual spontaneous breaking of a continuous symmetry at positive temperature. The associated $U(1)$ phase symmetry cannot support true long-range condensate order in the homogeneous two-dimensional thermodynamic limit.

The source relates this to the **Berezinskii–Kosterlitz–Thouless (BKT) transition**, where vortices unbind and algebraic correlations replace true condensate order.

> [!note] Interactions in the BKT statement
> The BKT discussion concerns interacting two-dimensional fluids. The homogeneous ideal gas just calculated has neither finite-temperature BEC nor the interacting-fluid BKT transition.

### Limits

- For $n_{2\mathrm D}\signal{\Lambda}^2\ll1$, $z\approx n_{2\mathrm D}\signal{\Lambda}^2$, the classical two-dimensional Maxwell–Boltzmann result.
- For $n_{2\mathrm D}\signal{\Lambda}^2\gg1$, $1-z\approx e^{-n_{2\mathrm D}\signal{\Lambda}^2}$. Fugacity is extremely close to one but never reaches it at finite temperature and density. The condensate fraction tends to zero in the thermodynamic limit.

### Two-dimensional harmonic trap

For

$$
\energy{V}_{\mathrm{trap}}(\mechanical{r})
=\frac12\mechanical{m}\signal{\omega}_{\mathrm{trap}}^2\mechanical{r}^2,
$$

the one-particle spectrum is

$$
\energy{\varepsilon}_{n_x,n_y}
=\signal{\hbar}\signal{\omega}_{\mathrm{trap}}(n_x+n_y+1).
$$

The level with $n=n_x+n_y$ has degeneracy $n+1$. In the continuum regime,

$$
g_{\mathrm{trap}}(\energy{\varepsilon})
\approx\frac{\energy{\varepsilon}}
{(\signal{\hbar}\signal{\omega}_{\mathrm{trap}})^2}
\qquad(\energy{\varepsilon}\gg\signal{\hbar}\signal{\omega}_{\mathrm{trap}}).
$$

This linear growth makes the excited-state count

$$
N_{\mathrm{ec}}
=\int_0^\infty
\frac{g_{\mathrm{trap}}(\energy{\varepsilon})\,d\energy{\varepsilon}}
{z^{-1}e^{\thermal{\beta}\energy{\varepsilon}}-1}
=\left(\frac{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}
{\signal{\hbar}\signal{\omega}_{\mathrm{trap}}}\right)^2g_2(z).
$$

> [!note] Trap energy zero
> In this occupation integral, excitation energy and chemical potential are measured relative to the trap ground state. The zero-point energy in the preceding spectrum is absorbed into that reference.

At unit fugacity, $g_2(1)=\zeta(2)=\pi^2/6$ is finite. The trap therefore has a finite saturation temperature:

$$
N=\left(\frac{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}}
{\signal{\hbar}\signal{\omega}_{\mathrm{trap}}}\right)^2\zeta(2)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}
=\signal{\hbar}\signal{\omega}_{\mathrm{trap}}
\sqrt{\frac{6N}{\pi^2}}.
\tag{13.26}
$$

**Physical meaning.** The difference comes from the low-energy state density. In the uniform two-dimensional gas it is constant, allowing the excited states to accommodate the density at every positive temperature. Harmonic confinement makes the low-energy state density vanish linearly, allowing saturation and macroscopic ground-state occupation in the ideal-trap description.

The source cites ultracold two-dimensional gas work by Hadzibabic, Dalibard, and collaborators (2006 and later) in discussing confinement, coherence, and BKT behaviour. The uniform-system theorem and the trapped ideal-gas calculation have different spatial and limiting assumptions.
