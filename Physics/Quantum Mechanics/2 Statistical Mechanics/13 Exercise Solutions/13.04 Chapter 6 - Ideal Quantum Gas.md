## Exercise 6.1: Thermodynamic relation for an ideal quantum gas

**Task.** Consider a three-dimensional ideal quantum gas, with either Fermi–Dirac or Bose–Einstein statistics.

Show that pressure and total internal energy obey $\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}=(2/3)\energy{U}$.

**Solution.** Equation (6.9), obtained from $\energy{\Phi}=-\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}\ln\Xi=-\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}$, gives

$$
\mechanical{P}
=\frac{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}{\mechanical{V}}
\int_0^\infty g(\energy{\varepsilon})
\ln\Xi_\alpha(\energy{\varepsilon})\,d\energy{\varepsilon}.
$$

For fermions, $\ln\Xi_\alpha=\ln[1+e^{-\thermal{\beta}(\energy{\varepsilon}-\energy{\mu})}]$; for bosons, it is $-\ln[1-e^{-\thermal{\beta}(\energy{\varepsilon}-\energy{\mu})}]$. In three dimensions, Equation (6.7) gives $g(\energy{\varepsilon})=A_{3\mathrm D}\energy{\varepsilon}^{1/2}$.

### Integration by parts

Use the unified form

$$
\ln\Xi_\alpha
=\mp\ln[1\mp e^{-\thermal{\beta}(\energy{\varepsilon}-\energy{\mu})}],
$$

with the upper sign for bosons and lower sign for fermions. It vanishes as energy tends to infinity. Choosing $(2/3)\energy{\varepsilon}^{3/2}$ as the antiderivative of $\energy{\varepsilon}^{1/2}$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}
&=\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
\int_0^\infty A_{3\mathrm D}\energy{\varepsilon}^{1/2}
\ln\Xi_\alpha\,d\energy{\varepsilon}\\
&=\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}A_{3\mathrm D}
\left[\frac23\energy{\varepsilon}^{3/2}\ln\Xi_\alpha\right]_0^\infty\\
&\quad-\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}A_{3\mathrm D}
\int_0^\infty\frac23\energy{\varepsilon}^{3/2}
\frac{d\ln\Xi_\alpha}{d\energy{\varepsilon}}\,d\energy{\varepsilon}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The boundary term vanishes at zero energy and at infinity. The derivative is

$$
\frac{d\ln\Xi_\alpha}{d\energy{\varepsilon}}
=-\frac{\thermal{\beta}e^{-\thermal{\beta}(\energy{\varepsilon}-\energy{\mu})}}
{1\mp e^{-\thermal{\beta}(\energy{\varepsilon}-\energy{\mu})}}
=-\frac{\thermal{\beta}}
{e^{\thermal{\beta}(\energy{\varepsilon}-\energy{\mu})}\mp1}
=-\thermal{\beta}\bar n(\energy{\varepsilon}).
$$

Since $\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}\thermal{\beta}=1$,

$$
\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}
=\frac23\int_0^\infty A_{3\mathrm D}\energy{\varepsilon}^{3/2}
\bar n(\energy{\varepsilon})\,d\energy{\varepsilon}
=\frac23\int_0^\infty\energy{\varepsilon}g(\energy{\varepsilon})
\bar n(\energy{\varepsilon})\,d\energy{\varepsilon}.
\tag{13.15}
$$

The integral is the total internal energy:

$$
\energy{U}
=\int_0^\infty\energy{\varepsilon}\,
g(\energy{\varepsilon})\bar n(\energy{\varepsilon})\,d\energy{\varepsilon}.
$$

The factor $g\bar n\,d\energy{\varepsilon}$ counts particles in the energy interval. Therefore

$$
\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}=\frac23\energy{U}.
$$

### Checks and interpretation

**Units.** The integrated particle count is dimensionless. Multiplying by energy gives the same units as pressure times volume.

**Classical limit.** With $\bar n\approx z e^{-\thermal{\beta}\energy{\varepsilon}}$, Gaussian integrals give $\energy{U}=3N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}/2$ and $\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}=N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}$, satisfying the relation.

> [!note] Classical occupation
> The source suppresses the fugacity in the Maxwell–Boltzmann occupation. In a general grand-canonical gas the factor $z$ is required.

**Origin.** The result follows from the nonrelativistic dispersion $\energy{\varepsilon}=\mechanical{\mathbf p}^2/(2\mechanical{m})$, giving a square-root density of states in three dimensions. It is independent of Bose, Fermi, or Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics. In d dimensions with quadratic dispersion,

$$
\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}=\frac2d\energy{U}.
$$

For ultrarelativistic particles, $\energy{\varepsilon}=\signal{\hbar}\mechanical{c}\signal{k}$ and $g(\energy{\varepsilon})\propto\energy{\varepsilon}^2$. The same method gives $\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}=\energy{U}/3$, as for radiation.

## Exercise 6.2: Zero-temperature Fermi-gas energy

**Task.** Calculate

$$
\energy{U}_0=\int_0^{\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}}
\energy{\varepsilon}g(\energy{\varepsilon})\,d\energy{\varepsilon},
$$

and show $\energy{U}_0=(3/5)N\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}$.

**Solution.** At zero temperature the Fermi–Dirac distribution is the step function $\bar n_{\mathrm{FD}}=\Theta(\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}-\energy{\varepsilon})$. States below the Fermi energy are occupied, and states above it are empty. Equation (6.7) gives

$$
g(\energy{\varepsilon})=A_{3\mathrm D}\energy{\varepsilon}^{1/2},
\qquad
A_{3\mathrm D}=
\frac{g_s\mechanical{V}(2\mechanical{m})^{3/2}}
{4\pi^2\signal{\hbar}^3}.
$$

### Particle count

At zero temperature,

$$
N=\int_0^{\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}}
g(\energy{\varepsilon})\,d\energy{\varepsilon}
=\frac23A_{3\mathrm D}\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}^{3/2}.
$$

Thus $A_{3\mathrm D}=3N/(2\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}^{3/2})$. Inverting gives Equation (6.10):

$$
\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}
=\frac{\signal{\hbar}^2}{2\mechanical{m}}
\left(\frac{6\pi^2n}{g_s}\right)^{2/3}.
$$

### Internal energy at zero temperature

$$
\energy{U}_0
=A_{3\mathrm D}\int_0^{\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}}
\energy{\varepsilon}^{3/2}\,d\energy{\varepsilon}
=\frac25A_{3\mathrm D}\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}^{5/2}.
$$

Since $A_{3\mathrm D}\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}^{3/2}=3N/2$,

$$
\energy{U}_0=\frac25\frac32N\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}
=\frac35N\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}.
$$

### Checks

- **Units:** $N\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}$ has energy units.
- **Energy per particle:** $\energy{U}_0/N=3\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}/5$. Occupied states span zero to the Fermi energy, with density growing as the square root of energy.
- **Degeneracy pressure:** Equation (13.15) gives

$$
\mechanical{P}_0\mechanical{V}
=\frac23\energy{U}_0=\frac25N\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F},
\qquad
\mechanical{P}_0=\frac25n\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}.
$$

- **Classical comparison:** the classical prediction $\energy{U}_{\mathrm{cl}}=(3/2)N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}$ vanishes at zero temperature. The Fermi gas retains finite energy because Pauli exclusion prevents all particles from occupying the same ground state.

## Exercise 6.3: Grand partition function and pressure

**Task.** Express $\ln\Xi$ for ideal Bose and Fermi gases as a density-of-states integral and derive pressure as a function of temperature, volume, and chemical potential.

**Solution.** The grand partition function factorizes, $\Xi=\prod_\alpha\Xi_\alpha$. With the upper sign for bosons and lower sign for fermions,

$$
\ln\Xi=\sum_\alpha\ln\Xi_\alpha
=\mp\sum_\alpha
\ln[1\mp e^{-\thermal{\beta}(\energy{\varepsilon}_\alpha-\energy{\mu})}].
$$

The continuum limit (6.8) gives

$$
\ln\Xi
=\mp\int_0^\infty g(\energy{\varepsilon})
\ln[1\mp e^{-\thermal{\beta}(\energy{\varepsilon}-\energy{\mu})}]
\,d\energy{\varepsilon}.
\tag{13.16}
$$

### Three-dimensional integration by parts

With $g(\energy{\varepsilon})=A_{3\mathrm D}\energy{\varepsilon}^{1/2}$ and vanishing boundary terms,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ln\Xi
&=\mp A_{3\mathrm D}\int_0^\infty
\energy{\varepsilon}^{1/2}
\ln[1\mp e^{-\thermal{\beta}(\energy{\varepsilon}-\energy{\mu})}]
\,d\energy{\varepsilon}\\
&=\frac23A_{3\mathrm D}\thermal{\beta}
\int_0^\infty
\frac{\energy{\varepsilon}^{3/2}\,d\energy{\varepsilon}}
{e^{\thermal{\beta}(\energy{\varepsilon}-\energy{\mu})}\mp1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Set $x=\thermal{\beta}\energy{\varepsilon}$ and $z=e^{\thermal{\beta}\energy{\mu}}$:

$$
\ln\Xi=\frac23A_{3\mathrm D}\thermal{\beta}
(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})^{5/2}
\int_0^\infty\frac{x^{3/2}\,dx}{z^{-1}e^x\mp1}.
$$

The Bose and Fermi integrals satisfy

$$
\int_0^\infty\frac{x^{3/2}\,dx}{z^{-1}e^x\mp1}
=\Gamma(5/2)
\begin{cases}
g_{5/2}(z),&\text{Bose},\\
f_{5/2}(z),&\text{Fermi}.
\end{cases}
$$

Use $\Gamma(5/2)=3\sqrt\pi/4$ and

$$
\frac23\Gamma(5/2)A_{3\mathrm D}
(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})^{3/2}
=\frac{g_s\mechanical{V}}{\signal{\lambda}_T^3}.
$$

With $\signal{\lambda}_T=\signal{h}/\sqrt{2\pi\mechanical{m}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}$, the result is

$$
\ln\Xi=\frac{g_s\mechanical{V}}{\signal{\lambda}_T^3}
\begin{cases}
g_{5/2}(z),&\text{Bose},\\
f_{5/2}(z),&\text{Fermi}.
\end{cases}
\tag{13.17}
$$

### Pressure

From $\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}=\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}\ln\Xi$,

$$
\frac{\mechanical{P}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}
=\frac{g_s}{\signal{\lambda}_T^3}
\begin{cases}
g_{5/2}(z),&\text{Bose},\\
f_{5/2}(z),&\text{Fermi}.
\end{cases}
$$

This agrees with Section 6.11.

### Checks

**Units.** The thermal wavelength has length units, while $g_s\mechanical{V}/\signal{\lambda}_T^3$ and the Bose/Fermi integrals are dimensionless. Thus $\ln\Xi$ is dimensionless.

**Classical limit.** For $z\to0$, both $f_n(z)$ and $g_n(z)$ tend to $z$. Then

$$
\frac{\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}
\to\frac{g_s\mechanical{V}}{\signal{\lambda}_T^3}z=N,
$$

recovering the ideal-gas law.

**Degenerate limits.** For bosons, $z\to1^-$ and $\energy{\mu}\to0^-$; $g_{5/2}(z)\to\zeta(5/2)$, so pressure stays finite below the condensation temperature. For fermions at large fugacity,

$$
f_{5/2}(z)\sim\frac{(\ln z)^{5/2}}{\Gamma(7/2)},
$$

giving $\mechanical{P}\to(2/5)n\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}$.

## Exercise 6.4: Low-temperature Fermi heat capacity

**Task.** For a degenerate Fermi gas with $\thermal{T}\ll\thermal{T}_{\mathrm F}$, use the Sommerfeld expansion to derive its heat capacity.

At fixed volume, show

$$
\thermal{C}_V\approx\frac{\pi^2}{2}
N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\frac{\thermal{T}}{\thermal{T}_{\mathrm F}},
$$

rather than the classical constant $3N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}/2$.

**Solution.** Start with

$$
\energy{U}(\thermal{T})
=\int_0^\infty\energy{\varepsilon}\,
g(\energy{\varepsilon})\bar n_{\mathrm{FD}}(\energy{\varepsilon})
\,d\energy{\varepsilon},
$$

where $g=A_{3\mathrm D}\energy{\varepsilon}^{1/2}$ and
$\bar n_{\mathrm{FD}}=[e^{\thermal{\beta}(\energy{\varepsilon}-\energy{\mu})}+1]^{-1}$.

### Sommerfeld lemma

For a regular test function $\phi$ with polynomial growth, at thermal energy much smaller than chemical potential,

$$
\int_0^\infty\phi(\energy{\varepsilon})
\bar n_{\mathrm{FD}}(\energy{\varepsilon})\,d\energy{\varepsilon}
=\int_0^{\energy{\mu}}\phi(\energy{\varepsilon})\,d\energy{\varepsilon}
+\frac{\pi^2}{6}(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})^2
\phi'(\energy{\mu})+O(\thermal{T}^4).
\tag{13.18}
$$

This follows by integration by parts and the symmetry of $-\partial\bar n_{\mathrm{FD}}/\partial\energy{\varepsilon}$ about the chemical potential.

### Chemical potential at fixed particle count

Apply the lemma to $\phi=g$:

$$
N=A_{3\mathrm D}\left[
\frac23\energy{\mu}^{3/2}
+\frac{\pi^2}{12}(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})^2
\energy{\mu}^{-1/2}+\cdots
\right].
$$

At zero temperature, $N=(2/3)A_{3\mathrm D}\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}^{3/2}$. Keep N fixed, write $\energy{\mu}=\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}+\delta\energy{\mu}$ with a quadratic temperature shift, and expand:

$$
\energy{\mu}(\thermal{T})
=\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}
\left[
1-\frac{\pi^2}{12}
\left(\frac{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}{\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}}\right)^2
+O((\thermal{T}/\thermal{T}_{\mathrm F})^4)
\right].
\tag{13.19}
$$

### Internal energy at low temperature

With $\phi=\energy{\varepsilon}g=A_{3\mathrm D}\energy{\varepsilon}^{3/2}$,

$$
\energy{U}=A_{3\mathrm D}\left[
\frac25\energy{\mu}^{5/2}
+\frac{\pi^2}{4}(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})^2\energy{\mu}^{1/2}
+\cdots
\right].
$$

Equation (13.19) gives

$$
\energy{\mu}^{5/2}
=\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}^{5/2}
\left[
1-\frac{5\pi^2}{24}
\left(\frac{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}{\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}}\right)^2
+\cdots
\right].
$$

Collect the quadratic terms and use $(2/5)A_{3\mathrm D}\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}^{5/2}=(3/5)N\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}$:

$$
\energy{U}(\thermal{T})
=\frac35N\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}
\left[
1+\frac{5\pi^2}{12}
\left(\frac{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}{\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}}\right)^2
+O((\thermal{T}/\thermal{T}_{\mathrm F})^4)
\right].
$$

### Heat capacity

$$
\begin{aligned}
\thermal{C}_V
&=\left(\frac{\partial\energy{U}}{\partial\thermal{T}}\right)_{N,V}\\
&\approx\frac35N\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}
\frac{5\pi^2}{6}
\frac{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}^2\thermal{T}}
{\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}^2}\\
&=\frac{\pi^2}{2}N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}
\frac{\thermal{T}}{\thermal{T}_{\mathrm F}}
\qquad(\thermal{T}\ll\thermal{T}_{\mathrm F}).
\end{aligned}
$$

### Checks and interpretation

The ratio $\thermal{C}_V/(N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B})$ is dimensionless, as is temperature divided by Fermi temperature. At a representative metal ratio of $10^{-2}$, the heat-capacity ratio is about $5\times10^{-2}$, far below the classical value $3/2$.

Electronic heat capacity grows linearly with temperature. In the source's molar convention, the Sommerfeld coefficient is

$$
\thermal{\gamma}=\frac{\pi^2}{2}\frac{\thermal{R}}{\thermal{T}_{\mathrm F}}.
$$

Only electrons in an energy band of width of order $\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}$ around the Fermi surface can absorb energy. Their fraction is of order $\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}/\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}$, and each contributes heat capacity of order $\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}$.

## Exercise 6.5: Planck spectrum

**Task.** Photons are spin-1 bosons with two physical polarizations, $g_s=2$, and zero chemical potential. Show that the blackbody spectral energy density is

$$
\energy{u}(\signal{\nu})=
\frac{8\pi\signal{h}\signal{\nu}^3}{\mechanical{c}^3}
\frac1{e^{\signal{h}\signal{\nu}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})}-1}.
$$

**Solution.** Photons have linear dispersion,

$$
\energy{\varepsilon}
=\signal{\hbar}\signal{\omega}
=\mechanical{c}\mechanical{p}
=\signal{\hbar}\mechanical{c}\signal{k}.
$$

Photon number is not conserved.

The cavity walls create and absorb photons in thermal equilibrium, so $\energy{\mu}=0$ and $z=1$. A mode has mean occupation

$$
\bar n(\signal{\omega})
=\frac1{e^{\thermal{\beta}\signal{\hbar}\signal{\omega}}-1}.
$$

### Photon density of states

In a cubic volume $\mechanical{V}=\mechanical{L}^3$ with periodic boundaries, states are specified by wavevector and either of two polarizations. The density in wavevector space is $\mechanical{V}/(2\pi)^3$:

$$
d\mathcal N=
2\frac{\mechanical{V}}{(2\pi)^3}
4\pi\signal{k}^2\,d\signal{k}
=\frac{\mechanical{V}}{\pi^2}\signal{k}^2\,d\signal{k}.
$$

With $\signal{\omega}=\mechanical{c}\signal{k}$,

$$
g(\signal{\omega})\,d\signal{\omega}
=\frac{\mechanical{V}}{\pi^2\mechanical{c}^3}
\signal{\omega}^2\,d\signal{\omega}.
$$

Equivalently, in energy,

$$
g(\energy{\varepsilon})
=\frac{\mechanical{V}}{\pi^2(\signal{\hbar}\mechanical{c})^3}
\energy{\varepsilon}^2.
$$

This is the $g_s=2$ case of Exercise 6.7.

### Energy per volume

Multiply mean occupation by mode energy and mode density, then divide by volume:

$$
\energy{u}(\signal{\omega})
=\frac{\signal{\hbar}\signal{\omega}\bar n(\signal{\omega})
g(\signal{\omega})}{\mechanical{V}}
=\frac{\signal{\hbar}\signal{\omega}^3}{\pi^2\mechanical{c}^3}
\frac1{e^{\thermal{\beta}\signal{\hbar}\signal{\omega}}-1}.
$$

### Ordinary-frequency spectrum

Since $\signal{\omega}=2\pi\signal{\nu}$, the interval relation
$\energy{u}(\signal{\nu})\,d\signal{\nu} =\energy{u}(\signal{\omega})\,d\signal{\omega}$ gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\energy{u}(\signal{\nu})
&=2\pi\energy{u}(2\pi\signal{\nu})\\
&=\frac{16\pi^2\signal{\hbar}\signal{\nu}^3}{\mechanical{c}^3}
\frac1{e^{\signal{h}\signal{\nu}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})}-1}\\
&=\frac{8\pi\signal{h}\signal{\nu}^3}{\mechanical{c}^3}
\frac1{e^{\signal{h}\signal{\nu}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})}-1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This uses $\signal{h}=2\pi\signal{\hbar}$ and is **Planck's blackbody radiation law**.

### Planck-law checks

**Units.** Spectral energy density per ordinary frequency has units $\unit{J\,m^{-3}\,Hz^{-1}}$. The factor $\signal{h}\signal{\nu}^3/\mechanical{c}^3$ has the same units.

**Rayleigh–Jeans limit.** For $\signal{h}\signal{\nu}\ll\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}$,

$$
e^{\signal{h}\signal{\nu}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})}-1
\approx\frac{\signal{h}\signal{\nu}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}},
\qquad
\energy{u}(\signal{\nu})
\approx\frac{8\pi\signal{\nu}^2}{\mechanical{c}^3}
\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}.
$$

This is the classical limit; the full Bose factor avoids its ultraviolet catastrophe.

**Wien limit.** At high frequency,

$$
\energy{u}(\signal{\nu})
\sim\frac{8\pi\signal{h}\signal{\nu}^3}{\mechanical{c}^3}
e^{-\signal{h}\signal{\nu}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})}.
$$

**Stefan–Boltzmann law.** Integration over frequency gives a fourth-power temperature dependence:

$$
\frac{\energy{U}}{\mechanical{V}}
=\int_0^\infty\energy{u}(\signal{\nu})\,d\signal{\nu}
=\frac{4\thermal{\sigma}}{\mechanical{c}}\thermal{T}^4.
$$

## Exercise 6.6: Ordering of quantum occupations

**Task.** At the same energy greater than chemical potential, temperature, and chemical potential, show

$$
n_{\mathrm{BE}}(\energy{\varepsilon})
>n_{\mathrm{MB}}(\energy{\varepsilon})
>n_{\mathrm{FD}}(\energy{\varepsilon}),
$$

and interpret the result.

**Solution.** Put $x=\thermal{\beta}(\energy{\varepsilon}-\energy{\mu})>0$. Then

$$
n_{\mathrm{BE}}=\frac1{e^x-1},
\qquad
n_{\mathrm{MB}}=e^{-x},
\qquad
n_{\mathrm{FD}}=\frac1{e^x+1}.
$$

Since $0<e^x-1<e^x<e^x+1$, reciprocation reverses the inequalities:

$$
\frac1{e^x-1}>\frac1{e^x}>\frac1{e^x+1}.
$$

Thus the stated occupation ordering follows.

The unified form is

$$
n_{\mathrm{FD,BE}}
=\frac{n_{\mathrm{MB}}}{1\pm n_{\mathrm{MB}}},
$$

with plus for fermions and minus for bosons. The denominator gives the quantum correction: Bose occupation is enhanced and Fermi occupation suppressed.

### Physical interpretation

**Bosons: statistical enhancement.** Symmetric wavefunctions favour bunching. The bosonic transition or addition factor $n+1$ enhances occupation of an already populated mode. This is related to stimulated emission and, in equilibrium, to Bose condensation as chemical potential approaches the ground-state energy from below.

**Fermions: statistical suppression.** Antisymmetry and Pauli exclusion prevent two identical fermions from occupying the same one-particle state. The occupation is therefore lower than its classical value at fixed temperature, chemical potential, and energy. This underlies degeneracy pressure.

**Classical limit.** If $x\gg1$, all three distributions tend to the same Boltzmann exponential. Quantum-statistical corrections become small in the dilute regime $n\signal{\lambda}_T^3\ll1$.

> [!note] Meaning of the enhancement factor
> The factor $n+1$ describes the bosonic enhancement of a transition or particle-addition probability. It is not a universal multiplicative factor between the full equilibrium occupation and the classical occupation.

## Exercise 6.7: Ultrarelativistic density of states

**Task.** For particles with $\energy{\varepsilon}=\signal{\hbar}\mechanical{c}\signal{k}$, calculate the three-dimensional density of states and show

$$
g_{\mathrm{rel}}(\energy{\varepsilon})
=\frac{g_s\mechanical{V}\energy{\varepsilon}^2}
{2\pi^2(\signal{\hbar}\mechanical{c})^3}.
$$

Compare this with the nonrelativistic result and discuss the equation of state.

**Solution.** Repeat the state-counting method with linear dispersion instead of $\energy{\varepsilon}=\signal{\hbar}^2\signal{k}^2/(2\mechanical{m})$.

### State counting

For periodic boundaries in volume $\mechanical{V}=\mechanical{L}^3$,

$$
d\mathcal N=g_s\frac{\mechanical{V}}{(2\pi)^3}\,d^3\signal{k}.
$$

Spherical coordinates give

$$
d\mathcal N
=\frac{g_s\mechanical{V}}{2\pi^2}
\signal{k}^2\,d\signal{k}.
$$

From $\energy{\varepsilon}=\signal{\hbar}\mechanical{c}\signal{k}$,

$$
\signal{k}=\frac{\energy{\varepsilon}}{\signal{\hbar}\mechanical{c}},
\qquad
d\signal{k}=\frac{d\energy{\varepsilon}}{\signal{\hbar}\mechanical{c}},
\qquad
\signal{k}^2\,d\signal{k}
=\frac{\energy{\varepsilon}^2\,d\energy{\varepsilon}}
{(\signal{\hbar}\mechanical{c})^3}.
$$

Thus

$$
g_{\mathrm{rel}}(\energy{\varepsilon})\,d\energy{\varepsilon}
=\frac{g_s\mechanical{V}}{2\pi^2}
\frac{\energy{\varepsilon}^2\,d\energy{\varepsilon}}
{(\signal{\hbar}\mechanical{c})^3},
$$

which proves the stated density of states.

### Comparison

$$
g_{\mathrm{NR}}(\energy{\varepsilon})\propto\energy{\varepsilon}^{1/2},
\qquad
g_{\mathrm{rel}}(\energy{\varepsilon})\propto\energy{\varepsilon}^{2}.
$$

The ultrarelativistic density grows faster. For a general dispersion $\energy{\varepsilon}\propto\signal{k}^s$ in d dimensions,

$$
g(\energy{\varepsilon})\propto\energy{\varepsilon}^{d/s-1}.
$$

The exponents are $1/2$ for $d=3,s=2$ and $2$ for $d=3,s=1$.

### Equation of state

Write $g(\energy{\varepsilon})=B\energy{\varepsilon}^2$, where $B=g_s\mechanical{V}/[2\pi^2(\signal{\hbar}\mechanical{c})^3]$. Repeat the integration-by-parts argument of Exercise 6.1 with antiderivative $\energy{\varepsilon}^3/3$.

Using upper signs for bosons and lower signs for fermions, with vanishing boundary terms,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}
&=\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}\ln\Xi\\
&=\mp\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
\int_0^\infty B\energy{\varepsilon}^2
\ln[1\mp e^{-\thermal{\beta}(\energy{\varepsilon}-\energy{\mu})}]
\,d\energy{\varepsilon}\\
&=\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}B
\int_0^\infty\frac13\energy{\varepsilon}^3
\thermal{\beta}\bar n(\energy{\varepsilon})\,d\energy{\varepsilon}\\
&=\frac13\int_0^\infty
\energy{\varepsilon}g_{\mathrm{rel}}(\energy{\varepsilon})
\bar n(\energy{\varepsilon})\,d\energy{\varepsilon}
=\frac13\energy{U}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus $\mechanical{P}\mechanical{V}=\energy{U}/3$ for ultrarelativistic particles. The pressure-to-energy-density ratio is half its nonrelativistic value.

### Consequence: Stefan–Boltzmann scaling

For photons, chemical potential is zero and spin degeneracy is two:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\energy{U}}{\mechanical{V}}
&=\frac1{\pi^2(\signal{\hbar}\mechanical{c})^3}
\int_0^\infty
\frac{\energy{\varepsilon}^3\,d\energy{\varepsilon}}
{e^{\thermal{\beta}\energy{\varepsilon}}-1}\\
&=\frac{(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})^4}
{\pi^2(\signal{\hbar}\mechanical{c})^3}
\int_0^\infty\frac{x^3\,dx}{e^x-1}\\
&=\frac{(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})^4}
{\pi^2(\signal{\hbar}\mechanical{c})^3}\Gamma(4)\zeta(4)\\
&=\frac{\pi^2}{15}
\frac{(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})^4}
{(\signal{\hbar}\mechanical{c})^3}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence

$$
\mechanical{P}
=\frac{\pi^2}{45}
\frac{(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})^4}
{(\signal{\hbar}\mechanical{c})^3}.
$$

Both pressure and energy density scale as the fourth power of temperature.

**Units.** The product $\signal{\hbar}\mechanical{c}$ has units of energy times length. The relativistic density of states therefore has units of inverse energy.

### High-energy limit for relativistic fermions

At zero temperature, the corresponding Fermi energy is

$$
\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}^{\mathrm{rel}}
=\signal{\hbar}\mechanical{c}
\left(\frac{6\pi^2n}{g_s}\right)^{1/3}.
$$

Integration gives

$$
\energy{U}_0=
\frac{g_s\mechanical{V}(\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}^{\mathrm{rel}})^4}
{8\pi^2(\signal{\hbar}\mechanical{c})^3}
=\frac34N\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}^{\mathrm{rel}},
$$

rather than the nonrelativistic factor $3/5$. Therefore

$$
\mechanical{P}_0
=\frac{\energy{U}_0}{3\mechanical{V}}
=\frac14n\energy{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm F}^{\mathrm{rel}}.
$$

Relativistic degeneracy pressure is central to compact-star models and the Chandrasekhar mass scale.
