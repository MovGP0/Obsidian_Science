## Exercise 4.1: Independent quantum harmonic oscillators

**Task.** Find the partition function and free energy of $N$ quantum harmonic oscillators with angular frequencies $\signal{\omega}_1,\ldots,\signal{\omega}_N$. Show that free energy and the other thermodynamic quantities are additive.

**Solution.** The oscillators are distinguishable, identified by their frequencies and generally fixed at different sites. They are independent, with total Hamiltonian

$$
\energy{\mathcal H}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\energy{\mathcal H}_i,
\qquad
\energy{\mathcal H}_i=
\frac{\mechanical{p}_i^2}{2\mechanical{m}_i}
+\frac12\mechanical{m}_i\signal{\omega}_i^2\mechanical{q}_i^2.
$$

On the tensor-product Hilbert space,

$$
e^{-\thermal{\beta}\energy{\mathcal H}}
=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}e^{-\thermal{\beta}\energy{\mathcal H}_i}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\operatorname{Tr}(e^{-\thermal{\beta}\energy{\mathcal H}})
=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\operatorname{Tr}_i(e^{-\thermal{\beta}\energy{\mathcal H}_i}).
$$

There is no Gibbs factor $1/N!$, since the oscillators are distinguishable. Equation (4.13) gives the single-oscillator trace:

$$
\mathcal Z(\thermal{T})
=\prod_{i=1}^{N}Z_1^{\mathrm{qu}}(\signal{\omega}_i)
=\prod_{i=1}^{N}
\frac1{2\sinh(\thermal{\beta}\signal{\hbar}\signal{\omega}_i/2)}.
$$

### Free energy

By Theorem 4.1,

$$
\energy{F}
=-\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}\ln\mathcal Z
=-\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ln Z_1^{\mathrm{qu}}(\signal{\omega}_i)
=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\energy{F}_i.
$$

For one oscillator,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\energy{F}_i
&=-\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
\ln\!\left[\frac1{2\sinh(\thermal{\beta}\signal{\hbar}\signal{\omega}_i/2)}\right]\\
&=\frac{\signal{\hbar}\signal{\omega}_i}{2}
+\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
\ln(1-e^{-\thermal{\beta}\signal{\hbar}\signal{\omega}_i}).
\end{aligned}
$$

The first term in $\energy{F}_i$ is the zero-point energy; the second describes thermal mode excitations.

### Additive quantities

Quantities obtained by linear differentiation inherit additivity:

$$
\energy{U}=-\frac{\partial\ln\mathcal Z}{\partial\thermal{\beta}}
=\sum_i\frac{\signal{\hbar}\signal{\omega}_i}{2}
\coth\!\left(\frac{\thermal{\beta}\signal{\hbar}\signal{\omega}_i}{2}\right)
=\sum_i\energy{U}_i,
$$

$$
\thermal{S}=-\frac{\partial\energy{F}}{\partial\thermal{T}}
=\sum_i\thermal{S}_i,
\qquad
\thermal{C}_V=
\left.\frac{\partial\energy{U}}{\partial\thermal{T}}\right|_V
=\sum_i\thermal{C}_{V,i}.
$$

Statistical independence also makes the energy variance additive:

$$
\sigma_E^2=\sum_i\sigma_{E_i}^2\propto N.
$$

For comparable modes, relative fluctuations therefore scale as

$$
\frac{\sigma_E}{\langle\energy{E}\rangle}
\sim\frac{\sqrt N}{N}=\frac1{\sqrt N},
$$

as in Section 4.4. Relative fluctuations are not additive.

**Dimensional check.** The partition function is dimensionless, and free energy and $\signal{\hbar}\signal{\omega}$ have energy units. For $\thermal{\beta}\signal{\hbar}\signal{\omega}_i\ll1$,

$$
2\sinh(\thermal{\beta}\signal{\hbar}\signal{\omega}_i/2)
\approx\thermal{\beta}\signal{\hbar}\signal{\omega}_i,
$$

so

$$
\energy{F}_i
\to\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
\ln(\thermal{\beta}\signal{\hbar}\signal{\omega}_i)
=-\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
\ln\frac{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}{\signal{\hbar}\signal{\omega}_i},
$$

which recovers the classical result in Section 4.7.

**Physical comment.** Free-energy additivity expresses statistical independence: joint probabilities factorize, giving $\ln\mathcal Z=\sum_i\ln Z_i$. For phonons in a solid, replacing the discrete frequency sum by a mode distribution $g(\signal{\omega})\,d\signal{\omega}$ leads toward the Debye model.

## Exercise 4.2: Three nondegenerate energy levels

**Task.** For levels $0,\energy{\varepsilon},2\energy{\varepsilon}$, calculate $\mathcal Z(\thermal{T})$, $\langle\energy{E}\rangle(\thermal{T})$, and $\thermal{C}(\thermal{T})$. Find their zero- and infinite-temperature limits.

**Solution.** Set $x=\thermal{\beta}\energy{\varepsilon}=\energy{\varepsilon}/(\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})$.

### (a) Partition function

Equation (4.4) gives

$$
\mathcal Z(\thermal{T})=1+e^{-x}+e^{-2x}
=\frac{1-e^{-3x}}{1-e^{-x}}
\qquad(x>0).
$$

This is a finite geometric sum, or q-number structure, with $q=e^{-x}$ and $\mathcal Z=1+q+q^2=(1-q^3)/(1-q)$.

### (b) Mean energy

Use $\langle\energy{E}\rangle=-\partial_{\beta}\ln\mathcal Z$ and $\partial_\beta=\energy{\varepsilon}\partial_x$:

$$
\langle\energy{E}\rangle
=-\energy{\varepsilon}\frac{d\ln\mathcal Z}{dx}
=\energy{\varepsilon}
\frac{e^{-x}+2e^{-2x}}{1+e^{-x}+e^{-2x}}.
$$

Multiply numerator and denominator by $e^x$:

$$
\langle\energy{E}\rangle(\thermal{T})
=\energy{\varepsilon}\frac{1+2e^{-x}}{e^x+1+e^{-x}}.
\tag{13.9}
$$

> [!note] Source correction
> One intermediate numerator in the source is printed as $e^{-3x}+2e^{-2x}$. It must be $e^{-x}+2e^{-2x}$. The source's final Equation (13.9) has the correct form.

### (c) Heat capacity

Since

$$
\thermal{C}
=\frac{d\langle\energy{E}\rangle}{d\thermal{T}}
=-\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{\beta}^2
\frac{d\langle\energy{E}\rangle}{d\thermal{\beta}},
$$

write $\langle\energy{E}\rangle=\energy{\varepsilon}f(x)$, with $f=(e^{-x}+2e^{-2x})/\mathcal Z$. Then $\thermal{C}=-\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}x^2f'(x)$. Using $\mathcal Z'=-e^{-x}-2e^{-2x}$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{df}{dx}
&=\frac{(-e^{-x}-4e^{-2x})\mathcal Z
-(e^{-x}+2e^{-2x})(-e^{-x}-2e^{-2x})}{\mathcal Z^2}\\
&=-\frac{e^{-x}(1+4e^{-x}+e^{-2x})}{\mathcal Z^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus

$$
\thermal{C}(\thermal{T})=
\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}
\left(\frac{\energy{\varepsilon}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}\right)^2
\frac{e^{-x}(1+4e^{-x}+e^{-2x})}
{(1+e^{-x}+e^{-2x})^2}.
\tag{13.10}
$$

### (d) Temperature limits

- **Low temperature**, $x\to\infty$: $\mathcal Z\to1$, $\langle\energy{E}\rangle\sim\energy{\varepsilon}e^{-x}\to0$, and $\thermal{C}\sim\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}x^2e^{-x}\to0$. The first excitation gives an activated contribution with gap $\energy{\varepsilon}$.
- **High temperature**, $x\to0$: $\mathcal Z\to3$ and all three levels become equally probable. The mean energy approaches $(0+\energy{\varepsilon}+2\energy{\varepsilon})/3=\energy{\varepsilon}$, while $\thermal{C}\sim(2/3)\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}x^2\to0$. A finite level spectrum saturates.

**Verification.** Expanding Equation (13.9),

$$
\langle\energy{E}\rangle
\approx\energy{\varepsilon}
\left[1-\frac23x+O(x^2)\right],
$$

so $\thermal{C}\approx(2/3)\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}x^2$. At large $x$, $\thermal{C}\approx\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}x^2e^{-x}$, as expected for a finite gap.

**Physical comment.** This is a generalized Schottky peak. The maximum occurs at a temperature of order $\energy{\varepsilon}/\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}$. Numerical solution of $d\thermal{C}/dx=0$ gives $x^*\approx1.881$ and $\thermal{C}^*\approx0.637\,\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}$. For two nondegenerate levels the corresponding values are $x^*\approx2.40$ and $\thermal{C}^*\approx0.439\,\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}$. The change reflects the different level structure.

## Exercise 4.3: Rigid diatomic gas

**Task.** For rotational levels

$$
\energy{E}_\ell=
\frac{\signal{\hbar}^2\ell(\ell+1)}{2\mechanical{I}},
$$

with degeneracy $2\ell+1$, verify

$$
\thermal{C}_V\to\frac52N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}
\quad\text{at high temperature},
\qquad
\thermal{C}_V\to\frac32N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}
\quad\text{at low temperature}.
$$

**Solution.** Section 4.6 gives the translational contribution $\thermal{C}_V^{\mathrm{tr}}=3N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}/2$. It remains to calculate the rotational part.

Define the characteristic temperature from Equation (4.18),

$$
\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm{rot}}
=\frac{\signal{\hbar}^2}{2\mechanical{I}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}},
\qquad
\energy{E}_\ell=\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm{rot}}\ell(\ell+1).
$$

For one molecule,

$$
Z_1^{\mathrm{rot,qu}}(\thermal{T})
=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+1)
\exp\!\left[-\frac{\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm{rot}}}{\thermal{T}}\ell(\ell+1)\right].
\tag{13.11}
$$

### High-temperature limit

For $\thermal{T}\gg\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm{rot}}$, put $u=\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm{rot}}/\thermal{T}\ll1$. The summand varies slowly with $\ell$. Euler–Maclaurin summation, or replacement by an integral, gives

$$
Z_1^{\mathrm{rot,qu}}
\approx\int_0^\infty(2\ell+1)e^{-u\ell(\ell+1)}\,d\ell.
$$

With $t=\ell(\ell+1)$ and $dt=(2\ell+1)d\ell$,

$$
Z_1^{\mathrm{rot,qu}}
\approx\int_0^\infty e^{-ut}\,dt
=\frac1u=\frac{\thermal{T}}{\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm{rot}}}.
$$

This recovers the classical result (4.18). The next corrections are

$$
Z_1^{\mathrm{rot,qu}}
=\frac1u\left[1+\frac u3+\frac{u^2}{15}+O(u^3)\right],
$$

so

$$
\ln Z_1^{\mathrm{rot,qu}}
=-\ln u+\frac u3+\frac{u^2}{90}+O(u^3).
$$

The coefficient $1/90$ follows from $1/15-\tfrac12(1/3)^2$. The molecular energy is

$$
\energy{u}^{\mathrm{rot}}
=-\partial_\beta\ln Z_1^{\mathrm{rot,qu}}
=\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
-\frac{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm{rot}}}{3}
+\text{higher-order terms}.
$$

The constant correction does not contribute to heat capacity. Therefore

$$
\thermal{C}_V^{\mathrm{rot}}
=N\frac{d\energy{u}^{\mathrm{rot}}}{d\thermal{T}}
\longrightarrow N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B},
\qquad
\thermal{C}_V^{\mathrm{tot}}
\longrightarrow\frac52N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}.
$$

This agrees with three translational and two rotational quadratic degrees of freedom.

### Low-temperature limit

For $\thermal{T}\ll\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm{rot}}$, retain $\ell=0,1$ in Equation (13.11):

$$
Z_1^{\mathrm{rot,qu}}
\approx1+3e^{-2\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm{rot}}/\thermal{T}}.
$$

Higher levels are suppressed at least as strongly as $e^{-6\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm{rot}}/\thermal{T}}$.

Then $\ln Z_1^{\mathrm{rot,qu}}\approx3e^{-2\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm{rot}}/\thermal{T}}$, and

$$
\energy{u}^{\mathrm{rot}}
=-\frac{\partial\ln Z_1^{\mathrm{rot,qu}}}{\partial\thermal{\beta}}
\approx6\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm{rot}}
e^{-2\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm{rot}}/\thermal{T}}.
$$

The molecular rotational heat capacity is

$$
\thermal{c}_V^{\mathrm{rot}}
\approx12\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}
\left(\frac{\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm{rot}}}{\thermal{T}}\right)^2
e^{-2\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm{rot}}/\thermal{T}}
\longrightarrow0.
$$

The exponential suppression reflects the gap $2\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm{rot}}$ between $\ell=0$ and $\ell=1$. Thus

$$
\thermal{C}_V^{\mathrm{tot}}
\longrightarrow\frac32N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}
\qquad(\thermal{T}\ll\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm{rot}}).
$$

The rotational degrees of freedom are frozen out.

**Dimensional check and scales.** The rotational partition function is dimensionless. The source gives $\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm{rot}}\approx87\,\unit{K}$ for hydrogen, with an approximate lower plateau below $50\,\unit{K}$ and upper plateau above about $200\,\unit{K}$. For nitrogen, $\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm{rot}}\approx2.9\,\unit{K}$, so rotation is active at room temperature.

> [!note] Interpolation and translational regime
> The source calls the connection between the plateaus monotonic. The high-temperature expansion instead gives a small correction $\thermal{c}_V^{\mathrm{rot}}/\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}=1+\thermal{\Theta}_{\mathrm{rot}}^2/(45\thermal{T}^2)+\cdots$, so the classical rotational value can be approached from above. The translational contribution here assumes a classical dilute gas; it is not a claim about the strict zero-temperature quantum gas.

**Physical comment.** Freeze-out is a quantum effect. When thermal energy is much smaller than a discrete excitation gap, transitions are exponentially suppressed and classical equipartition no longer applies. The same mechanism appears in the Einstein model of phonons and in the freezing of molecular vibrations.

> [!note] Homonuclear molecules
> Molecules such as hydrogen and nitrogen have a rotational symmetry number $\sigma=2$. Nuclear-spin statistics produces ortho and para forms. These effects lie beyond the exercise's scope.

## Exercise 4.4: Ideal-gas pressure and a two-dimensional analogue

**Task.** Derive ideal-gas pressure from

$$
\mechanical{p}=\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
\left(\frac{\partial\ln\mathcal Z}{\partial\mechanical{V}}\right)_T.
$$

Find the corresponding quantity $\mechanical{\gamma}$ for a gas confined to area $\mechanical{A}$.

### Three-dimensional gas

Equation (4.11) gives

$$
Z_N=\frac1{N!}
\left(\frac{\mechanical{V}}{\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}^3}\right)^N,
\qquad
\ln Z_N=N\ln\mechanical{V}
-3N\ln\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}-\ln N!.
$$

Neither the thermal wavelength nor the factorial depends on volume. Thus

$$
\mechanical{p}
=\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
\left(\frac{\partial\ln Z_N}{\partial\mechanical{V}}\right)_{T,N}
=\frac{N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}{\mechanical{V}},
\qquad
\mechanical{p}\mechanical{V}=N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}.
$$

This independently recovers the equation of state obtained from free energy.

### Two-dimensional gas

For free particles, $\energy{\mathcal H}=\sum_i\mechanical{\mathbf p}_i^2/(2\mechanical{m})$, with two-component momenta and phase-space measure $d^2\mechanical{q}\,d^2\mechanical{p}/\signal{h}^2$. For one particle,

$$
\begin{aligned}
Z_1^{(2)}
&=\frac1{\signal{h}^2}\int d^2\mechanical{q}\int d^2\mechanical{p}\,
e^{-\thermal{\beta}\mechanical{\mathbf p}^2/(2\mechanical{m})}\\
&=\frac{\mechanical{A}}{\signal{h}^2}
(2\pi\mechanical{m}\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T})
=\frac{\mechanical{A}}{\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

For N indistinguishable particles,

$$
Z_N^{(2)}=\frac1{N!}
\left(\frac{\mechanical{A}}{\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}^2}\right)^N.
$$

Using the thermodynamic-limit Stirling approximation,

$$
\energy{F}^{(2)}
=-\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}\ln Z_N^{(2)}
=-N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
\left[\ln\frac{\mechanical{A}}{N\signal{\lambda}_{\mathrm{th}}^2}+1\right].
$$

### Quantity conjugate to area

Define the pressure analogue

$$
\mechanical{\gamma}
=-\left(\frac{\partial\energy{F}^{(2)}}{\partial\mechanical{A}}\right)_{T,N}
=\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
\left(\frac{\partial\ln Z_N^{(2)}}{\partial\mechanical{A}}\right)_{T,N}
=\frac{N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}{\mechanical{A}}.
$$

The two-dimensional equation of state is

$$
\mechanical{\gamma}\mechanical{A}
=N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}.
\tag{13.12}
$$

It has the same structure as the three-dimensional ideal-gas law.

**Dimensional check.**

$$
[\mechanical{\gamma}]
=\frac{[\energy{F}]}{[\mechanical{A}]}
=\unit{J\,m^{-2}}=\unit{N\,m^{-1}}.
$$

The quantity is a two-dimensional pressure, often written $\mechanical{\Pi}$. Equation (13.12) is the dilute ideal-gas limit of surface pressure in a Langmuir monolayer, such as fatty-acid films at an air–water interface.

**Physical comment.** This positive outward pressure differs from ordinary liquid surface tension,

$$
\mechanical{\sigma}_{\mathrm{liq}}
=+\left(\frac{\partial\energy{F}}{\partial\mechanical{A}}\right)>0.
$$

A liquid interface costs free energy as its area increases. For a two-dimensional gas, expansion at fixed temperature and particle count increases entropy and lowers free energy, so its negative area derivative is positive.

Equipartition gives two kinetic degrees of freedom per particle and $\energy{U}=N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}$. More generally,

$$
\mechanical{\gamma}\mechanical{A}
=\frac2d\energy{U}
=N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}
\qquad(d=2),
$$

analogous to $\mechanical{p}\mechanical{V}=(2/3)\energy{U}$ for a three-dimensional ideal gas.

> [!note] Source heading
> The source calls this “negative surface tension”. Its defined quantity $\mechanical{\gamma}$ is positive; it is the negative of the usual area derivative used for tensile surface stress.

## Exercise 4.5: Adiabatic demagnetization

**Task.** Show that an isentropic change of an N-spin system gives $\thermal{T}_{\mathrm f}\to0$ as $\magnetic{B}_{\mathrm f}\to0$.

**Solution.** Equations (4.15) and (4.17) give

$$
\thermal{S}(\thermal{T},\magnetic{B})
=N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}s(x),
\qquad
s(x)=\ln[2\cosh x]-x\tanh x,
\qquad
x=\frac{\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}}.
\tag{13.13}
$$

The function has these properties:

1. $s(0)=\ln2$, the entropy maximum for $2^N$ equally probable spin configurations.
2. $s(x)\to0$ as $|x|\to\infty$, for complete polarization.
3. $s'(x)=-x\operatorname{sech}^2x\le0$ for $x\ge0$; it decreases strictly for positive $x$.

On the positive-temperature, nonnegative-field branch, entropy fixes the ratio of field to temperature.

### Adiabatic condition

A reversible adiabatic change is isentropic:

$$
\thermal{S}(\thermal{T}_0,\magnetic{B}_0)
=\thermal{S}(\thermal{T}_{\mathrm f},\magnetic{B}_{\mathrm f}).
$$

Hence

$$
\frac{\magnetic{B}_0}{\thermal{T}_0}
=\frac{\magnetic{B}_{\mathrm f}}{\thermal{T}_{\mathrm f}}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\thermal{T}_{\mathrm f}
=\thermal{T}_0\frac{\magnetic{B}_{\mathrm f}}{\magnetic{B}_0}.
\tag{13.14}
$$

The ideal model therefore approaches zero temperature as the field tends to zero. This is the principle of **adiabatic demagnetization cooling** (Debye, 1926; Giauque, 1933).

### Practical limits and the apparent paradox

Equation (13.14) does not violate the third law. The model assumes perfectly noninteracting spins; real crystals have dipolar and superexchange interactions.

The interactions produce a residual internal field, typically $\magnetic{B}_{\mathrm{int}}\sim10^{-3}\,\unit{T}$. Below that field scale, the independent-spin description fails and ordering or slow relaxation limits cooling.

Further limits are:

- At very low temperature the spin subsystem decouples from the lattice: a **phonon bottleneck**. Spin–phonon heat transport becomes too weak for equilibration in finite time.
- A quasistatic field change requires a sweep rate small compared with the spin–lattice relaxation rate:

$$
\left|\frac{d\magnetic{B}}{d\mechanical{t}}\right|
\ll\frac{\magnetic{B}}{\thermal{\tau}_1}.
$$

The source quotes relaxation times of order hundreds of milliseconds at low temperature. The ideal zero-temperature limit requires both final external field and internal field to vanish, with an arbitrarily slow process.

> [!note] Sweep-rate sign
> The source writes the rate condition without absolute-value bars. During demagnetization the field derivative is negative; the physical slowness condition concerns its magnitude.

### Verification

For a fixed initial ratio of field to temperature:

- If $\magnetic{B}_0/\thermal{T}_0\ll\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}/\magnetic{\mu}$, then $x\to0$ and $\thermal{S}\to N\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\ln2$.
- If $\magnetic{B}_0/\thermal{T}_0\gg\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}/\magnetic{\mu}$, then $x\to\infty$. Since $\cosh x\approx e^x/2$ and $\tanh x\approx1$, $s(x)\approx\ln(e^x)-x=0$, and entropy vanishes.

The field energy $\magnetic{\mu}\magnetic{B}$ and thermal energy $\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\thermal{T}$ both have units of joules, so $x$ is dimensionless. Entropy has the units of Boltzmann constant.

**Physical comment.** Magnetic refrigeration can reach millikelvin temperatures in paramagnetic salts, such as cerium or chromium salts. Adiabatic nuclear demagnetization can reach microkelvin temperatures or below. Equation (13.14) expresses the fact that the spin Boltzmann weights depend only on the product of inverse temperature and field.

An isentropic change keeps the level occupation probabilities fixed by rescaling field and temperature together.
