> [!TLDR]
> Near criticality, order-parameter fluctuations extend from microscopic to macroscopic scales.

Near the critical point, the correlation length $\mechanical{\xi}\to\infty$. Order-parameter fluctuations then extend across many spatial scales, from the atomic lattice to macroscopic lengths. Traditional methods cannot treat all these scales easily.

Leo Kadanoff's idea (1966) uses the system's scale invariance at criticality. Repeatedly combine microscopic degrees of freedom into larger blocks, then describe the blocks as a new system.

Away from the transition, the finite correlation length separates two regimes: microscopic details matter at short distances, while widely separated regions become nearly independent. Near the transition this separation moves to much larger distances. There is then no small set of independent microscopic regions on which a local mean-field average can rely.

A block description should be compared at distances large compared with the lattice spacing and small compared with both the sample size and, away from criticality, the correlation length. Scale invariance describes this critical range, not atomic structure or every length in a finite laboratory sample. A useful reconstruction question is why a finite sample cannot display a literal infinite correlation length: its largest available separation limits the range over which scaling can be tested.
