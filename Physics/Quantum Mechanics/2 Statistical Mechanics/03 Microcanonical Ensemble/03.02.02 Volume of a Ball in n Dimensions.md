> [!TLDR]
> A Gaussian integral gives the volume of a ball in any integer dimension.

Calculating the ideal-gas phase-space volume requires the volume of a ball.

## Proposition 3.1 — Volume of an n-dimensional ball

For radius $R$:

$$
V_n(R)=\frac{\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma(n/2+1)}R^n.
\tag{3.1}
$$

Here $\Gamma$ is the Euler gamma function. The symbols in this mathematical derivation are integration and geometric variables, rather than thermodynamic quantities.

## Proof

Consider the Gaussian integral:

$$
I_n=\int_{\mathbb R^n}e^{-|\mathbf x|^2}\,\mathrm d^n x.
$$

Factorization into one-dimensional integrals gives:

$$
I_n=\left(\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-x^2}\,\mathrm dx\right)^n
=\pi^{n/2}.
$$

Now use spherical coordinates. Write the area of the unit sphere as $S_{n-1}$. Then:

$$
I_n=S_{n-1}\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}r^{n-1}\,\mathrm dr
=\frac{S_{n-1}}2\Gamma(n/2).
$$

Comparison gives $S_{n-1}=2\pi^{n/2}/\Gamma(n/2)$. Integrate the spherical shells:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V_n(R)
&=S_{n-1}\int_0^R r^{n-1}\,\mathrm dr\\
&=\frac{2\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma(n/2)}\frac{R^n}{n}\\
&=\frac{\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma(n/2+1)}R^n.
\end{aligned}
$$

The last step uses $\Gamma(n/2+1)=(n/2)\Gamma(n/2)$. $\square$

The familiar cases are:

$$
V_1(R)=2R,\qquad V_2(R)=\pi R^2,\qquad
V_3(R)=\frac43\pi R^3.
$$

For large $n$, Stirling's approximation gives:

$$
\Gamma(n/2+1)\approx\sqrt{\pi n}\,(n/2)^{n/2}e^{-n/2}.
$$

The fraction of the ball inside radius $(1-\epsilon)R$ is $(1-\epsilon)^n$. For fixed $0<\epsilon<1$, this tends to zero as dimension increases. Thus most volume lies in a thin outer shell.
