> [!TLDR]
> Worked examples apply the partition function to rotation, energy fluctuations, and the Gibbs paradox.

## Example 4.1 — Classical rigid diatomic ideal gas

**Question.** A gas has ${\mechanical{N}}$ rigid diatomic molecules with three translational and two rotational degrees of freedom. Calculate its partition function, internal energy, and heat capacity.

**Solution.** The Hamiltonian separates:

$$
{\energy{\mathcal H}}={\energy{\mathcal H}}_{\mathrm{tr}}+{\energy{\mathcal H}}_{\mathrm{rot}},
\qquad \mathcal Z_1=\mathcal Z_1^{\mathrm{tr}}\mathcal Z_1^{\mathrm{rot}}.
$$

The translational factor is $\mathcal Z_1^{\mathrm{tr}}={\mechanical{V}}/{\signal{\lambda}}_{\mathrm{th}}^3$.

For a classical rigid rotor:

$$
\mathcal Z_1^{\mathrm{rot}}
=\frac1{{\energy{h}}^2}\int\mathrm d{\mechanical{\theta}}\,\mathrm d\phi\,\mathrm d{\mechanical{p}}_{\mechanical{\theta}}\,\mathrm d{\mechanical{p}}_\phi
\exp\left[-\frac{{\beta}}{2{\mechanical{I}}}\left({\mechanical{p}}_{\mechanical{\theta}}^2+\frac{{\mechanical{p}}_\phi^2}{\sin^2{\mechanical{\theta}}}\right)\right].
$$

The momentum integrations are Gaussian:

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-{\beta} {\mechanical{p}}_{\mechanical{\theta}}^2/(2{\mechanical{I}})}\,\mathrm d{\mechanical{p}}_{\mechanical{\theta}}
=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi {\mechanical{I}}}{{\beta}}},
$$

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-{\beta} {\mechanical{p}}_\phi^2/(2{\mechanical{I}}\sin^2{\mechanical{\theta}})}\,\mathrm d{\mechanical{p}}_\phi
=\sin{\mechanical{\theta}}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi {\mechanical{I}}}{{\beta}}}.
$$

The angular integrals give $\int_0^\pi\sin{\mechanical{\theta}}\,\mathrm d{\mechanical{\theta}}=2$ and $\int_0^{2\pi}\mathrm d\phi=2\pi$. Therefore:

$$
\mathcal Z_1^{\mathrm{rot}}
=\frac{8\pi^2{\mechanical{I}}}{{\beta} {\energy{h}}^2}
=\frac{2{\mechanical{I}}}{{\beta}{\energy{\hbar}}^2}
=\frac{2{\mechanical{I}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}{\thermal{T}}}{{\energy{\hbar}}^2}
=\frac {\thermal{T}}{{\thermal{\Theta}}_{\mathrm{rot}}},
\qquad {\thermal{\Theta}}_{\mathrm{rot}}=\frac{{\energy{\hbar}}^2}{2{\mechanical{I}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}}. \tag{4.18}
$$

Thus:

$$
\mathcal Z_1=\frac {\mechanical{V}}{{\signal{\lambda}}_{\mathrm{th}}^3}\frac {\thermal{T}}{{\thermal{\Theta}}_{\mathrm{rot}}},
\qquad \mathcal Z_N=\frac{\mathcal Z_1^{\mechanical{N}}}{{\mechanical{N}}!}.
$$

The energy per molecule is:

$$
{\energy{u}}=-\frac{\mathrm d\ln\mathcal Z_1}{\mathrm d{\beta}}
=\frac32{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}{\thermal{T}}+{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}{\thermal{T}}=\frac52{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}{\thermal{T}}.
$$

The total heat capacity is ${\thermal{C}_V}={\mechanical{N}}\,\partial {\energy{u}}/\partial {\thermal{T}}=5{\mechanical{N}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}/2$.

> [!note] Rotational symmetry
> The source uses a rotor symmetry number of one. For a homonuclear molecule, equivalent orientations and nuclear-spin statistics require additional care. The simple classical symmetry factor changes entropy but not this high-temperature heat capacity.

For a second treatment of the classical rotor and its quantum limits, see [David Tong, Statistical Physics — Diatomic Gas](https://www.damtp.cam.ac.uk/user/tong/statphys/statmechhtml/S2.html#S2.S4).

## Example 4.2 — Energy fluctuations of a monatomic ideal gas

**Question.** Calculate the energy variance and verify ${\energy{\sigma}}_E^2={\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}{\thermal{T}}^2{\thermal{C}_V}$.

**Solution.** From Eq. (4.11):

$$
\ln\mathcal Z_N
={\mechanical{N}}\ln\left(\frac {\mechanical{V}}{{\signal{\lambda}}_{\mathrm{th}}^3}\right)-\ln {\mechanical{N}}!
={\mechanical{N}}\ln {\mechanical{V}}+\frac{3{\mechanical{N}}}{2}\ln\left(\frac{2\pi {\mechanical{m}} {\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}{\thermal{T}}}{{\energy{h}}^2}\right)-\ln {\mechanical{N}}!.
$$

Equivalently, the beta-dependent part is $-(3{\mechanical{N}}/2)\ln{\beta}$. Dimensional logarithms here stand for their combined dimensionless expression above.

$$
\langle {\energy{E}}\rangle=-\frac{\partial\ln\mathcal Z_N}{\partial{\beta}}
=\frac{3{\mechanical{N}}}{2{\beta}},
\qquad
{\energy{\sigma}}_E^2=\frac{\partial^2\ln\mathcal Z_N}{\partial{\beta}^2}
=\frac{3{\mechanical{N}}}{2{\beta}^2}=\frac{3{\mechanical{N}}}{2}({\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}{\thermal{T}})^2.
$$

Since ${\thermal{C}_V}=3{\mechanical{N}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}/2$, this is exactly ${\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}{\thermal{T}}^2{\thermal{C}_V}$. The relative fluctuation is:

$$
\frac{{\energy{\sigma}}_E}{\langle {\energy{E}}\rangle}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3{\mechanical{N}}}}.
$$

For ${\mechanical{N}}=10^{23}$ it is about $2.6\times10^{-12}$, consistent with the source's order-of-magnitude value $10^{-12}$.

## Example 4.3 — Chemical potential and the Gibbs paradox

**Question.** Two identical monatomic ideal gases, each with ${\mechanical{N}}/2$ particles and volume ${\mechanical{V}}/2$, have the same temperature. Calculate the entropy change when their partition is removed.

**Without the Gibbs factor**, the volume-dependent entropy is ${\thermal{S}}_{\mathrm{dist}}={\mechanical{N}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}\ln {\mechanical{V}}$, plus temperature terms. Hence:

$$
{\thermal{S}}_i={\mechanical{N}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}\ln({\mechanical{V}}/2)+\text{temperature terms},
\qquad {\thermal{S}}_f={\mechanical{N}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}\ln {\mechanical{V}}+\text{temperature terms},
$$

$$
\Delta {\thermal{S}}={\mechanical{N}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}\ln2>0.
$$

This incorrect extensive mixing term for identical gases is the Gibbs paradox.

**With the Gibbs factor**, use Eq. (4.12):

$$
\begin{aligned}
{\thermal{S}}_i&=2\frac {\mechanical{N}}2{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}
\left[\ln\left(\frac{{\mechanical{V}}/2}{({\mechanical{N}}/2){\signal{\lambda}}_{\mathrm{th}}^3}\right)+\frac52\right]\\
&={\mechanical{N}}{\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}}\left[\ln\left(\frac {\mechanical{V}}{{\mechanical{N}}{\signal{\lambda}}_{\mathrm{th}}^3}\right)+\frac52\right]
={\thermal{S}}_f.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus $\Delta {\thermal{S}}=0$ to thermodynamic order. The Gibbs correction is essential for consistency.

> [!note] Finite-system qualification
> These expressions use Stirling's approximation and retain the extensive entropy. Exact finite-size counting with a fixed initial number in each half can leave a subextensive correction; it does not restore the spurious extensive mixing entropy.
