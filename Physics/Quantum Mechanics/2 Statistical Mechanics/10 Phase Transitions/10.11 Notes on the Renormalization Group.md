> [!TLDR]
> The renormalization group connects scale invariance, universality, and critical exponents.

Wilson developed the modern renormalization-group framework in the 1970s. It explains why systems with different microscopic structures can have the same critical behavior. It also gives a systematic method to calculate critical exponents.

The physical puzzle is why microscopic differences can disappear from long-distance critical behavior while a few differences, such as a field or temperature shift, remain decisive. The renormalization group follows how an effective equilibrium model changes when short-distance fluctuations are removed and the remaining system is viewed at a new scale. It does not describe the passage of physical time.

Read the coarse-graining idea first, then use fixed-point stability to separate relevant from irrelevant perturbations. Only after this distinction should eigenvalues be translated into critical exponents. The epsilon expansion supplies a controlled calculation close to the upper critical dimension, with a separate extrapolation issue in three dimensions. A shared fixed point can explain universal powers even when transition temperatures and observable amplitudes retain microscopic information.
