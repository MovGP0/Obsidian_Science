> [!TLDR]
> Water has a melting curve with a negative slope. Its triple point, critical point, ice phases, and density maximum give further constraints on its phase diagram.

## Question

**Exercise 10.1 — Pressure–temperature diagram of water.** Discuss the phase diagram of water. Explain how it differs from the generic diagram.

## Calculations and explanation

Before calculating, predict which phase pressure favors. At the same amount of substance the denser phase occupies less volume, so pressure favors liquid water over ordinary ice near the normal melting point. Use equilibrium coexistence and neglect interface effects. The result is local to the ice-I melting boundary; a higher-pressure ice phase can change the volume ordering.

### Melting curve

Ice has a lower mass density than liquid water. Use the molar mass ${\mechanical{M}}=18.015\times10^{-3}\,\unit{kg\,mol^{-1}}$ to convert the density difference into a molar volume difference:

$$
\Delta {\mechanical{v}}={\mechanical{M}}\left(\frac{1}{{\mechanical{\rho}}_{\mathrm{liq}}}-\frac{1}{{\mechanical{\rho}}_{\mathrm{ice}}}\right)
\approx18.015\times10^{-3}\left(\frac{1}{1000}-\frac{1}{917}\right)
\approx-1.63\times10^{-6}\,\unit{m^3\,mol^{-1}}.
$$

The negative sign means that melting reduces the volume. The Clausius–Clapeyron equation relates this change to the slope of the coexistence curve:

$$
\frac{d{\mechanical{P}}}{d{\thermal{T}}}=\frac{{\energy{L}}}{{\thermal{T}}\Delta {\mechanical{v}}}
\approx\frac{6012}{273.15(-1.63\times10^{-6})}
\approx-1.35\times10^7\,\unit{Pa\,K^{-1}}.
$$

Thus, an increase in pressure lowers the melting temperature near atmospheric pressure. The melting curve slopes to the left in a diagram with temperature on the horizontal axis.

### Characteristic points

- **Triple point:** ${\thermal{T}}_t=273.16\,\unit{K}$, ${\mechanical{P}}_t=611.73\,\unit{Pa}\approx6.12\,\unit{mbar}$. Ice, liquid water, and vapor coexist.
- **Critical point:** ${\thermal{T}}_c\approx647.1\,\unit{K}$, ${\mechanical{P}}_c\approx220.6\,\unit{bar}$. The liquid–vapor coexistence curve ends here.

### Further properties

Water has several crystalline ice phases, such as ice I, II, III, V, and VI. Additional phase boundaries occur at high pressure. A diagram with only one solid region cannot show them all.

At approximately $1\,\unit{atm}$, liquid water has its maximum density near $4\,\unit{^{\circ}C}$. This density maximum is not a phase transition. It is related to the hydrogen-bond structure of liquid water.

Check the dimensional chain: molar mass divided by mass density gives molar volume, and latent heat per mole divided by temperature and molar-volume change gives pressure per kelvin. The negative slope therefore follows from the density ordering, not from the choice of grams or kilograms. The reciprocal slope predicts a melting-temperature decrease under an increased pressure.

The density maximum inside the liquid region is a useful counterexample: an extremum of a smooth state property is not itself a phase boundary. To infer a boundary one needs coexistence or a thermodynamic nonanalyticity, not only a density extremum. The numerical inputs are the source benchmarks, so the calculation is a consistency estimate rather than a new measurement of water's phase diagram.

## Solution

The main difference from the generic diagram is the negative slope of the ice-I melting curve. Water also has several solid phases and a density maximum within its liquid phase.
