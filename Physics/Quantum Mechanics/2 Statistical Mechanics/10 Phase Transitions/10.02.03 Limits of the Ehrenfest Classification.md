> [!TLDR]
> The Ehrenfest classification does not describe all types of critical behavior.

A derivative label is useful when it connects to a measured discontinuity, but it cannot replace a model of collective behavior. Assume an equilibrium bulk limit before asking which derivative is singular. In a finite sample every narrow response peak can remain smooth, so a direct derivative count from a finite-resolution plot can misidentify the transition.

The original classification has several limits:

1. A divergent derivative does not fit the simple picture of a finite derivative jump.
2. The classification does not distinguish universality classes of continuous transitions.
3. The behavior of an order parameter is not determined by naming the order of a free-energy derivative.

For example, two continuous transitions can both have no latent heat while having different order-parameter symmetries and different critical exponents. The same historical order then gives no reason to equate their microscopic models or scaling laws. Conversely, a divergent derivative and a finite jump need not have the same limiting form even if both are placed under a broad continuous-transition label.

When using the classification on a new system, first ask whether a discontinuous phase change, a critical singularity, or a smooth crossover is present. Then ask what the order parameter and correlations do. This sequence separates an equilibrium classification from the physical explanation and from finite experimental resolution.

Modern descriptions use the presence or absence of latent heat, the continuity of the order parameter, symmetry, correlations, and critical scaling. See [[10.05.04 Temperature Dependence of the Order Parameter]] for the comparison of continuous behavior and a finite jump.
