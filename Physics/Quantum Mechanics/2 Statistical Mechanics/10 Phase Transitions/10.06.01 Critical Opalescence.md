> [!TLDR]
> Large density fluctuations scatter light strongly near the liquid–gas critical point.

Near the liquid–gas critical point, a fluid can become strongly turbid to visible light. This is **critical opalescence**, observed by Andrews in 1869. The correlation length of the density fluctuations increases strongly near the transition.

The scattered intensity is proportional to the static structure factor, apart from optical and normalization factors:

$$I(\mathbf {\signal{q}})\propto S(\mathbf {\signal{q}})=\int d^3{\mechanical{r}}\,e^{i\mathbf {\signal{q}}\cdot\mathbf {\mechanical{r}}}G(\mathbf {\mechanical{r}}).$$

Here:

$$G(\mathbf {\mechanical{r}})=\langle\delta{\mechanical{\rho}}(\mathbf {\mechanical{r}})\delta{\mechanical{\rho}}(0)\rangle.$$

At criticality, the correlation function has a power-law decay instead of an exponential decay. The long-wavelength structure factor is related to the compressibility:

$$S(0)\propto{\mechanical{\kappa}}_T.$$

As the compressibility increases, long-wavelength density fluctuations become large and light scattering becomes strong. In this article, $S(\mathbf {\signal{q}})$ denotes a structure factor, not thermodynamic entropy.
