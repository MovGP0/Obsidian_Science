> [!TLDR]
> The three coexistence curves meet at the triple point. The liquid–gas curve ends at the critical point.

The pressure–temperature diagram separates the stable phases of a pure substance. A coexistence curve contains states in which two phases are in equilibrium. All three curves meet at the triple point.

**Fig. 10.1** Generic phase diagram in the pressure–temperature plane. The sublimation, melting, and vaporization curves meet at the triple point. The vaporization curve ends at the critical point. The region at temperatures and pressures above that point contains a supercritical fluid. The curves are schematic; the axes have no numerical scale.

The generic melting curve has a positive slope. Water has a negative melting slope near atmospheric pressure because ice has a larger molar volume than liquid water. See [[10.03.03 Application - Melting Curve]].

> [!note] Critical-point condition
> A fluid is supercritical when both ${\thermal{T}}>{\thermal{T}}_c$ and ${\mechanical{P}}>{\mechanical{P}}_c$. Crossing only one of these values does not establish a supercritical state.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ${\thermal{T}}$ | $\unit{K}$ | Temperature | Absolute temperature of the system. |
| ${\mechanical{P}}$ | $\unit{Pa}$ | Pressure | Thermodynamic pressure. |
| ${\thermal{T}}_c$ | $\unit{K}$ | Critical temperature | Temperature of the stated phase transition. |
| ${\mechanical{P}}_c$ | $\unit{Pa}$ | Critical pressure | Pressure at the liquid–gas critical point. |
