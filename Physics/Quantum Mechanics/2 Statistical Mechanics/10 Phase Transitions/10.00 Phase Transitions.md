> [!TLDR]
> Phase transitions connect collective microscopic behavior with changes in the macroscopic state.

Phase transitions are central phenomena in condensed matter physics and statistical mechanics. Water freezes. A magnet loses its spontaneous magnetization when it is heated. An electron system can become superconducting below a critical temperature. Each change results from the collective organization of a large number of microscopic components.

This chapter develops the theory from observed behavior to the Ehrenfest classification and the Clausius–Clapeyron equation. It then introduces order parameters, critical points, critical exponents, Landau theory, and the renormalization group.

Use the chapter to answer two different questions. First, which phase minimizes the equilibrium thermodynamic potential under the imposed constraints? Second, how do fluctuations change when two phases become indistinguishable? Read the coexistence and phase-diagram sections for the first question. Read the order-parameter and correlation sections before using critical exponents for the second.

Landau theory and the van der Waals model then provide calculations whose assumptions can be tested. Both give a mean-field description: a smooth local average replaces part of the fluctuating environment. Compare their predictions with the scaling and renormalization-group sections to identify which results survive when fluctuations matter. A singular transition refers to an equilibrium thermodynamic limit; a finite sample generally rounds that singularity. The worked problems test the thermodynamic identities and model predictions, rather than supply new measurements.
