> [!TLDR]
> The relevant scaling exponents at a fixed point determine the critical exponents.

A critical fixed point is a description that reproduces itself after coarse graining and rescaling. Its stability answers a physical question: which small changes must be tuned away to retain critical behavior? Linearization applies only sufficiently close to the fixed point. Practical RG calculations often use energy divided by thermal energy as a dimensionless Hamiltonian; the schematic notation below requires a consistent normalization throughout.

A fixed point satisfies

$$
\mathcal H^*=\mathcal R_b[\mathcal H^*].
$$

Linearize the transformation near this point:

$$
\mathcal R_b[\mathcal H^*+\delta\mathcal H]
\simeq\mathcal H^*+\mathsf M_b\,\delta\mathcal H.
$$

The eigenvalues of the linear map are written as

$$
\lambda_i=b^{y_i}.
$$

- $y_i>0$: the perturbation grows. It is **relevant**.
- $y_i<0$: the perturbation decreases. It is **irrelevant**.
- $y_i=0$: the perturbation is **marginal**. Higher-order terms must be examined.

For the thermal and field scaling exponents $y_t$ and $y_h$,

$$
\nu=\frac{1}{y_t},\qquad \eta=d+2-2y_h.
$$

The first relation follows because the thermal perturbation grows as $b^{y_t}$, whereas the correlation length decreases by a factor $b$. The second follows from the scaling dimension of the order-parameter correlation function.

> [!note] Source terminology correction
> The matrix eigenvalues are $\lambda_i$. The quantities $y_i=\ln\lambda_i/\ln b$ are their scaling exponents. The source calls $y_t$ an eigenvalue; the distinction is necessary when a scale factor is specified.

For a scale factor greater than one, a positive scaling exponent gives a multiplier greater than one and amplifies that direction after repeated steps. A zero scaling exponent only makes the linear multiplier one; nonlinear terms can still cause a slow drift. Thus marginal does not mean exactly unchanged.

To reconstruct the thermal relation, rescale the correlation length down by the scale factor while the reduced temperature grows by that factor raised to its thermal scaling exponent. Consistency with a correlation length proportional to a negative temperature power requires the product of that exponent and the correlation-length exponent to be one. This derives the inverse relation shown. Thermal tuning alone reaches criticality only if other relevant directions, such as a symmetry-breaking field, are already fixed. These are model-flow statements, not measured exponent estimates.
