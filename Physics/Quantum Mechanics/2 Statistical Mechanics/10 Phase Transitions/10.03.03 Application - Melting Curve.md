> [!TLDR]
> The sign of the volume change determines whether pressure raises or lowers the melting temperature.

For melting, the solid and liquid molar volumes have similar magnitudes. Their difference is small:

$$\Delta {\mechanical{v}}={\mechanical{v}}_{\mathrm{liq}}-{\mechanical{v}}_{\mathrm{sol}}.$$

The heat of fusion is positive. Therefore:

- If $\Delta {\mechanical{v}}>0$, as for most materials, then $\mathrm d{\mechanical{P}}/\mathrm d{\thermal{T}}>0$. Increased pressure raises the melting temperature.
- If $\Delta {\mechanical{v}}<0$, as for water, bismuth, and gallium in the stated regimes, then $\mathrm d{\mechanical{P}}/\mathrm d{\thermal{T}}<0$. Increased pressure lowers the melting temperature.

For water near its normal melting point, the source uses ${\mechanical{\rho}}_{\mathrm{ice}}\approx917\,\unit{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and ${\mechanical{\rho}}_{\mathrm{water}}\approx1000\,\unit{kg\,m^{-3}}$.

## Example 10.2 — Melting curve of ice

At ${\thermal{T}}=273.15\,\unit{K}$, take the heat of fusion as $334\,\unit{J\,g^{-1}}$, or ${\energy{L}}=6012\,\unit{J\,mol^{-1}}$. Convert the density difference into a molar-volume difference:

$$\begin{aligned}
\Delta {\mechanical{v}}&={\mechanical{M}}_{\mathrm{H_2O}}\left(\frac1{{\mechanical{\rho}}_{\mathrm{liq}}}-\frac1{{\mechanical{\rho}}_{\mathrm{sol}}}\right)\\
&\approx-1.63\times10^{-6}\,\unit{m^3\,mol^{-1}}.
\end{aligned}$$

Then:

$$\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm d{\mechanical{P}}}{\mathrm d{\thermal{T}}}&=\frac{6012}{273.15(-1.63\times10^{-6})}\,\unit{Pa\,K^{-1}}\\
&\approx-1.35\times10^7\,\unit{Pa\,K^{-1}}\approx-133\,\unit{atm\,K^{-1}}.
\end{aligned}$$

To lower the melting point by about one kelvin requires a pressure increase of about 133 atmospheres in this local approximation. Pressure melting contributes only a small part of the effect in ordinary ice skating; surface melting and friction are more important. Pressure effects also matter in glacier motion.
