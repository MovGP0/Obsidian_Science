> [!TLDR]
> The Gibbs phase rule counts independent intensive variables in an equilibrium multiphase system.

**Theorem 10.1 — Gibbs phase rule.** For a system with $C$ independent chemical components and $\varphi$ phases in equilibrium, with no additional constraints, the number of thermodynamic degrees of freedom is:

$$\mathcal F=C-\varphi+2.$$

The degrees of freedom are independent intensive variables.

### Proof

Each phase has temperature, pressure, and $C-1$ independent mole fractions: $C+1$ variables per phase. For $\varphi$ phases there are initially $\varphi(C+1)$ variables.

Choose one reference phase. Equality of temperature, pressure, and the $C$ chemical potentials with each of the other phases supplies $(\varphi-1)(C+2)$ independent conditions. Thus:

$$\mathcal F=\varphi(C+1)-(\varphi-1)(C+2)=C-\varphi+2.$$

### Example 10.3 — Applications

1. A pure substance in one phase has $C=1$, $\varphi=1$, and $\mathcal F=2$. Temperature and pressure can be selected independently.
2. A pure substance with two coexisting phases has $\mathcal F=1$. Select temperature and the coexistence pressure is fixed.
3. A pure substance with three coexisting phases has $\mathcal F=0$. Temperature and pressure are fixed at the triple point.
4. The liquid–gas critical point of a pure substance is isolated in its phase diagram. It also satisfies the criticality conditions:

$$\left(\frac{\partial {\mechanical{P}}}{\partial {\mechanical{V}}}\right)_{T_c}=0,\qquad
\left(\frac{\partial^2{\mechanical{P}}}{\partial {\mechanical{V}}^2}\right)_{T_c}=0.$$

> [!NOTE] Critical-point counting
> The source applies the two-phase rule and then writes the informal count $1-2+1=0$. At the critical point, the two phases become identical, so the ordinary count for distinct coexisting phases needs special care. Use the equation of state and both criticality conditions to determine the critical temperature, pressure, and volume. The informal count is not a derivation of the Gibbs phase rule at criticality.
