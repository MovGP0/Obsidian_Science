> [!TLDR]
> Response functions can have a divergence, a jump, or a finite cusp at a transition.

A response function is defined only after its constraints are specified. For compressibility, a fixed amount of fluid is allowed to equilibrate with a heat reservoir while pressure changes. Holding temperature fixed is different from an insulated compression; the two procedures need not give the same response.

Response functions measure how a system changes when an external parameter changes. Examples are the isothermal compressibility, magnetic susceptibility, and heat capacity.

For a fluid near its critical point:

$$
{\mechanical{\kappa}}_T=-\frac1{\mechanical{V}}\left(\frac{\partial {\mechanical{V}}}{\partial {\mechanical{P}}}\right)_T\propto|t|^{-\gamma},\qquad t=\frac{{\thermal{T}}-{\thermal{T}}_c}{{\thermal{T}}_c}\longrightarrow0.
$$

The reduced temperature $t$ and critical exponent $\gamma$ are dimensionless. A positive $\gamma$ gives a divergent compressibility. The corresponding magnetic susceptibility has the same type of critical scaling in the appropriate universality class.

> [!NOTE] A singularity need not be infinite
> The source states generally that the response functions diverge. This requires qualification. A heat capacity can instead have a finite jump or a finite cusp. Helium-4 has a small negative heat-capacity exponent and a finite cusp at the lambda transition. See [[10.06.04 Specific Heat near the Lambda Transition]].

The minus sign makes the compressibility positive on a stable single-phase branch, since volume decreases when pressure increases. The relative volume factor removes dependence on sample amount, and leaves inverse-pressure units. The power law omits an amplitude with those units; the dimensionless reduced temperature does not supply them.

The critical limit must also follow the appropriate path, such as the critical isochore of a one-phase fluid. An arbitrary path through the two-phase region does not measure the same single-phase derivative. A proposed check is to compare slopes of equilibrated isotherms near and far from criticality. A growing compressibility corresponds to a flatter pressure–volume isotherm. No measured isotherms are included, and the scaling form is not intended far from the critical region.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ${\mechanical{\kappa}}$ | $\unit{Pa^{-1}}$ | Compressibility | Relative volume response to pressure. |

| ${\mechanical{P}}$ | $\unit{Pa}$ | Pressure | Thermodynamic pressure. |
