> [!TLDR]
> Phase transitions can change density, magnetization, electrical resistance, or fluid flow.

## Liquid–gas transition

The liquid–gas transition is a familiar first-order transition. Heat water at a constant pressure of one atmosphere. It reaches its boiling temperature, approximately $100\,\unit{^{\circ}C}$. Liquid and vapor then coexist. The water absorbs the latent heat of vaporization without a temperature increase:

$${\energy{L}}\approx2257\,\unit{J\,g^{-1}}.$$

The density changes discontinuously, with ${\mechanical{\rho}}_{\mathrm{liq}}\gg{\mechanical{\rho}}_{\mathrm{gas}}$. The density difference decreases as the pressure increases. It vanishes at the critical point. For water:

$${\thermal{T}}_c=647.1\,\unit{K},\qquad {\mechanical{P}}_c=220.6\,\unit{bar}.$$

Beyond the liquid–gas critical point, the two phases no longer have a distinct boundary. The fluid can enter the supercritical region.

## Ferromagnetism

A ferromagnetic material, such as iron, nickel, or cobalt, has spontaneous magnetization ${\magnetic{M}}\ne0$ below its Curie temperature. Above this temperature, the material is paramagnetic. Its magnetization is zero when no external field is present.

At zero field, the transition is continuous: the magnetization approaches zero continuously, while its temperature derivative can diverge. Representative Curie temperatures from the source are:

| Material | Curie temperature |
| --- | --- |
| Iron | $1043\,\unit{K}$ |
| Nickel | $628\,\unit{K}$ |
| Cobalt | $1388\,\unit{K}$ |

## Superconductivity

Some metals, including mercury, lead, and niobium, and some ceramic oxides, including YBCO and BSCCO, become superconducting below a critical temperature. Their electrical resistance vanishes. They also expel magnetic flux from their interior: the Meissner effect.

For a conventional type-I superconductor, the zero-field transition is continuous. A transition across the critical field at a nonzero temperature below the critical temperature is generally first order. A complex order parameter describes the superconducting condensate:

$${\energy{\Delta}}=|{\energy{\Delta}}|e^{i\varphi}.$$

> [!NOTE] Scope of the symmetry statement
> The source calls this spontaneous breaking of the U(1) gauge symmetry. The order parameter selects a condensate phase in the usual effective description. Local gauge redundancy is not an ordinary physical symmetry that can break in the same sense as spin reversal.

## Superfluidity

Liquid helium-4 becomes superfluid below its lambda-transition temperature, approximately $2.17\,\unit{K}$ at atmospheric pressure. It shows flow without viscous dissipation and quantized circulation. The name *lambda transition* refers to the shape of its heat-capacity curve; see [[10.06.04 Specific Heat near the Lambda Transition]].

The condensate order parameter is a complex wavefunction:

$${\mechanical{\Psi}}=|{\mechanical{\Psi}}|e^{i\varphi}.$$

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ${\energy{L}}$ | $\unit{J\,g^{-1}}$ | Specific latent heat | Heat absorbed per gram in the stated phase change. |
| ${\mechanical{\rho}}$ | $\unit{kg\,m^{-3}}$ | Mass density | Mass per volume in the stated phase. |
| ${\thermal{T}}_c$ | $\unit{K}$ | Critical temperature | Temperature of the stated phase transition. |
| ${\mechanical{P}}_c$ | $\unit{Pa}$ | Critical pressure | Pressure at the liquid–gas critical point. |
| ${\magnetic{M}}$ | $\unit{A\,m^{-1}}$ | Magnetization | Magnetic dipole moment per volume. |
| ${\energy{\Delta}}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Superconducting gap | Complex pairing amplitude with energy units. |
| ${\mechanical{\Psi}}$ | $\unit{m^{-3/2}}$ | Condensate amplitude | Complex field normalized so that its squared magnitude is number density. |
