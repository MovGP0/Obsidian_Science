> [!TLDR]
> Below the critical temperature, two magnetized equilibrium states are related by spin inversion, and domains can contain both orientations.

Below $\thermal{T}_{\mathrm c}$, there are two equilibrium states related by $\mathbb Z_2$ symmetry:

$$
\magnetic{m}=+\magnetic{m}_{\mathrm s}>0,
\qquad
\magnetic{m}=-\magnetic{m}_{\mathrm s}<0.
$$

These are the two homogeneous pure phases of the short-range ferromagnet at zero field, where an ordered phase exists. Each contains thermal fluctuations; a phase is not a single all-up or all-down configuration. An equal statistical mixture has zero mean magnetization without eliminating the order within either phase.

In a macroscopic system, a small positive field selects the state with positive magnetization. The source explains persistence after field removal by an energy barrier of order $N\Delta\energy{f}\to\infty$ in the thermodynamic limit. The two states are separated in the sense of ergodicity.

> [!note] Barrier scaling
> The expression $N\Delta\energy{f}$ describes a spatially uniform mean-field path between states. In a short-range lattice model, reversal can proceed through domain walls. Its barrier depends on the interfaces and boundary conditions, rather than always scaling with the volume.

Inhomogeneities or mixed boundary conditions can divide the system into **Weiss domains**. These are macroscopic regions with opposite signs of uniform magnetization. **Domain walls** separate the regions, with energy costs proportional to wall area.

For an elementary energy check, each ferromagnetic bond cut by a reversed region changes from energy $-\energy{J}$ to $+\energy{J}$. The zero-temperature cost is therefore $2\energy{J}$ per broken bond. This counts an interface, not every spin in the enclosed volume. At positive temperature, interface entropy also contributes to its free energy. The nearest-neighbour model alone does not supply a general theory of the domain patterns of real magnets, where additional interactions and boundaries can matter.

The equilibrium Hamiltonian also supplies no reversal time. A large barrier explains why a specified local dynamical rule can give slow reversal, but a quantitative persistence or hysteresis prediction requires that rule and its time scale. This separates the equilibrium phase statement from a claim about a measured relaxation process.

> [!note] Remark 11.5 (Mermin–Wagner theorem)
> For the short-range systems considered here, in one and two dimensions the Mermin–Wagner theorem (1966) excludes spontaneous breaking of a continuous spin symmetry at positive temperature. The Ising symmetry is discrete, so the theorem does not apply. The two-dimensional Ising model does have a positive critical temperature.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\thermal{T}$ | $\unit{K}$ | Temperature | The absolute temperature; a c subscript marks a critical value. |
| $\magnetic{m}$ | $1$ | Magnetization per spin | The mean normalized spin; subscripts identify particular or spontaneous values. |
| $\energy{h}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Field parameter | The applied field multiplied by the spin magnetic moment. |
| $N$ | $1$ | Spin count | The number of lattice sites or spins. |
| $\energy{f}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Free energy per spin | The Helmholtz free energy per spin; subscript 0 marks a reference value. |
| $\energy{J}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Exchange coupling | The energy scale of a nearest-neighbour bond. |
