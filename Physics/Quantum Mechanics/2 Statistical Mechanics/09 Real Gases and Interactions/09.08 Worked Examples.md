> [!TLDR]
> Four worked examples connect molecular models with virial corrections and liquid–vapor equilibrium.

## Example 9.1 — second virial coefficient of hard spheres

**Question.** Find the coefficient for molecules with diameter ${\mechanical{\sigma}}$ and potential

$$
{\energy{u}}({\mechanical{r}})=\begin{cases}+\infty,&{\mechanical{r}}<{\mechanical{\sigma}},\\0,&{\mechanical{r}}\ge{\mechanical{\sigma}}.\end{cases}
$$

**Calculation.** The Boltzmann factor is zero inside the core and one outside it. Thus

$$
f({\mechanical{r}})=\begin{cases}-1,&{\mechanical{r}}<{\mechanical{\sigma}},\\0,&{\mechanical{r}}\ge{\mechanical{\sigma}},\end{cases}
$$

and Eq. (9.11) becomes

$$
{\mechanical{B}}_2=-2\pi\int_0^{\mechanical{\sigma}}(-1){\mechanical{r}}^2\,d{\mechanical{r}}
=\frac{2\pi{\mechanical{\sigma}}^3}{3}=4{\mechanical{v}}_0={\mechanical{b}},\qquad {\mechanical{v}}_0=\frac{\pi{\mechanical{\sigma}}^3}{6}.
$$

**Interpretation.** The result is independent of temperature. It comes from the restriction on available positions. The positive coefficient raises the pressure relative to the ideal-gas value.

## Example 9.2 — critical point of nitrogen

**Question.** Use the molar parameters ${\energy{a}}_m=0.1370\,\unit{Pa\,m^6\,mol^{-2}}$ and ${\mechanical{b}}_m=3.87\times10^{-5}\,\unit{m^3\,mol^{-1}}$ to calculate the critical point.

**Calculation.** Use the molar form of the critical relations, with ${\thermal{R}}=8.314\,\unit{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$:

$$
{\thermal{T}}_c=\frac{8{\energy{a}}_m}{27{\thermal{R}}{\mechanical{b}}_m}
=\frac{8(0.1370)}{27(8.314)(3.87\times10^{-5})}\,\unit{K}
\approx126.3\,\unit{K},
$$

$$
{\mechanical{P}}_c=\frac{{\energy{a}}_m}{27{\mechanical{b}}_m^2}\approx3.39\times10^6\,\unit{Pa}\approx33.4\,\unit{atm},
$$

$$
{\mechanical{v}}_{m,c}=3{\mechanical{b}}_m=1.161\times10^{-4}\,\unit{m^3\,mol^{-1}}.
$$

The source gives experimental values of about 126.2 kelvin and 33.5 atmospheres. The model fits these two values well, but its compressibility factor

$$
{\mechanical{Z}}_c=\frac{{\mechanical{P}}_c{\mechanical{v}}_{m,c}}{{\thermal{R}}{\thermal{T}}_c}=\frac38=0.375
$$

differs from the source's experimental value of about 0.290. Agreement in critical pressure and temperature does not make the model exact.

## Example 9.3 — Maxwell construction at reduced temperature 0.85

**Question.** Find the coexistence pressure and liquid and vapor volumes.

Use ${\thermal{t}}={\thermal{T}}/{\thermal{T}}_c=0.85$, ${\mechanical{p}}={\mechanical{P}}/{\mechanical{P}}_c$, and ${\mechanical{\upsilon}}={\mechanical{V}}/{\mechanical{V}}_c$. Then

$$
{\mechanical{p}}({\mechanical{\upsilon}})=\frac{6.8}{3{\mechanical{\upsilon}}-1}-\frac3{{\mechanical{\upsilon}}^2}.
$$

**Calculation.** Solve the equal-pressure conditions and the equal-area condition together:

$$
{\mechanical{p}}({\mechanical{\upsilon}}_\ell)={\mechanical{p}}({\mechanical{\upsilon}}_g)={\mechanical{p}}_0,\qquad
\int_{\upsilon_\ell}^{{\mechanical{\upsilon}}_g}{\mechanical{p}}({\mechanical{\upsilon}})\,d{\mechanical{\upsilon}}={\mechanical{p}}_0({\mechanical{\upsilon}}_g-{\mechanical{\upsilon}}_\ell).
$$

An antiderivative is

$$
\frac{6.8}{3}\ln(3{\mechanical{\upsilon}}-1)+\frac3{\mechanical{\upsilon}}.
$$

Numerical solution gives

$$
{\mechanical{p}}_0\approx0.50449165,\qquad {\mechanical{\upsilon}}_\ell\approx0.55336046,\qquad {\mechanical{\upsilon}}_g\approx3.12763929.
$$

These are the smallest and largest of the three intersections with the isotherm. The liquid and vapor coexist at the same temperature, pressure, and chemical potential.

> [!note] Numerical refinement
> The source gives a pressure of 0.504 times the critical pressure and a vapor volume of 3.13 times the critical volume. These agree with the calculation to the stated precision. Its liquid-volume value of 0.554 is slightly high; the recalculated value rounds to 0.553 at three decimal places.

## Example 9.4 — virial correction for carbon dioxide

**Question.** At ${\thermal{T}}=400\,\unit{K}$ and ${\mechanical{P}}=10\,\unit{atm}\approx1.013\times10^6\,\unit{Pa}$, use the measured molar coefficient ${\mechanical{B}}_{2,m}=-62.7\,\unit{cm^3\,mol^{-1}}$. Compare it with the van der Waals prediction from ${\energy{a}}_m=0.3658\,\unit{Pa\,m^6\,mol^{-2}}$ and ${\mechanical{b}}_m=4.286\times10^{-5}\,\unit{m^3\,mol^{-1}}$.

**Calculation.** The ideal molar volume is

$$
{\mechanical{v}}_m^{\mathrm{id}}=\frac{{\thermal{R}}{\thermal{T}}}{{\mechanical{P}}}\approx3.283\times10^{-3}\,\unit{m^3\,mol^{-1}}.
$$

To first order, replace molar volume in the correction by this ideal value:

$$
{\mechanical{Z}}-1\approx\frac{{\mechanical{B}}_{2,m}{\mechanical{P}}}{{\thermal{R}}{\thermal{T}}}
=\frac{(-62.7\times10^{-6})(1.013\times10^6)}{8.314(400)}
\approx-0.0191.
$$

The compressibility factor is about 1.9 percent below one. The van der Waals model gives

$$
{\mechanical{B}}_{2,m}^{\mathrm{vdW}}={\mechanical{b}}_m-\frac{{\energy{a}}_m}{{\thermal{R}}{\thermal{T}}}
\approx-6.71\times10^{-5}\,\unit{m^3\,mol^{-1}}
=-67.1\,\unit{cm^3\,mol^{-1}}.
$$

The difference from the measured coefficient is about 7 percent. Attraction dominates the leading pressure correction under these conditions.
