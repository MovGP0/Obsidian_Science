> [!TLDR]
> Relativistic electron pressure produces a characteristic maximum white-dwarf mass.

At sufficiently high density, the electron Fermi momentum satisfies ${\mechanical{p}}_F\gg {\mechanical{m}}_e{\signal{c}}$. Then ${\energy{\varepsilon}}\approx{\energy{\hbar}} {\signal{c}}{\signal{k}}$ and the ground-state kinetic energy becomes

$$
{\energy{E}}_{\mathrm{kin}}^{\mathrm{UR}}=\frac34{\mechanical{N}}_e{\energy{\varepsilon}}_F^{\mathrm{UR}}
\propto{\energy{\hbar}} {\signal{c}}\frac{{\mechanical{N}}_e^{4/3}}{\mechanical{R}}.
$$

Both the relativistic kinetic and gravitational energies now scale as ${\mechanical{R}}^{-1}$:

$$
{\energy{E}}_{\mathrm{tot}}^{\mathrm{UR}}({\mechanical{R}})=\frac{{\mechanical{A}}'}{\mechanical{R}}-\frac B {\mechanical{R}}=\frac{{\mechanical{A}}'-B}{\mechanical{R}}.
$$

- If ${\mechanical{A}}'>B$, expansion lowers this asymptotic energy. There is no finite minimum within the purely ultrarelativistic model.
- If ${\mechanical{A}}'<B$, compression lowers it, which signals instability.
- If ${\mechanical{A}}'=B$, this leading approximation is independent of radius.

> [!note] Source clarification
> The source calls the equality case an unstable equilibrium. The leading expression alone instead gives marginal balance, with no preferred radius. Stability requires terms omitted from this approximation. A subcritical star can have a stable radius in the full, partly nonrelativistic model; the expansion result above does not mean that every such star expands without limit.

**Proposition 7.1 — Chandrasekhar mass, simplified derivation.** For composition with ${\mechanical{Z}}$ electrons per ${\mechanical{A}}$ nucleons,

$$
{\mechanical{N}}_e\approx\frac{{\mechanical{M}}}{{\mechanical{\mu}}_e {\mechanical{m}}_u},\qquad{\mechanical{\mu}}_e=\frac {\mechanical{A}}{\mechanical{Z}}.
$$

The source uses the proton mass in place of ${\mechanical{m}}_u$ for this estimate. The relativistic energy coefficient follows from

$$
{\energy{E}}_{\mathrm{kin}}^{\mathrm{UR}}\approx\frac{3{\energy{\hbar}} {\signal{c}}}{4}(3\pi^2)^{1/3}{\mechanical{N}}_e
\left(\frac{{\mechanical{N}}_e}{{\mechanical{V}}}\right)^{1/3}
=\frac{3{\energy{\hbar}} {\signal{c}}}{4}\frac{(3\pi^2)^{1/3}}{(4\pi/3)^{1/3}}
\frac{{\mechanical{N}}_e^{4/3}}{\mechanical{R}}.
$$

Thus ${\mechanical{A}}'\propto {\mechanical{N}}_e^{4/3}$, while $B\propto {\mechanical{G}}{\mechanical{M}}^2\propto {\mechanical{G}}{\mechanical{\mu}}_e^2{\mechanical{m}}_u^2{\mechanical{N}}_e^2$. The critical equality fixes a mass scale proportional to $({\energy{\hbar}} {\signal{c}}/{\mechanical{G}})^{3/2}/({\mechanical{\mu}}_e^2{\mechanical{m}}_u^2)$.

The full Newtonian calculation with the Lane–Emden solution for polytropic index three gives

$$
\boxed{{\mechanical{M}}_{\mathrm{Ch}}\approx\frac{5.83}{{\mechanical{\mu}}_e^2}{\mechanical{M}}_\odot.}
$$

For carbon or oxygen, ${\mechanical{\mu}}_e=2$, which gives about $1.46{\mechanical{M}}_\odot$. A commonly quoted value is about $1.44{\mechanical{M}}_\odot$. The precise physical limit depends on the assumptions and corrections used.

> [!note] Historical and physical scope
> The source quotes Chandrasekhar's early estimate near 0.91 solar masses and compares the modern scale with Type Ia supernova progenitors. These are source statements. The coefficient 5.83 comes from the stellar-structure solution, not from the uniform-density scaling argument above. Composition, Coulomb effects, relativity, rotation, and ignition can modify the fate of a real star.
