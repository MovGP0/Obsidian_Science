> [!TLDR]
> At zero temperature, the mean energy per particle is three fifths of the Fermi energy.

Every state below ${\energy{\varepsilon}}_F$ is occupied and every state above it is empty. Using ${\energy{g}}({\energy{\varepsilon}})=C{\energy{\varepsilon}}^{1/2}$ gives

$$
\frac{{\energy{E}}_0}{{\mechanical{V}}}=\int_0^{{\energy{\varepsilon}}_F}{\energy{\varepsilon}} {\energy{g}}({\energy{\varepsilon}})\,d{\energy{\varepsilon}}
=C\int_0^{{\energy{\varepsilon}}_F}{\energy{\varepsilon}}^{3/2}d{\energy{\varepsilon}}
=\frac25 C{\energy{\varepsilon}}_F^{5/2}.
$$

The particle density is

$$
{\mechanical{n}}=\int_0^{{\energy{\varepsilon}}_F}C{\energy{\varepsilon}}^{1/2}d{\energy{\varepsilon}}
=\frac23 C{\energy{\varepsilon}}_F^{3/2}.
$$

Divide the energy density by the particle density:

$$
\boxed{\frac{{\energy{E}}_0}{{\mechanical{N}}}=\frac35{\energy{\varepsilon}}_F.}
$$

The finite ground-state kinetic energy is a consequence of exclusion: all particles cannot occupy the lowest orbital state. Fig. 7.2 compares the density of occupied states at different temperatures.

**Fig. 7.2** Density of states and occupied density of states. At zero temperature, all states are filled up to εꜰ. At positive temperature, occupation extends above the Fermi energy and holes appear below it. Each finite-temperature chemical potential is determined at fixed particle density. The energy width of the transition is of order kBT.
