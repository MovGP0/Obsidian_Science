## Example 7.1 — Fermi energy of copper

Copper has mass density ${\mechanical{\rho}}=8960\,\unit{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and molar mass ${\mechanical{M}}_{\mathrm{mol}}=63.55\times10^{-3}\,\unit{kg\,mol^{-1}}$. Assume one conduction electron per atom. Calculate ${\signal{k}}_F,{\energy{\varepsilon}}_F,{\thermal{T}}_F,{\mechanical{v}}_F$.

The number density is

$$
{\mechanical{n}}=\frac{{\mechanical{\rho}} {\mechanical{N}}_A}{{\mechanical{M}}_{\mathrm{mol}}}
=\frac{8960\times6.022\times10^{23}}{63.55\times10^{-3}}
\approx8.49\times10^{28}\,\unit{m^{-3}}.
$$

For ${\mechanical{g}}_s=2$,

$$
{\signal{k}}_F=(3\pi^2{\mechanical{n}})^{1/3}\approx1.36\times10^{10}\,\unit{m^{-1}}.
$$

The characteristic energy is

$$
{\energy{\varepsilon}}_F=\frac{{\energy{\hbar}}^2{\signal{k}}_F^2}{2{\mechanical{m}}_e}
\approx\frac{(1.055\times10^{-34})^2(1.36\times10^{10})^2}{2\times9.109\times10^{-31}}
\approx1.13\times10^{-18}\,\unit{J}\approx7.04\,\unit{eV}.
$$

Then

$$
{\thermal{T}}_F=\frac{{\energy{\varepsilon}}_F}{{\thermal{k}}_B}\approx8.18\times10^4\,\unit{K},
\qquad
{\mechanical{v}}_F=\frac{{\energy{\hbar}} {\signal{k}}_F}{{\mechanical{m}}_e}\approx1.57\times10^6\,\unit{m\,s^{-1}}\approx0.0052{\signal{c}}.
$$

The electrons are nonrelativistic but strongly degenerate at room temperature: ${\thermal{T}}/{\thermal{T}}_F\approx300/81800\approx3.7\times10^{-3}$.

## Example 7.2 — Electronic heat capacity of copper

Calculate the free-electron Sommerfeld coefficient, then estimate the electronic heat capacity at 300 K and 4 K.

The coefficient per volume is

$$
{\thermal{\gamma}}_V=\frac{\pi^2}{2}\frac{{\mechanical{n}}{\thermal{k}}_B^2}{{\energy{\varepsilon}}_F}
\approx70.7\,\unit{J\,m^{-3}\,K^{-2}}.
$$

Use ${\mechanical{V}}_{\mathrm{mol}}={\mechanical{M}}_{\mathrm{mol}}/{\mechanical{\rho}}\approx7.09\times10^{-6}\,\unit{m^3\,mol^{-1}}$:

$$
{\thermal{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{free}}={\thermal{\gamma}}_V{\mechanical{V}}_{\mathrm{mol}}
\approx5.01\times10^{-4}\,\unit{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-2}}
=0.501\,\unit{mJ\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-2}}.
$$

The source's measured coefficient is ${\thermal{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{exp}}=0.695\,\unit{mJ\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-2}}$. Its ratio to the free-electron value is about 1.39, an effective density-of-states enhancement.

Using that measured coefficient at 300 K,

$$
{\thermal{C}}_{V,\mathrm{mol}}^{(e)}\approx0.695\times10^{-3}\times300
=0.209\,\unit{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}.
$$

The source compares this with the Dulong–Petit estimate $3{\thermal{R}}_{\mathrm{gas}}\approx24.9\,\unit{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ for the lattice. The electronic term is less than one percent of that estimate.

At 4 K,

$$
{\thermal{C}}_{V,\mathrm{mol}}^{(e)}=0.695\times10^{-3}\times4
=2.78\times10^{-3}\,\unit{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}},
$$

$$
{\thermal{C}}_{V,\mathrm{mol}}^{\mathrm{ph}}=\beta_{\mathrm{ph}}{\thermal{T}}^3
\approx0.046\times10^{-3}\times64
=2.94\times10^{-3}\,\unit{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}.
$$

The two terms are comparable near ${\thermal{T}}^*=\sqrt{0.695/0.046}\,\unit{K}\approx3.9\,\unit{K}$. At lower temperature the electronic term dominates.

## Example 7.3 — Iron and density-of-states enhancement

For iron, the source gives ${\thermal{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{exp}}=4.98\,\unit{mJ\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-2}}$, ${\mechanical{n}}\approx1.71\times10^{29}\,\unit{m^{-3}}$, and ${\mechanical{Z}}=2$ conduction electrons per atom. Estimate the enhancement relative to the free-electron model.

$$
{\signal{k}}_F=(3\pi^2{\mechanical{n}})^{1/3}\approx1.71\times10^{10}\,\unit{m^{-1}},
\qquad
{\energy{\varepsilon}}_F^{(0)}=\frac{{\energy{\hbar}}^2{\signal{k}}_F^2}{2{\mechanical{m}}_e}
\approx1.79\times10^{-18}\,\unit{J}\approx11.2\,\unit{eV}.
$$

This gives ${\thermal{T}}_F^{(0)}\approx1.30\times10^5\,\unit{K}$. The free molar coefficient is

$$
{\thermal{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{free}}=\frac{\pi^2}{2}\frac{{\mechanical{Z}}{\thermal{R}}_{\mathrm{gas}}}{{\thermal{T}}_F^{(0)}}
\approx0.632\,\unit{mJ\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-2}}.
$$

In a single-band mass interpretation,

$$
\frac{{\mechanical{m}}^*}{{\mechanical{m}}_e}\approx\frac{{\thermal{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{exp}}}{{\thermal{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{free}}}
=\frac{4.98}{0.632}\approx7.9.
$$

The large ratio reflects the limitations of a simple free-electron description of iron, including d-band structure, correlations, and magnetism. It is an effective estimate, not a unique band mass.
