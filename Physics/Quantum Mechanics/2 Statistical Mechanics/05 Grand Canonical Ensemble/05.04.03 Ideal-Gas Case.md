> [!TLDR]
> The ideal-gas particle-number variance equals its mean, as required for a Poisson distribution.

The ideal-gas law is ${\mechanical{P}}{\mechanical{V}}=\langle {\mechanical{N}}\rangle {\thermal{k}_{B}} {\thermal{T}}$. Hence ${\mechanical{\kappa}}_T=1/{\mechanical{P}}$. Equation (5.10) becomes:

$$
(\Delta {\mechanical{N}})^2=\langle {\mechanical{N}}\rangle.\tag{5.11}
$$

This is consistent with a Poisson distribution. The full distribution is derived in [[05.06.01 Classical Ideal Gas in the Grand Canonical Ensemble]].

**Example 5.1 — Fluctuations in a macroscopic gas.** One mole has mean particle number $\langle {\mechanical{N}}\rangle=6.022\times10^{23}$. Its relative standard deviation is:

$$
\frac{\Delta {\mechanical{N}}}{\langle {\mechanical{N}}\rangle}
=\frac1{\sqrt{6.022\times10^{23}}}\approx1.3\times10^{-12}.
$$

Such a small relative fluctuation cannot be resolved by an ordinary macroscopic instrument. This supports the thermodynamic use of mean values.

This is the classical dilute ideal gas. An ideal quantum gas can have number correlations from Bose or Fermi statistics even without interactions, so the word ideal alone does not guarantee Poisson counting. In the classical model, the variance equality is necessary but does not by itself prove the full probability law; the exponential grand sum supplies that stronger result in the linked derivation.

The one-mole estimate has an absolute standard deviation of order a trillion particles, despite its tiny relative value. This explains why large absolute fluctuations and reproducible macroscopic density are compatible. For a small observation volume with mean count near one, the relative fluctuation is large and individual count probabilities are more informative than a Gaussian approximation. A sealed container still has no total-count variance; the example concerns an exchangeable subsystem.
