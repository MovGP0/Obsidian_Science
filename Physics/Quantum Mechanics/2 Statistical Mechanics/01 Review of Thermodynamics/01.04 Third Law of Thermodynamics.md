> [!TLDR]
> The third law constrains entropy and heat capacities near absolute zero. A nondegenerate ground state has zero entropy, while residual degeneracy can give a nonzero limiting entropy.

## Theorem 1.6 — Nernst theorem, third law

As the absolute temperature approaches zero, the entropy of a system in internal equilibrium approaches a limiting value. For a pure system in a perfect crystalline state, the conventional zero-entropy statement is:

$$
\lim_{\thermal{T}\to0}\thermal{S}(\thermal{T})=0.
$$

Nernst formulated the theorem in 1906. Planck later refined its statement. It has important practical and conceptual consequences.

## Consequence 1 — Heat capacities vanish

As $\thermal{T}\to0$, the heat capacities approach zero. The entropy relation is:

$$
\thermal{S}(\thermal{T})=\thermal{S}(0)
+\int_0^{\thermal{T}}\frac{\thermal{C}(T')}{T'}\,\mathrm dT'.
$$

The integral must converge. This is incompatible with a nonzero constant heat capacity extending to absolute zero. In particular, it contradicts the classical Dulong–Petit prediction:

$$
\thermal{C}_V=3\mechanical{n}\thermal{R},
$$

at all temperatures. Quantum mechanics explains the low-temperature behavior, for example through the Debye model.

> [!note] Correction to the source — Heat-capacity limit
> The source states that convergence requires $\thermal{C}(\thermal{T})/\thermal{T}\to0$. That condition is too strong. A heat capacity proportional to temperature gives a finite constant ratio and still makes the entropy integral converge. The relevant condition is convergence of the integral, with heat capacity tending to zero in the usual regular cases. See [David Tong, Statistical Physics, section 4.5](https://www.damtp.cam.ac.uk/user/tong/statphys/statmechhtml/S4.html).

## Consequence 2 — Thermal expansion coefficients vanish

The Maxwell relations in [[01.06 Maxwell Relations|section 1.6]] give:

$$
\thermal{\alpha}_P
=\frac{1}{\mechanical{V}}\left(\frac{\partial\mechanical{V}}{\partial\thermal{T}}\right)_{\mechanical{P}}
=-\frac{1}{\mechanical{V}}\left(\frac{\partial\thermal{S}}{\partial\mechanical{P}}\right)_{\thermal{T}}
\xrightarrow{\thermal{T}\to0}0.
$$

For low-temperature solids in the Debye regime, experiments give $\thermal{\alpha}_P\propto\thermal{T}^{\,3}$, consistent with the third law.

## Consequence 3 — Absolute zero cannot be reached

No finite thermodynamic cooling process can bring a system exactly to $\thermal{T}=0$.

> [!note] Correction to the source — Cooling argument
> The source tries to explain unattainability by saying that, as heat capacity vanishes, a small amount of removed heat produces a smaller temperature decrease. This argument has the direction reversed. At fixed volume, the heat-capacity definition gives $\mathrm d\thermal{T}=\delta\thermal{Q}/\thermal{C}_V$, so a fixed small heat transfer produces a larger temperature change when the heat capacity is smaller. This local relation alone does not prove that absolute zero can or cannot be reached.

> [!note] Remark 1.2 — Ground-state degeneracy
> The third law is connected to quantum mechanics. If the ground-state degeneracy is $g_0$, the limiting entropy is $\thermal{S}(0)=\thermal{k}_{\mathrm B}\ln g_0$. A nondegenerate ground state has $g_0=1$ and therefore $\thermal{S}(0)=0$. Systems with residual degeneracy at zero temperature, such as isotope mixtures in the source example, can have nonzero residual entropy. This is compatible with the Nernst statement when that residual entropy does not change in reversible thermodynamic processes.
