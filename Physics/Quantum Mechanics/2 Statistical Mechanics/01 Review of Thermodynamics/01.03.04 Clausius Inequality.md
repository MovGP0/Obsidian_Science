> [!TLDR]
> The entropy change is at least the heat received divided by the reservoir temperature. Equality holds for a reversible process.

Apply this inequality to a closed system, with heat positive into it. The temperature in the heat-transfer term is that of the external reservoir at the time of exchange. It is not a guessed uniform temperature of a system that may be far from equilibrium. If several reservoirs supply heat at once, add one heat-over-temperature contribution for each reservoir.

For an irreversible cycle:

$$
\oint\frac{\delta\thermal{Q}}{\thermal{T}}<0.
$$

The corresponding differential inequality is:

$$
\mathrm d\thermal{S}\geq\frac{\delta\thermal{Q}}{\thermal{T}}.
$$

Equality holds for a reversible process. A strictly positive difference represents entropy production.

## Proposition 1.1 — Clausius inequality

For any process from state $A$ to state $B$:

$$
\thermal{S}_B-\thermal{S}_A
\geq\int_A^B\frac{\delta\thermal{Q}}{\thermal{T}}.
$$

Here, $\thermal{T}$ is the temperature of the reservoir with which heat is exchanged. The equality applies to reversible processes.

One way to reconstruct the endpoint inequality is to follow the actual path from A to B, then return from B to A on a reversible comparison path. Apply the cyclic inequality to this combined cycle. The return-path integral is the negative of the entropy change from A to B, which gives the displayed inequality.

For an insulated closed system the heat-transfer term vanishes, so entropy cannot decrease. For a reversible cycle the entropy returns to its original value and the cyclic integral is zero. An open system needs additional entropy transported by matter; omitting that contribution would test a different boundary.
