> [!TLDR]
> Entropy is defined through reversible heat transfer divided by temperature. The integral around any reversible cycle vanishes, so entropy differences depend only on the endpoints.

## Definition 1.8 — Entropy

For a system that undergoes a reversible process, **entropy** $\thermal{S}$ is defined by:

$$
\mathrm d\thermal{S}=\frac{\delta\thermal{Q}_{\mathrm{rev}}}{\thermal{T}}.
$$

The definition is consistent because the integral

$$
\int_A^B\frac{\delta\thermal{Q}_{\mathrm{rev}}}{\thermal{T}}
$$

is independent of the reversible path. Thus, entropy is a state function. The proof uses Carnot cycles.

Consider an arbitrary reversible cycle. Following Clausius, approximate it by a sequence of infinitesimal Carnot cycles. For a small cycle labelled $i$, the hot and cold isotherms have temperatures $\thermal{T}_i^{(H)}$ and $\thermal{T}_i^{(C)}$. Equation (1.4) gives:

$$
\frac{\delta\thermal{Q}_i^{(H)}}{\thermal{T}_i^{(H)}}
+\frac{\delta\thermal{Q}_i^{(C)}}{\thermal{T}_i^{(C)}}=0.
$$

Here, heat is signed as entering the system, so the cold-isotherm transfer is negative. This differs from the positive rejected-heat magnitude used in the efficiency formula.

Sum over the small cycles and take the limit. For every reversible cycle:

$$
\oint\frac{\delta\thermal{Q}_{\mathrm{rev}}}{\thermal{T}}=0.
$$

Two reversible paths from $A$ to $B$ can be joined by traversing the second path backwards. They form a reversible cycle, whose integral is zero. Their integrals must therefore agree. This is the step that changes a path-dependent heat transfer into a state-function difference after division by the local absolute temperature.

The reference value of entropy is not fixed by this construction; it gives differences between equilibrium states. The reversible path can be a calculation route even when the actual process is irreversible. For example, an ideal gas can expand freely in an insulated container and gain entropy despite receiving no heat. A reversible isothermal expansion between its endpoints gives that entropy change, as shown in [[01.08 Worked Examples|Example 1.2]].

As a units check, heat divided by temperature has entropy units, joules per kelvin. For reversible adiabatic changes the heat transfer is zero, so entropy is constant. The reverse implication for a general irreversible process does not follow without an entropy balance.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\thermal{S}$ | $\unit{J\,K^{-1}}$ | Entropy | State function defined through reversible heat transfer. |
| $\thermal{Q}_{\mathrm{rev}},\thermal{Q}_i^{(H)},\thermal{Q}_i^{(C)}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Reversible heat transfers | Signed heat supplied to the system in the complete or elementary cycles. |
