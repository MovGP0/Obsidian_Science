> [!TLDR]
> Four worked examples apply thermodynamics to a Carnot engine, free expansion, a van der Waals gas, and the pressure dependence of entropy.

## Example 1.1 — Carnot cycle with an ideal gas

Calculate the work and heat in all four processes for $\mechanical{n}$ moles of a monatomic ideal gas:

$$
\thermal{C}_V=\frac32\mechanical{n}\thermal{R},
\qquad
\thermal{\gamma}=\frac53,
\qquad
\thermal{T}_H>\thermal{T}_C.
$$

The states $A,B,C,D$ follow the cycle in [[01.03.02 Carnot Cycle and Carnot's Theorem|Fig. 1.1]]. Heat into the gas and work performed by the gas are positive.

### A → B — Isothermal expansion at the hot temperature

Ideal-gas internal energy depends only on temperature, so $\Delta\energy{U}_{AB}=0$. The first law gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\thermal{Q}_{AB}=\energy{W}_{AB}
&=\int_{\mechanical{V}_A}^{\mechanical{V}_B}\mechanical{P}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{V}\\
&=\int_{\mechanical{V}_A}^{\mechanical{V}_B}\frac{\mechanical{n}\thermal{R}\thermal{T}_H}{\mechanical{V}}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{V}\\
&=\mechanical{n}\thermal{R}\thermal{T}_H\ln\frac{\mechanical{V}_B}{\mechanical{V}_A}>0.
\end{aligned}
$$

The absorbed heat is therefore $\thermal{Q}_H=\thermal{Q}_{AB}$.

### B → C — Reversible adiabatic expansion

No heat is transferred, so $\delta\thermal{Q}=0$ and $\mathrm d\energy{U}=-\mechanical{P}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{V}$. Thus:

$$
\energy{W}_{BC}=-\Delta\energy{U}_{BC}
=-\thermal{C}_V(\thermal{T}_C-\thermal{T}_H)
=\thermal{C}_V(\thermal{T}_H-\thermal{T}_C)>0.
$$

For a reversible ideal-gas adiabat, $\thermal{T}\mechanical{V}^{\thermal{\gamma}-1}$ is constant:

$$
\thermal{T}_H\mechanical{V}_B^{\thermal{\gamma}-1}
=\thermal{T}_C\mechanical{V}_C^{\thermal{\gamma}-1}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{\mechanical{V}_C}{\mechanical{V}_B}
=\left(\frac{\thermal{T}_H}{\thermal{T}_C}\right)^{1/(\thermal{\gamma}-1)}.
$$

### C → D — Isothermal compression at the cold temperature

As in the hot isothermal step:

$$
\thermal{Q}_{CD}=\energy{W}_{CD}
=\mechanical{n}\thermal{R}\thermal{T}_C\ln\frac{\mechanical{V}_D}{\mechanical{V}_C}<0.
$$

The heat rejected to the surroundings has positive magnitude:

$$
\thermal{Q}_C=-\thermal{Q}_{CD}
=\mechanical{n}\thermal{R}\thermal{T}_C\ln\frac{\mechanical{V}_C}{\mechanical{V}_D}.
$$

### D → A — Adiabatic compression

$$
\thermal{Q}_{DA}=0,
\qquad
\energy{W}_{DA}=-\thermal{C}_V(\thermal{T}_H-\thermal{T}_C)<0.
$$

### Volume ratios

The second adiabat satisfies:

$$
\thermal{T}_C\mechanical{V}_D^{\thermal{\gamma}-1}
=\thermal{T}_H\mechanical{V}_A^{\thermal{\gamma}-1}.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\frac{\mechanical{V}_D}{\mechanical{V}_A}
=\left(\frac{\thermal{T}_H}{\thermal{T}_C}\right)^{1/(\thermal{\gamma}-1)}
=\frac{\mechanical{V}_C}{\mechanical{V}_B}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{\mechanical{V}_C}{\mechanical{V}_D}=\frac{\mechanical{V}_B}{\mechanical{V}_A}.
$$

This identity lets both isothermal heat transfers use the same logarithmic volume ratio.

### Work and efficiency

The adiabatic work contributions cancel over the complete cycle. The net work is:

$$
\energy{W}=\thermal{Q}_H-\thermal{Q}_C
=\mechanical{n}\thermal{R}(\thermal{T}_H-\thermal{T}_C)
\ln\frac{\mechanical{V}_B}{\mechanical{V}_A}.
$$

The efficiency is:

$$
\energy{\eta}=\frac{\energy{W}}{\thermal{Q}_H}
=\frac{\thermal{T}_H-\thermal{T}_C}{\thermal{T}_H}
=1-\frac{\thermal{T}_C}{\thermal{T}_H}.
$$

This confirms equation (1.4).

### Check of the second law

Entropy is a state function, so its change over the complete cycle is zero:

$$
\Delta\thermal{S}_{\mathrm{cycle}}
=\frac{\thermal{Q}_H}{\thermal{T}_H}-\frac{\thermal{Q}_C}{\thermal{T}_C}
=\mechanical{n}\thermal{R}\ln\frac{\mechanical{V}_B}{\mechanical{V}_A}
-\mechanical{n}\thermal{R}\ln\frac{\mechanical{V}_B}{\mechanical{V}_A}=0.
$$

## Example 1.2 — Joule free expansion

A container is divided into two parts by an impermeable partition. One part contains gas; the other is empty. Remove the partition abruptly. The gas expands into the vacuum, as shown in Fig. 1.2.

### Thermodynamic analysis

The system is the gas. Its walls are thermally insulated, so $\thermal{Q}=0$. The gas performs no work against an external pressure because it expands into a vacuum, so $\energy{W}=0$. The first law gives:

$$
\Delta\energy{U}=\thermal{Q}-\energy{W}=0.
$$

Internal energy remains constant. For an ideal gas, $\energy{U}$ depends only on temperature. Therefore $\Delta\thermal{T}=0$: ideal-gas free expansion produces neither heating nor cooling.

For a real gas, $\energy{U}=\energy{U}(\thermal{T},\mechanical{V})$. Its temperature response is given by the **Joule coefficient**:

$$
\left(\frac{\partial\thermal{T}}{\partial\mechanical{V}}\right)_{\energy{U}}
=-\frac{(\partial\energy{U}/\partial\mechanical{V})_{\thermal{T}}}{\thermal{C}_V}
=-\frac{\thermal{T}(\partial\mechanical{P}/\partial\thermal{T})_{\mechanical{V}}-\mechanical{P}}{\thermal{C}_V}.
$$

### van der Waals gas

Write the equation in terms of molar volume $\mechanical{v}=\mechanical{V}/\mechanical{n}$:

$$
\left(\mechanical{P}+\frac{\mechanical{a}}{\mechanical{v}^{\,2}}\right)
(\mechanical{v}-\mechanical{b})=\thermal{R}\thermal{T}.
$$

At fixed amount of matter:

$$
\left(\frac{\partial\mechanical{P}}{\partial\thermal{T}}\right)_{\mechanical{V}}
=\frac{\thermal{R}}{\mechanical{v}-\mechanical{b}}.
$$

Insert this into the internal-pressure equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left(\frac{\partial\energy{U}}{\partial\mechanical{V}}\right)_{\thermal{T}}
&=\thermal{T}\frac{\thermal{R}}{\mechanical{v}-\mechanical{b}}-\mechanical{P}\\
&=\frac{\thermal{R}\thermal{T}}{\mechanical{v}-\mechanical{b}}
-\frac{\thermal{R}\thermal{T}}{\mechanical{v}-\mechanical{b}}
+\frac{\mechanical{a}}{\mechanical{v}^{\,2}}
=\frac{\mechanical{a}}{\mechanical{v}^{\,2}}>0.
\end{aligned}
$$

The parameter $\mechanical{a}$ measures intermolecular attraction. At fixed temperature, increasing the volume separates molecules. Work against their attraction increases potential energy and therefore internal energy.

During free expansion, the internal energy is fixed. If molar volume increases from $\mechanical{v}_1$ to $\mechanical{v}_2$, integration for constant total heat capacity gives:

$$
\Delta\thermal{T}
=-\frac{\mechanical{a}\mechanical{n}}{\thermal{C}_V}
\left(\frac{1}{\mechanical{v}_1}-\frac{1}{\mechanical{v}_2}\right)<0.
$$

Thus, the van der Waals gas cools during free expansion. Its kinetic energy supplies the increase in intermolecular potential energy.

### Entropy change

Although internal energy is unchanged, the expansion is irreversible and entropy increases. To calculate the entropy difference, use an alternative reversible path between the same endpoint states.

For the ideal gas, the final and initial temperatures are equal. A reversible isothermal expansion therefore joins the same endpoints:

$$
\Delta\thermal{S}=\int\frac{\delta\thermal{Q}_{\mathrm{rev}}}{\thermal{T}}
=\frac{1}{\thermal{T}}\int_{\mechanical{V}_1}^{\mechanical{V}_2}\mechanical{P}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{V}
=\mechanical{n}\thermal{R}\ln\frac{\mechanical{V}_2}{\mechanical{V}_1}>0.
$$

The entropy increases as expected for an irreversible process. Energy is conserved, but its ability to produce useful work has decreased.

> [!note] Scope of the entropy calculation
> The isothermal comparison path and unchanged temperature apply to the ideal gas. The real van der Waals gas in the preceding calculation cools, so its entropy change must include that temperature change.

## Example 1.3 — van der Waals gas: potentials and instability

The van der Waals gas is a simple real-gas model. It includes both excluded molecular volume and attractive intermolecular interactions. For $\mechanical{n}$ moles:

$$
\left(\mechanical{P}+\frac{\mechanical{a}\mechanical{n}^{\,2}}{\mechanical{V}^{\,2}}\right)
(\mechanical{V}-\mechanical{n}\mechanical{b})=\mechanical{n}\thermal{R}\thermal{T}.
$$

Here, $\mechanical{a}>0$ describes attraction. The parameter $\mechanical{b}>0$ is an effective excluded volume per mole.

### Isotherms and mechanical instability

Solve for pressure:

$$
\mechanical{P}=\frac{\mechanical{n}\thermal{R}\thermal{T}}{\mechanical{V}-\mechanical{n}\mechanical{b}}
-\frac{\mechanical{a}\mechanical{n}^{\,2}}{\mechanical{V}^{\,2}}. \tag{1.10}
$$

**Fig. 1.3** van der Waals isotherms in reduced coordinates: $\mechanical{P}_r=\mechanical{P}/\mechanical{P}_c$, $\mechanical{V}_r=\mechanical{V}/\mechanical{V}_c$, and $\thermal{T}_r=\thermal{T}/\thermal{T}_c$. Above the critical temperature the gas is a single-phase fluid. Below it, the homogeneous equation of state has a van der Waals loop with an unstable portion where $(\partial\mechanical{P}/\partial\mechanical{V})_{\thermal{T}}>0$, bounded by the spinodal points. At the critical point, both the first and second volume derivatives of pressure vanish.

The plotted curves use the reduced equation $\mechanical{P}_r=8\thermal{T}_r/(3\mechanical{V}_r-1)-3/\mechanical{V}_r^{\,2}$. The critical point is at $(\mechanical{V}_r,\mechanical{P}_r)=(1,1)$.

At sufficiently low temperature, an isotherm has a local minimum and maximum, forming a van der Waals loop. In the portion where $(\partial\mechanical{P}/\partial\mechanical{V})_{\thermal{T}}>0$, mechanical stability fails. The homogeneous state is unstable and separates into liquid and gas phases.

### Critical point

At the critical point, the two phases become indistinguishable. The conditions are:

$$
\left(\frac{\partial\mechanical{P}}{\partial\mechanical{V}}\right)_{\thermal{T}_c}=0,
\qquad
\left(\frac{\partial^2\mechanical{P}}{\partial\mechanical{V}^{\,2}}\right)_{\thermal{T}_c}=0.
$$

Apply them to equation (1.10):

$$
\frac{\mechanical{n}\thermal{R}\thermal{T}_c}{(\mechanical{V}_c-\mechanical{n}\mechanical{b})^2}
=\frac{2\mechanical{a}\mechanical{n}^{\,2}}{\mechanical{V}_c^{\,3}},
\qquad
\frac{2\mechanical{n}\thermal{R}\thermal{T}_c}{(\mechanical{V}_c-\mechanical{n}\mechanical{b})^3}
=\frac{6\mechanical{a}\mechanical{n}^{\,2}}{\mechanical{V}_c^{\,4}}.
$$

Divide the equations to eliminate the common factors. This gives $\mechanical{V}_c-\mechanical{n}\mechanical{b}=2\mechanical{V}_c/3$, hence $\mechanical{V}_c/3=\mechanical{n}\mechanical{b}$. Substitute back:

$$
\thermal{T}_c=\frac{8\mechanical{a}}{27\mechanical{b}\thermal{R}},
\qquad
\mechanical{P}_c=\frac{\mechanical{a}}{27\mechanical{b}^{\,2}},
\qquad
\mechanical{V}_c=3\mechanical{n}\mechanical{b}.
$$

The **critical compressibility factor** is:

$$
Z_c=\frac{\mechanical{P}_c\mechanical{V}_c}{\mechanical{n}\thermal{R}\thermal{T}_c}
=\frac38=0.375.
$$

It is independent of the model parameters. This is an example of the law of corresponding states. The source quotes experimental values from about 0.23 for water to about 0.29 for noble gases. Thus, the model is qualitatively useful but not quantitatively accurate near the critical point.

### Helmholtz free energy

Integrate the internal-pressure result:

$$
\left(\frac{\partial\energy{U}}{\partial\mechanical{V}}\right)_{\thermal{T}}
=\frac{\mechanical{a}\mechanical{n}^{\,2}}{\mechanical{V}^{\,2}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\energy{U}(\thermal{T},\mechanical{V})
=\energy{U}_{\mathrm{id}}(\thermal{T})-\frac{\mechanical{a}\mechanical{n}^{\,2}}{\mechanical{V}}.
$$

The ideal-gas kinetic contribution is $\energy{U}_{\mathrm{id}}(\thermal{T})=\thermal{C}_V\thermal{T}$, where the total heat capacity is taken as constant. The source gives Helmholtz free energy as:

$$
\energy{F}(\thermal{T},\mechanical{V})
=-\mechanical{n}\thermal{R}\thermal{T}\ln(\mechanical{V}-\mechanical{n}\mechanical{b})
-\frac{\mechanical{a}\mechanical{n}^{\,2}}{\mechanical{V}}
-\thermal{C}_V\thermal{T}\ln\thermal{T}
+\energy{f}_0(\thermal{T},\mechanical{n}).
$$

The last term contains the volume-independent contributions, including reference terms and the entropy contribution $-\thermal{T}\thermal{s}_0(\mechanical{n})$.

Differentiate with respect to volume to recover equation (1.10). Differentiate with respect to temperature to obtain entropy:

$$
\thermal{S}
=-\left(\frac{\partial\energy{F}}{\partial\thermal{T}}\right)_{\mechanical{V}}
=\mechanical{n}\thermal{R}\ln(\mechanical{V}-\mechanical{n}\mechanical{b})
+\thermal{C}_V\ln\thermal{T}+\thermal{s}_0(\mechanical{n}).
$$

Compare this with the ideal-gas expression:

$$
\thermal{S}_{\mathrm{id}}
=\mechanical{n}\thermal{R}\ln\mechanical{V}
+\thermal{C}_V\ln\thermal{T}+\thermal{s}_0(\mechanical{n}).
$$

The free volume replaces total volume, as expected from the excluded-volume interpretation of the parameter.

> [!note] Reference constants in the source formulas
> Logarithms of dimensional variables are shorthand for logarithms of ratios to fixed reference scales. Their reference terms are included in $\energy{f}_0$ and $\thermal{s}_0$. To make the displayed energy and entropy formulas mutually consistent for constant $\thermal{C}_V$, the volume-independent term must satisfy $\energy{f}_0=\thermal{C}_V\thermal{T}-\thermal{T}\thermal{s}_0(\mechanical{n})$, up to the chosen reference terms. An arbitrary temperature dependence would not give the stated internal energy or entropy.

## Example 1.4 — Maxwell relation: pressure dependence of entropy

How does the entropy of a real fluid change when its pressure increases at fixed temperature? Maxwell relation (1.8) gives:

$$
\left(\frac{\partial\thermal{S}}{\partial\mechanical{P}}\right)_{\thermal{T}}
=-\left(\frac{\partial\mechanical{V}}{\partial\thermal{T}}\right)_{\mechanical{P}}
=-\mechanical{V}\thermal{\alpha}_P.
$$

Most fluids expand on heating, so $\thermal{\alpha}_P>0$. Their entropy therefore decreases under isothermal compression. Higher pressure confines the molecules to a smaller volume and reduces the number of available configurations.

### Ideal gas

For an ideal gas, $\thermal{\alpha}_P=1/\thermal{T}$. Thus:

$$
\left(\frac{\partial\thermal{S}}{\partial\mechanical{P}}\right)_{\thermal{T}}
=-\frac{\mechanical{V}}{\thermal{T}}
=-\frac{\mechanical{n}\thermal{R}}{\mechanical{P}}.
$$

Integrate at fixed temperature from reference pressure $\mechanical{P}_0$:

$$
\thermal{S}(\thermal{T},\mechanical{P})
=\thermal{S}(\thermal{T},\mechanical{P}_0)
-\mechanical{n}\thermal{R}\ln\frac{\mechanical{P}}{\mechanical{P}_0}.
$$

This is a standard gas-thermodynamics result.

### Water near 4 °C

At $\mechanical{P}=1\,\unit{atm}$, water has an unusual response. Its expansion coefficient changes sign near $4\,\unit{{}^{\circ}C}$: it is negative below this temperature and positive above it. Therefore, isothermal compression below about 4 degrees Celsius **increases** the entropy. This counterintuitive result reflects the special hydrogen-bond structure of water.
