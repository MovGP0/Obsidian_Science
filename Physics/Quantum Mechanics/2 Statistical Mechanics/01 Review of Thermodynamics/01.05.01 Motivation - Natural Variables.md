> [!TLDR]
> Internal energy naturally depends on entropy and volume. Legendre transformations replace these variables with quantities that are easier to control experimentally, without losing thermodynamic information.

For a one-component system with a fixed amount of matter, the fundamental relation (1.2) is:

$$
\mathrm d\energy{U}=\thermal{T}\,\mathrm d\thermal{S}
-\mechanical{P}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{V}.
$$

It shows that internal energy is naturally a function $\energy{U}(\thermal{S},\mechanical{V})$. If this function is known, thermodynamic information can be recovered by differentiation:

$$
\thermal{T}=\left(\frac{\partial\energy{U}}{\partial\thermal{S}}\right)_{\mechanical{V}},
\qquad
\mechanical{P}=-\left(\frac{\partial\energy{U}}{\partial\mechanical{V}}\right)_{\thermal{S}}.
$$

Entropy and volume are not always the most convenient variables. In the laboratory, temperature is usually controlled by contact with a thermostat. Pressure can be controlled by contact with a barostat. Entropy is generally not controlled directly.

The **Legendre transformation** gives functions with other natural variables without losing information.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\energy{U}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Internal energy | Potential with entropy and volume as natural variables. |
| $\thermal{S}$ | $\unit{J\,K^{-1}}$ | Entropy | Natural thermal variable of internal energy. |
| $\mechanical{V}$ | $\unit{m^3}$ | Volume | Natural mechanical variable of internal energy. |
| $\thermal{T}$ | $\unit{K}$ | Temperature | Derivative of internal energy with respect to entropy. |
| $\mechanical{P}$ | $\unit{Pa}$ | Pressure | Negative derivative of internal energy with respect to volume. |
