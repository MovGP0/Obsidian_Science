> [!TLDR]
> An equilibrium state has constant macroscopic properties and can be described by a finite set of state variables. Extensive variables scale with system size; intensive variables do not.

A thermodynamic system left undisturbed for a sufficiently long time reaches a state of **thermodynamic equilibrium**. In this state:

1. No macroscopic flow of energy or matter is observed within the system.
2. All measurable macroscopic properties remain constant with time.
3. A finite set of macroscopic quantities, called **state variables**, describes the system completely.

The third point is particularly important. A system with approximately $10^{23}$ molecules can be characterized by only three or four numbers. Statistical mechanics explains this empirical result.

## Definition 1.2 — Intensive and extensive variables

A state variable is **extensive** if it doubles when the amount of matter doubles under otherwise identical conditions. It is **intensive** if it remains unchanged in this operation.

Examples of extensive variables are volume $\mechanical{V}$, amount of substance $\mechanical{n}$ or particle number $\mechanical{N}$, internal energy $\energy{U}$, entropy $\thermal{S}$, enthalpy $\energy{H}$, Helmholtz free energy $\energy{F}$, and Gibbs energy $\energy{G}$.

Examples of intensive variables are pressure $\mechanical{P}$, temperature $\thermal{T}$, chemical potential $\energy{\mu}$, density $\mechanical{\rho}$, and molar concentration.

The ratio of two extensive quantities is intensive. Examples are energy density and molar entropy:

$$
\energy{u}=\frac{\energy{U}}{\mechanical{V}},
\qquad
\thermal{s}=\frac{\thermal{S}}{\mechanical{n}}.
$$

Such quantities are obtained by dividing an extensive quantity by volume or amount of substance. The source groups these as specific or molar quantities, depending on the denominator.

> [!note] Remark 1.1 — Limits of the classification
> The intensive/extensive distinction assumes a homogeneous system and surface interactions that are negligible compared with bulk interactions. These assumptions can fail for long-range interactions, such as gravity, or for nanoparticles with a large surface-to-volume ratio. Standard thermodynamics must then be adapted with care.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\mechanical{V}$ | $\unit{m^3}$ | Volume | Space occupied by the system. |
| $\mechanical{n}$ | $\unit{mol}$ | Amount of substance | Amount of matter measured in moles. |
| $\mechanical{N}$ | $1$ | Particle number | Number of particles in the system. |
| $\energy{U}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Internal energy | Microscopic energy of the system. |
| $\thermal{S}$ | $\unit{J\,K^{-1}}$ | Entropy | Extensive thermodynamic state variable. |
| $\energy{H}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Enthalpy | Thermodynamic energy potential. |
| $\energy{F}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Helmholtz free energy | Potential with temperature and volume as natural variables. |
| $\energy{G}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Gibbs energy | Potential with temperature and pressure as natural variables. |
| $\mechanical{P}$ | $\unit{Pa}$ | Pressure | Intensive mechanical state variable. |
| $\thermal{T}$ | $\unit{K}$ | Temperature | Intensive thermal state variable. |
| $\energy{\mu}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Chemical potential | Energy change per added particle under specified constraints. |
| $\mechanical{\rho}$ | $\unit{kg\,m^{-3}}$ | Mass density | Mass per unit volume. |
| — | $\unit{mol\,m^{-3}}$ | Molar concentration | Amount of substance per unit volume. |
| $\energy{u}$ | $\unit{J\,m^{-3}}$ | Energy density | Internal energy per unit volume. |
| $\thermal{s}$ | $\unit{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ | Molar entropy | Entropy per mole. |
