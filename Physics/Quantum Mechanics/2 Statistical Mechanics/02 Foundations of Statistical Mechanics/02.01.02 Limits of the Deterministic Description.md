> [!TLDR]
> In a chaotic system, a small initial uncertainty grows exponentially and limits the prediction of individual trajectories.

A many-particle system can be highly sensitive to its initial conditions. In a chaotic regime, a small separation between nearby trajectories grows approximately as:

$$
\delta({\signal{t}})\sim\delta(0)e^{{\signal{\lambda}} {\signal{t}}},
\qquad {\signal{\lambda}}>0.
$$

Here ${\signal{\lambda}}$ is a positive Lyapunov exponent. The time needed for the separation to reach a specified scale $\delta_{\max}$ is:

$$
{\signal{t}}^*={\signal{\lambda}}^{-1}\ln\!\left(\frac{\delta_{\max}}{\delta(0)}\right).
$$

After this time, the initial uncertainty is large enough to prevent a useful prediction of the microscopic trajectory. The source illustrates this with an initial uncertainty of order $10^{-30}$ and a Lyapunov time ${\signal{\lambda}}^{-1}\sim1\,\unit{ps}$, and describes a loss of predictability within a few nanoseconds.

> [!note] Scope and numerical clarification
> More than two particles does not by itself imply chaos. For example, noninteracting particles in a rectangular box form an integrable system. Also, the time estimate needs a dimensionless ratio of uncertainty scales. If that ratio is $10^{30}$, the stated Lyapunov time gives about $69\,\unit{ps}$, rather than a few nanoseconds. The value $10^{-30}$ alone is not a universal quantum uncertainty.

These arguments show why a statistical description is useful, and often necessary in practice, for complex many-particle systems.

> [!note] Remark 2.1
> The need for statistics does not imply that the microscopic laws are unknown. Statistical mechanics assumes these laws. It uses statistics when direct use of all their solutions is impractical, or when finite uncertainty prevents useful trajectory predictions.
