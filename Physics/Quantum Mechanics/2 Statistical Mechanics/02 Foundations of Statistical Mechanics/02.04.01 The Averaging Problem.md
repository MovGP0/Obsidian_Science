> [!TLDR]
> Experiments average over time, while statistical mechanics usually calculates an average over an ensemble.

A measurement of a macroscopic quantity takes a finite time ${\signal{\tau}}_{\mathrm{mis}}$. This interval is very long compared with a typical molecular collision time, ${\signal{\tau}}_{\mathrm{coll}}\sim10^{-13}\,\unit{s}$, but short on a human time scale.

The measured quantity is therefore a time average:

$$
\overline A
=\frac1{{\signal{\tau}}_{\mathrm{mis}}}
\int_0^{{\signal{\tau}}_{\mathrm{mis}}}A({\mechanical{q}}({\signal{t}}),{\mechanical{p}}({\signal{t}}))\,\mathrm d{\signal{t}}.
$$

Statistical mechanics instead gives an ensemble average:

$$
\langle A\rangle=\int_\Gamma A({\mechanical{q}},{\mechanical{p}})\rho({\mechanical{q}},{\mechanical{p}})\,\mathrm d\Gamma.
$$

Why should these two averages agree? This is the central question of the **ergodic problem**.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ${\mechanical{q}}$ | $\unit{m}$ | Position coordinate | A Cartesian coordinate; generalized coordinates can have different units. |
| ${\mechanical{p}}$ | $\unit{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$ | Momentum | Momentum conjugate to a Cartesian position coordinate. |
| ${\signal{t}}$ | $\unit{s}$ | Time | Elapsed time; starred or subscripted forms specify prediction or recurrence times. |
| ${\signal{\tau}}$ | $\unit{s}$ | Time interval | A measurement, collision, or averaging interval. |
| $\rho$ | $\unit{(J\,s)^{-3N}}$ | Phase-space probability density | Density relative to dΓ for N point particles in three dimensions. |
| $A$ | $—$ | Observable | A measurable quantity with context-dependent units; B in correlation formulas is a second observable. |
| $\mathrm d\Gamma$ | $\unit{(J\,s)^{3N}}$ | Phase-space volume element | The product of all coordinate and conjugate-momentum intervals in three dimensions. |
| ${\mechanical{N}}$ | $1$ | Particle or oscillator count | The number of particles or distinguishable oscillators. |
