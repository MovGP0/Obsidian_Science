> [!TLDR]
> A macroscopic gas has too many degrees of freedom for a useful calculation of all particle trajectories.

Consider one mole of an ideal gas. Its volume is approximately $22.4\,\unit{L}$ under the normal conditions used in the source. Its particle count is of the order set by the Avogadro constant:

$$
{\mechanical{N}}_A=6.022\times10^{23}\,\unit{mol^{-1}}.
$$

Each monatomic particle has three translational degrees of freedom. A diatomic molecule also has two rotational degrees of freedom when these modes are active. The total number is therefore:

$$
f_{\mathrm{tot}}=f{\mechanical{N}}\approx3\times6\times10^{23},
\qquad f=3\quad\text{for a monatomic gas}.
$$

A complete deterministic description requires $6{\mechanical{N}}$ initial values for positions and momenta, followed by the integration of $6{\mechanical{N}}$ first-order Hamilton equations. For ${\mechanical{N}}\sim10^{23}$, this is computationally impractical. It also gives much more information than a macroscopic experiment needs.

We want quantities such as pressure, temperature, and internal energy. These quantities result from averages over a very large number of degrees of freedom. Statistical mechanics establishes this connection.

> [!note] Normal conditions
> The approximate molar volume of 22.4 litres refers to an ideal gas near 0 °C and one atmosphere. It depends on the specified reference pressure.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ${\mechanical{N}}$ | $1$ | Particle or oscillator count | The number of particles or distinguishable oscillators. |
| ${\mechanical{N}}_A$ | $\unit{mol^{-1}}$ | Avogadro constant | The number of particles per mole. |
| $f$ | $1$ | Degrees of freedom | The number per particle; the subscript tot denotes the total. |
