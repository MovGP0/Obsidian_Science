> [!TLDR]
> The Maxwell–Boltzmann approximation drops the ±1 terms when the exponential denominator is large.

The classical limit requires

$$
e^{-{\thermal{\beta}}({\energy{\varepsilon}}_\alpha-{\energy{\mu}})}\ll1
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad e^{{\thermal{\beta}}({\energy{\varepsilon}}_\alpha-{\energy{\mu}})}\gg1.\tag{6.5}
$$

Then

$$
{\mechanical{n}}_{\mathrm{FD},\mathrm{BE}}({\energy{\varepsilon}}_\alpha)
=\frac1{e^{{\thermal{\beta}}({\energy{\varepsilon}}_\alpha-{\energy{\mu}})}\pm1}
\simeq e^{-{\thermal{\beta}}({\energy{\varepsilon}}_\alpha-{\energy{\mu}})}
\equiv {\mechanical{n}}_{\mathrm{MB}}({\energy{\varepsilon}}_\alpha).
$$

**Definition 6.2 — Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution.**

$$
{\mechanical{n}}_{\mathrm{MB}}({\energy{\varepsilon}})=e^{{\thermal{\beta}}{\energy{\mu}}}e^{-{\thermal{\beta}}{\energy{\varepsilon}}}
={\mechanical{z}} e^{-{\thermal{\beta}}{\energy{\varepsilon}}},\qquad {\mechanical{z}}=e^{{\thermal{\beta}}{\energy{\mu}}}.
$$

Here ${\mechanical{z}}$ is the fugacity. As a mathematical expression, ${\mechanical{n}}_{\mathrm{MB}}$ has no upper bound. Its use as an approximation to a quantum gas requires small occupations. Values of order one or larger are outside that approximation.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ${\mechanical{n}}_\alpha$ | $\unit{1}$ | Occupation number | Number or mean number of particles in one state. |
| ${\energy{\varepsilon}}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Single-particle energy | Energy of one single-particle state. |
| ${\energy{\mu}}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Chemical potential | Controls the mean particle number. |
| ${\thermal{\beta}}$ | $\unit{J^{-1}}$ | Inverse temperature | Equal to $1/(k_BT)$. |
| ${\mechanical{z}}$ | $\unit{1}$ | Fugacity | Equal to $e^{{\thermal{\beta}}{\energy{\mu}}}$. |
