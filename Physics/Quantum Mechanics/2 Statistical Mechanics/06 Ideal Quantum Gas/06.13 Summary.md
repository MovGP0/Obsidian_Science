> [!TLDR]
> Exchange symmetry and state counting determine the ideal quantum-gas distributions and their classical limit.

The chapter develops quantum statistics through these steps:

1. Indistinguishability requires label-invariant observables. This chapter uses symmetric bosonic or antisymmetric fermionic sectors as physical input. Under its relativistic assumptions, the spin–statistics theorem connects the choice to spin.
2. Occupation numbers and Fock space provide the natural basis for variable particle number.
3. The ideal-gas grand partition function factorizes over single-particle states.
4. Fermi–Dirac occupations are ${\mechanical{n}}_{\mathrm{FD}}=[e^{{\thermal{\beta}}({\energy{\varepsilon}}-{\energy{\mu}})}+1]^{-1}$. At zero temperature, they form a step.
5. Bose–Einstein occupations are ${\mechanical{n}}_{\mathrm{BE}}=[e^{{\thermal{\beta}}({\energy{\varepsilon}}-{\energy{\mu}})}-1]^{-1}$. They require ${\energy{\mu}}<{\energy{\varepsilon}}_0$ at finite size and positive temperature. A macroscopic ground population can develop in the appropriate thermodynamic limit.
6. Maxwell–Boltzmann occupations are ${\mechanical{n}}_{\mathrm{MB}}=e^{-{\thermal{\beta}}({\energy{\varepsilon}}-{\energy{\mu}})}$. They approximate both quantum laws when relevant occupations are small. In three dimensions, ${\mechanical{n}}{\signal{\lambda}}_T^3/{\mechanical{g}}_s\ll1$ is the dilute criterion.
7. For a quadratic dispersion, ${\energy{g}}({\energy{\varepsilon}})\propto{\energy{\varepsilon}}^{d/2-1}$; in three dimensions it grows as $\sqrt{\energy{\varepsilon}}$.

The next chapters apply these results to the degenerate Fermi gas, the Sommerfeld model of metals, Bose–Einstein condensation, blackbody radiation, and the Debye solid.

To reconstruct the calculation for a new gas, identify the complete states and dispersion first. Choose the occupation law and its allowed chemical potential, then form the particle-number and energy sums. Use an integral only after checking level spacing and any separate ground-state contribution. Finally test the dilute limit or the zero-temperature filling limit.

A short self-check: can a two-state system hold three identical particles? Yes for bosons, with four number-basis configurations; no for fermions if the two states already include spin. This tests the state-counting input before any thermodynamic limit is used.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ${\mechanical{n}}_\alpha$ | $\unit{1}$ | Occupation number | Number or mean number of particles in one state. |
| ${\energy{\varepsilon}}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Single-particle energy | Energy of one single-particle state. |
| ${\energy{\mu}}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Chemical potential | Controls the mean particle number. |
| ${\thermal{\beta}}$ | $\unit{J^{-1}}$ | Inverse temperature | Equal to $1/(k_B{\thermal{T}})$. |
| ${\mechanical{n}}$ | $\unit{m^{-3}}$ | Number density | Number of particles per volume. |
| ${\signal{\lambda}}_T$ | $\unit{m}$ | Thermal de Broglie wavelength | Thermal quantum length for a particle of mass m. |
| ${\mechanical{g}}_s$ | $\unit{1}$ | Spin degeneracy | Number of internal spin states at a given orbital state. |
| ${\energy{g}}({\energy{\varepsilon}})$ | $\unit{J^{-1}}$ | Density of states | Total number of states per energy interval, including internal degeneracy. |
| ${\thermal{T}}$ | $\unit{K}$ | Temperature | Absolute temperature of the system. |
