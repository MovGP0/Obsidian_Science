> [!TLDR]
> An exchange has sign +1 for a symmetric wavefunction and −1 for an antisymmetric wavefunction.

For ${\mechanical{N}}$ identical particles, write the state as

$$
{\signal{\Psi}}({\mechanical{\mathbf r}}_1,\sigma_1;{\mechanical{\mathbf r}}_2,\sigma_2;\ldots;{\mechanical{\mathbf r}}_N,\sigma_N).
$$

Here ${\mechanical{\mathbf r}}_i$ is a position and $\sigma_i$ is a spin label. Use $\xi_i=({\mechanical{\mathbf r}}_i,\sigma_i)$ for the combined argument. Let $\hat P_{ij}$ exchange $\xi_i$ and $\xi_j$.

The exchanged state must represent the same physical ray. Within the exchange sectors considered here,

$$
\hat P_{ij}{\signal{\Psi}}=e^{i\varphi}{\signal{\Psi}}.
$$

A second exchange restores the original arguments: $\hat P_{ij}^2=\hat I$. Thus

$$
{\signal{\Psi}}=\hat P_{ij}^2{\signal{\Psi}}=e^{2i\varphi}{\signal{\Psi}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad e^{2i\varphi}=1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad e^{i\varphi}=\pm1.
$$

- Sign $+1$: the wavefunction is **symmetric** under exchange.
- Sign $-1$: the wavefunction is **antisymmetric** under exchange.

> [!note] Dimensional scope
> This chapter uses the usual bosonic and fermionic sectors in three spatial dimensions. In two dimensions, exchange paths have a different topology. Anyons can have more general exchange phases, or matrix exchange rules. An anyon exchange is a braid; the argument $\hat P_{ij}^2=\hat I$ cannot be applied to that braid unchanged. The source mentions anyons and the fractional quantum Hall effect but does not develop them.
