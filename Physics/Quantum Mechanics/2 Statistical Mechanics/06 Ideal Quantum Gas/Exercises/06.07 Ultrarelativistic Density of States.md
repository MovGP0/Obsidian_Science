> [!TLDR]
> A linear relativistic dispersion gives a three-dimensional density of states proportional to energy squared and PV = U/3.

## Question — Exercise 6.7

For an ultrarelativistic particle with ${\energy{\varepsilon}}={\energy{\hbar}} {\signal{c}}{\signal{k}}$ (for example, an idealized massless neutrino), calculate the three-dimensional density of states and show

$$
g_{\mathrm{rel}}({\energy{\varepsilon}})=\frac{{\mechanical{g}}_s{\mechanical{V}}}{2\pi^2({\energy{\hbar}} {\signal{c}})^3}\,{\energy{\varepsilon}}^2.
$$

Compare with the nonrelativistic result and discuss the equation of state.

## Worked calculation

This derivation is added to the supplied question. The factor ${\mechanical{g}}_s$ counts the internal or helicity states actually included; do not assign $2s+1$ automatically to a massless species.

### 1. Count wave-vector shells

The geometry of the state grid is unchanged:

$$
d\mathcal N={\mechanical{g}}_s\frac {\mechanical{V}}{(2\pi)^3}\,4\pi {\signal{k}}^2d{\signal{k}}
=\frac{{\mechanical{g}}_s{\mechanical{V}}}{2\pi^2}{\signal{k}}^2d{\signal{k}}.
$$

### 2. Change from wavenumber to energy

For the linear dispersion,

$$
{\signal{k}}=\frac{{\energy{\varepsilon}}}{{\energy{\hbar}} {\signal{c}}},\qquad d{\signal{k}}=\frac{d{\energy{\varepsilon}}}{{\energy{\hbar}} {\signal{c}}}.
$$

Therefore

$$
d\mathcal N=\frac{{\mechanical{g}}_s{\mechanical{V}}}{2\pi^2}\frac{{\energy{\varepsilon}}^2}{({\energy{\hbar}} {\signal{c}})^3}d{\energy{\varepsilon}},
\qquad
\boxed{g_{\mathrm{rel}}({\energy{\varepsilon}})=\frac{{\mechanical{g}}_s{\mechanical{V}}{\energy{\varepsilon}}^2}{2\pi^2({\energy{\hbar}} {\signal{c}})^3}.}
$$

The power is two, rather than the nonrelativistic power $1/2$. This change comes from the dispersion, not from a change in state-grid geometry.

### 3. Derive the equation of state

Write $g_{\mathrm{rel}}({\energy{\varepsilon}})=B{\energy{\varepsilon}}^2$ and $q({\energy{\varepsilon}})=\ln\Xi_\alpha({\energy{\varepsilon}})$. As in Exercise 6.1, $q'=-{\thermal{\beta}} {\mechanical{n}}({\energy{\varepsilon}})$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
{\mechanical{P}}{\mechanical{V}}&={\thermal{k}}_B{\thermal{T}}B\int_0^\infty{\energy{\varepsilon}}^2q({\energy{\varepsilon}})\,d{\energy{\varepsilon}}\\
&={\thermal{k}}_B{\thermal{T}}B\left[\frac{{\energy{\varepsilon}}^3}{3}q({\energy{\varepsilon}})\right]_0^\infty
+\frac{{\thermal{k}}_B{\thermal{T}}{\thermal{\beta}} B}{3}\int_0^\infty{\energy{\varepsilon}}^3{\mechanical{n}}({\energy{\varepsilon}})\,d{\energy{\varepsilon}}\\
&=\frac13{\energy{U}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The boundary terms vanish for the equilibrium distributions. Thus

$$
\boxed{{\mechanical{P}}=\frac{{\energy{U}}}{3{\mechanical{V}}}=\frac {\energy{u}}3.}
$$

Compare with ${\mechanical{P}}=2{\energy{U}}/(3{\mechanical{V}})$ for a nonrelativistic gas. In a dilute ultrarelativistic gas, $\langle{\energy{\varepsilon}}\rangle=3{\thermal{k}}_B{\thermal{T}}$, so both relations still give ${\mechanical{P}}{\mechanical{V}}={\mechanical{N}}{\thermal{k}}_B{\thermal{T}}$ in the appropriate classical limit. For photons, ${\energy{\mu}}=0$ and number is not independently fixed; the pressure is determined by temperature.
