> [!TLDR]
> For a finite grand canonical Bose gas at positive temperature, the chemical potential must remain below the ground-state energy.

## Chemical-potential constraint

Nonnegative occupations and convergence of Eq. (6.3) require

$$
e^{{\thermal{\beta}}({\energy{\varepsilon}}_\alpha-{\energy{\mu}})}>1
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad{\energy{\mu}}<{\energy{\varepsilon}}_\alpha
\quad\text{for every state }\alpha.
$$

If the ground-state energy is ${\energy{\varepsilon}}_0$, then

$$
{\energy{\mu}}<{\energy{\varepsilon}}_0.
$$

As ${\energy{\mu}}\to{\energy{\varepsilon}}_0^-$, the mean ground-state occupation diverges. This permits a macroscopic ground-state population.

## Unbounded occupation

Unlike fermions, bosons have no upper occupation limit:

$$
\lim_{\mu\to\varepsilon_\alpha^-}{\mechanical{n}}_{\mathrm{BE}}({\energy{\varepsilon}}_\alpha)=+\infty.
$$

> [!note] Condensation and the allowed limit
> The physical constraint applies to the lowest state, not to an arbitrarily selected excited state. An excited-state limit ${\energy{\mu}}\to{\energy{\varepsilon}}_\alpha^-$ would violate ${\energy{\mu}}<{\energy{\varepsilon}}_0$ first. A divergence of one mathematical occupation is not by itself proof of a finite-temperature phase transition. Bose–Einstein condensation also requires a thermodynamic limit and a finite capacity of the excited states. For a uniform, nonrelativistic ideal gas this occurs in three dimensions, but not at positive temperature in one or two dimensions. The condensation chapter is [[08.02 Bose–Einstein Condensation]].

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ${\mechanical{n}}_\alpha$ | $\unit{1}$ | Occupation number | Number or mean number of particles in one state. |
| ${\energy{\varepsilon}}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Single-particle energy | Energy of one single-particle state. |
| ${\energy{\mu}}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Chemical potential | Controls the mean particle number. |
| ${\thermal{\beta}}$ | $\unit{J^{-1}}$ | Inverse temperature | Equal to $1/(k_B{\thermal{T}})$. |
| ${\thermal{T}}$ | $\unit{K}$ | Temperature | Absolute temperature of the system. |
