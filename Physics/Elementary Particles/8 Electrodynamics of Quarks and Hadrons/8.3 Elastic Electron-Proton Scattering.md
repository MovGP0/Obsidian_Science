> [!TLDR]
> Two proton form factors describe elastic electron–proton scattering through the Rosenbluth cross-section formula.

<!-- PDF page 270; printed page 262 -->

We now turn to electron-proton scattering, our best probe of the internal structure of the proton. If the proton were a simple point charge, obeying the Dirac equation, we could just copy our analysis of electron-muon scattering, with $\mechanical{M}$ now the mass of the proton. The lowest-order Feynman diagram would be

<!-- PDF page 272; printed page 264 -->

![[elementary-particles-elastic-proton-point-vertex.svg|Elastic electron–proton scattering with a point-proton vertex.]]

and the (spin-averaged) amplitude would be [eq. (7.124)]

$$
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle=
\frac{\electric{g_e}^4}{\mechanical{q}^4}
L_{\mathrm{electron}}^{\mu\nu}L_{\mu\nu\,\mathrm{proton}}
\tag{8.12}
$$

where $\mechanical{q}=\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3}$ and [eq. (7.125)]

$$
L_{\mathrm{electron}}^{\mu\nu}
=2\{\mechanical{p_1^\mu}\mechanical{p_3^\nu}
+\mechanical{p_1^\nu}\mechanical{p_3^\mu}
+g^{\mu\nu}[(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})^2
-(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_3})]\}
\tag{8.13}
$$

(and a similar expression for $L_{\mathrm{proton}}^{\mu\nu}$, only with $\mechanical{m}\to\mechanical{M}$ and $1,3\to2,4$). We used these results in Example 7.7 to derive the Mott and Rutherford scattering formulas.

But the proton is *not* a simple point charge, and so, long before the advent of the quark model, a more flexible formalism was introduced for describing electron-proton scattering. We might represent the process, in lowest-order QED, by a diagram like this:

![[elementary-particles-elastic-proton-structured-vertex.svg|Elastic scattering with an unknown photon–proton vertex.]]

where the blob on the right serves to remind us that we don't really know how the (virtual) photon interacts with the proton. (We *do* assume, however, that the scattering is *elastic*: $e+p\to e+p$; we shall consider *inelastic* processes, $e+p\to e+X$, in the next section.) Now, the essential point is that the *electron* vertex and the *photon* propagator are *unchanged*, and therefore, since $\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle$ neatly factors [eq. (8.12)],

$$
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle=
\frac{\electric{g_e}^4}{\mechanical{q}^4}
L_{\mathrm{electron}}^{\mu\nu}K_{\mu\nu\,\mathrm{proton}}
\tag{8.14}
$$

where $K_{\mu\nu}$ is an unknown quantity describing the photon-proton vertex.

Well … not *completely* unknown, for this much we can say: it is certainly a second-rank tensor, and the only variables that it can possibly depend on are $\mechanical{p_2}$, $\mechanical{p_4}$, and $\mechanical{q}$. Since $\mechanical{q}=\mechanical{p_4}-\mechanical{p_2}$, these three are not independent, and we are free to use any two of them; the customary choice is $\mechanical{q}$ and $\mechanical{p_2}$ (I'll drop the subscript from here on: $\mechanical{p}\equiv\mechanical{p_2}$ is the initial proton momentum). Now, there aren't many tensors that can be *constructed* out of just two four-vectors; the most general possible form is

$$
K_{\mathrm{proton}}^{\mu\nu}
=-K_1g^{\mu\nu}
+\frac{K_2}{(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})^2}\mechanical{p^\mu}\mechanical{p^\nu}
+\frac{K_4}{(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})^2}\mechanical{q^\mu}\mechanical{q^\nu}
+\frac{K_5}{(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})^2}
(\mechanical{p^\mu}\mechanical{q^\nu}+\mechanical{p^\nu}\mechanical{q^\mu})
\tag{8.15}
$$

<!-- PDF page 273; printed page 265 -->

where the $K_i$ are (unknown) functions of the only scalar variable in the problem: $\mechanical{q}^2$. [Notice that $\mechanical{p}^2=(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})^2$ is a constant, and $\mechanical{q}\cdot\mechanical{p}=-\mechanical{q}^2/2$]. The factors $(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})^{-2}$ have been pulled out, in defining $K_2$, $K_4$, and $K_5$, just so all the $K$'s will have the same dimensions.[^ep-8-3-subscript] In principle, we could add an antisymmetric combination $(\mechanical{p^\mu}\mechanical{q^\nu}-\mechanical{p^\nu}\mechanical{q^\mu})$, but since $L^{\mu\nu}$ is symmetric (8.13), such a term would contribute nothing to $\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle$. Now, these four functions are not independent; it can be shown (Problem 8.4) that

$$
\mechanical{q_\mu}K^{\mu\nu}=1
\tag{8.16}
$$

from which it follows (Problem 8.5) that

$$
K_4=\frac{(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})^2}{\mechanical{q}^2}K_1
+\frac14K_2
\quad\text{and}\quad K_5=\frac12K_2
\tag{8.17}
$$

Thus $K^{\mu\nu}$ can be expressed in terms of just *two* (unknown) functions, $K_1(\mechanical{q}^2)$ and $K_2(\mechanical{q}^2)$:

$$
K_{\mathrm{proton}}^{\mu\nu}
=K_1\left(-g^{\mu\nu}
+\frac{\mechanical{q^\mu}\mechanical{q^\nu}}{\mechanical{q}^2}\right)
+\frac{K_2}{(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})^2}
\left(\mechanical{p^\mu}+\frac12\mechanical{q^\mu}\right)
\left(\mechanical{p^\nu}+\frac12\mechanical{q^\nu}\right)
\tag{8.18}
$$

A fundamental problem for any theory of proton structure is to determine these two functions. They are easy enough to measure experimentally, for they are directly related to the electron-proton elastic scattering cross section. According to equations (8.13) and (8.18), (Problem 8.7)

$$
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle
=\left(\frac{2\electric{g_e}^2}{\mechanical{q}^2}\right)^2
\left\{K_1[(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_3})
-2(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})^2]
+K_2\left[\frac{(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p})
(\mechanical{p_3}\cdot\mechanical{p})}{(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})^2}
+\frac{\mechanical{q}^2}4\right]\right\}
\tag{8.19}
$$

We shall work in the laboratory frame, with the target proton at rest, $\mechanical{p}=(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c},0,0,0)$. An electron with incident energy $\energy{E}$ scatters at an angle $\mechanical{\theta}$, emerging with energy $\energy{E'}$. Let us assume it's a moderately energetic collision ($\energy{E},\energy{E'}\gg\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2$, so that we can safely ignore the mass of the electron (set $\mechanical{m}=0$);[^ep-8-3-regimes] then $\mechanical{p_1}=\frac{\energy{E}}{\mechanical{c}}(1,\mechanical{\hat{\mathbf p}_i})$ and $\mechanical{p_3}=\frac{\energy{E'}}{\mechanical{c}}(1,\mechanical{\hat{\mathbf p}_f})$, with $\mechanical{\hat{\mathbf p}_i}\cdot\mechanical{\hat{\mathbf p}_f}=\cos\mechanical{\theta}$, and we find (Problem 8.8)

$$
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle
=\frac{\electric{g_e}^4\mechanical{c}^2}
{4\energy{E}\energy{E'}\sin^4(\mechanical{\theta}/2)}
\left(2K_1\sin^2\frac{\mechanical{\theta}}2
+K_2\cos^2\frac{\mechanical{\theta}}2\right)
\tag{8.20}
$$

The outgoing electron energy, $\energy{E'}$, is not an independent variable; it is kinematically determined by $\energy{E}$ and $\mechanical{\theta}$ (Problem 8.9):

$$
\energy{E'}=\frac{\energy{E}}
{1+\frac{2\energy{E}}{\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c}^2}\sin^2(\mechanical{\theta}/2)}
\tag{8.21}
$$

<!-- PDF page 274; printed page 266 -->

For a massless incident particle we have (Problem 6.10)

$$
\frac{d\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\mechanical{\Omega}}
=\left(\frac{\energy{\hbar}\energy{E'}}
{8\pi\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c}\energy{E}}\right)^2
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle
\tag{8.22}
$$

and so, for elastic electron-proton scattering

$$
\frac{d\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\mechanical{\Omega}}
=\left(\frac{\electric{\alpha}\energy{\hbar}}
{4\mechanical{M}\energy{E}\sin^2(\mechanical{\theta}/2)}\right)^2
\frac{\energy{E'}}{\energy{E}}
[2K_1\sin^2(\mechanical{\theta}/2)+K_2\cos^2(\mechanical{\theta}/2)]
\tag{8.23}
$$

where $\energy{E'}$ is given by equation (8.21). This is known as the *Rosenbluth formula*; it was first derived in 1950.<sup>1</sup> By counting the number of electrons scattered in a given direction, for a range of incident energies, we can determine the “form factors” $K_1(\mechanical{q}^2)$ and $K_2(\mechanical{q}^2)$, and compare the results with theoretical predictions<sup>2</sup> (see Fig. 8.4).

[^ep-8-3-subscript]: The subscript 3 is traditionally reserved for a term that enters in the corresponding analysis of *neutrino*-proton scattering, but does not occur here.

[^ep-8-3-regimes]: The Mott formula (7.127) neglects proton structure and proton recoil; it applies to the regime $\energy{E}\ll\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c}^2$, but it does not assume $\energy{E}\gg\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2$. We now work in the regime $\energy{E}\gg\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2$, but do *not* ignore proton structure and recoil (i.e., we do *not* assume $\energy{E}\ll\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c}^2$). In the intermediate range, $\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2\ll\energy{E}\ll\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c}^2$, the two results agree. (See Problem 8.10.)
