> [!TLDR]
> Hadron production in electron–positron collisions gives jets and a cross-section ratio that supports three quark colors.

<!-- PDF page 266; printed page 258 -->

Electrons and positrons do not participate in the strong interactions; at present energies, the only way an $e^+e^-$ collision can produce strongly interacting particles is through a virtual photon: $e^++e^-\to\gamma\to q+\bar q\to\text{hadrons}$. (By 1987 the Linear Collider at SLAC should be operating at energies high enough to produce virtual $Z^0$'s copiously; the dominant mechanism will then be the *weak* interaction: $e^++e^-\to Z^0\to q+\bar q\to\text{hadrons}$.) For a brief moment the quarks fly apart as free particles, but when they reach a separation distance of around $10^{-15}\unit{\mathrm{m}}$ (the diameter of a hadron), their (strong) interaction is so great that new quark-antiquark pairs are produced—this time mainly from gluons:

![[elementary-particles-hadronization-gluon-pairs.svg|Gluon production of additional quark–antiquark pairs during hadronization.]]

These quarks and antiquarks, literally *dozens* of them in a typical modern experiment, join together in myriad combinations to make the mesons and baryons that are actually recorded at the detector. In all the debris there is one unmistakable footprint left behind by the original quark-antiquark pair: the hadrons emerge in two back-to-back “jets,” one along the direction of the primordial quark, the other marking the direction of the antiquark (Fig. 8.1). [Occasionally one sees a three-jet event (Fig. 8.2), indicating that a gluon carrying a substantial fraction of the total energy was emitted in conjunction with the original $q\bar q$ production:

Now, the *first* stage in this process ($e^++e^-\to\gamma\to q+\bar q$) is ordinary QED; the calculation is exactly the same as for $e^++e^-\to\gamma\to\mu^++\mu^-$:

![[elementary-particles-hadron-production-momenta.svg|Four-momentum labels for the pair-production diagram.]]

The amplitude is

$$
\mathcal M=\frac{\electric{Q}\electric{g_e}^2}{(\mechanical{p_1}+\mechanical{p_2})^2}
[\signal{\bar v}(\mechanical{p_2})\gamma^\mu\signal{u}(\mechanical{p_1})]
[\signal{\bar u}(\mechanical{p_3})\gamma_\mu\signal{v}(\mechanical{p_4})]
\tag{8.1}
$$

where $\electric{Q}$ is the quark charge, in units of $\electric{e}$ ($\frac23$, for $u$, $c$, and $t$; $-\frac13$ for $d$, $s$, and $b$). Exploiting Casimir's trick, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle={}&\frac14
\left[\frac{\electric{Q}\electric{g_e}^2}{(\mechanical{p_1}+\mechanical{p_2})^2}\right]^2
\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^\mu(\not{\mechanical{p_1}}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})
\gamma^\nu(\not{\mechanical{p_2}}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})]\\
&\times\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^\mu(\not{\mechanical{p_4}}-\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})
\gamma^\nu(\not{\mechanical{p_3}}+\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})]
\end{aligned}
\tag{8.2}
$$

where $\mechanical{m}$ is the mass of the electron and $\mechanical{M}$ is that of the quark. (See Problem 8.1.) Invoking the trace theorems of Chapter 7, we can reduce this to

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle={}&8
\left[\frac{\electric{Q}\electric{g_e}^2}{(\mechanical{p_1}+\mechanical{p_2})^2}\right]^2
[(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_3})(\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_4})
+(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_4})(\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_3})\\
&+(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})^2(\mechanical{p_3}\cdot\mechanical{p_4})
+(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})^2(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2})
+2(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})^2(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})^2].
\end{aligned}
\tag{8.3}
$$

<!-- PDF page 269; printed page 261 -->

Or, in terms of the incident (*CM*) electron energy $\energy{E}$ and the angle $\mechanical{\theta}$ between the incoming electron and the outgoing quark:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle=\electric{Q}^2\electric{g_e}^4
\biggl\{1&+\left(\frac{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2}{\energy{E}}\right)^2
+\left(\frac{\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c}^2}{\energy{E}}\right)^2\\
&+\left[1-\left(\frac{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2}{\energy{E}}\right)^2\right]
\left[1-\left(\frac{\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c}^2}{\energy{E}}\right)^2\right]
\cos^2\mechanical{\theta}\biggr\}
\end{aligned}
\tag{8.4}
$$

The differential scattering cross section is given by equation (6.42); integrating over $\mechanical{\theta}$ and $\mechanical{\phi}$, we obtain the total cross section (Problem 8.2):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{\sigma}={}&\frac{\pi\electric{Q}^2}{3}
\left(\frac{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}\electric{\alpha}}{\energy{E}}\right)^2
\sqrt{\frac{1-(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c}^2/\energy{E})^2}
{1-(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2/\energy{E})^2}}\\
&\times\left[1+\frac12\left(\frac{\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c}^2}{\energy{E}}\right)^2\right]
\left[1+\frac12\left(\frac{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2}{\energy{E}}\right)^2\right]
\end{aligned}
\tag{8.5}
$$

Notice the threshold at $\energy{E}=\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c}^2$; for energies less than this the square root is imaginary, reflecting the fact that the process is kinematically forbidden when there is not enough energy to create the $q\bar q$ pair. If we are substantially above threshold ($\energy{E}>\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c}^2\gg\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2$), equation (8.5) simplifies considerably:[^ep-8-2-approximation]

$$
\mechanical{\sigma}=\frac{\pi}{3}
\left(\frac{\energy{\hbar}\electric{Q}\mechanical{c}\electric{\alpha}}{\energy{E}}\right)^2
\tag{8.6}
$$

As we crank up the beam energy, we encounter a succession of such thresholds—first the muon and the light quarks, later (at about $1500\unit{\mathrm{MeV}}$) the charm quark, the tau (at $1784\unit{\mathrm{MeV}}$), the bottom quark ($4700\unit{\mathrm{MeV}}$) and eventually (one hopes) the top quark. There is a beautiful way to display this structure: suppose we examine the *ratio* of the rate of hadron production to that for muon pairs:

$$
R\equiv\frac{\mechanical{\sigma}(e^+e^-\to\text{hadrons})}
{\mechanical{\sigma}(e^+e^-\to\mu^+\mu^-)}
\tag{8.7}
$$

Since the numerator includes all the quark-antiquark events,[^ep-8-2-tau] equation (8.6) gives

$$
R(\energy{E})=3\sum\electric{Q_i}^2
\tag{8.8}
$$

in which the sum is over all quark flavors with thresholds below $\energy{E}$. Notice the 3 in front—it records the fact that there are three colors for each flavor. We anticipate a “staircase” graph for $R(\energy{E})$, then, ascending one step at each new

<!-- PDF page 270; printed page 262 -->

quark threshold, with the height of the rise determined by the quark's charge. At low energy where only the $u$, $d$, and $s$ quarks contribute, we expect

$$
R=3[(\tfrac23)^2+(-\tfrac13)^2+(-\tfrac13)^2]=2
\tag{8.9}
$$

Between the $c$ threshold and the $b$ threshold we should have

$$
R=2+3(\tfrac23)^2=\tfrac{10}{3}
\tag{8.10}
$$

and above the $b$

$$
R=\tfrac{10}{3}+3(-\tfrac13)^2=\tfrac{11}{3}
\tag{8.11}
$$

The experimental results are shown in Figure 8.3. (The top quark should produce a jump to $R=5$; evidently its mass exceeds $18\unit{\mathrm{GeV}}/\mechanical{c}^2$, since no such rise is apparent in these data.) The agreement between theory and experiment is pretty good, especially at high energy. But you may well ask why it is not *perfect*. Apart from the approximation in going from equation (8.5) to equation (8.6) (which artificially sharpens the corners at each threshold), and the neglect of the tau (which leads to an underestimate in the region above $1784\unit{\mathrm{GeV}}$), we have made a fundamental oversimplification in assuming that we could treat the process as a sequence of two independent operations: $e^+e^-\to q\bar q$ (QED) followed by $q\bar q\to\text{hadrons}$ (QCD). In point of fact, the quarks produced in the first step are *not* free particles, obeying the Dirac equation; rather, they are *virtual* particles, on their way to a second interaction. This is particularly critical when the energy is right for formation of a bound state ($\phi=s\bar s$, $\psi=c\bar c$, $\Upsilon=b\bar b$); in the vicinity of such a “resonance” the interaction of the two quarks can scarcely be ignored. Hence the sharp spikes in the graph, which typically occur just below each threshold. In the broad plateau regions, however, the flatness of the graph is an indication that our naive picture is not too bad. Like the jet structure, this is a manifestation of asymptotic freedom, and not surprisingly it works better at high energies.

But, really, all this is quibbling anyway, for the importance of Figure 8.3 lies not in what the small discrepancies whisper, but in what the overall agreement shouts: the factor of 3 in equation (8.8) clearly belongs there. Without it the theory would be wildly off (look at the dashed line in Figure 8.3)—and not just as isolated resonances, but across-the-board. That 3, remember, counts the number of colors. Here, then, is compelling *experimental* evidence for the color hypothesis—a hypothesis that was introduced originally for esoteric theoretical reasons but is now, of course, an indispensable ingredient in the whole story of strong interactions.

[^ep-8-2-approximation]: This approximation is actually better than it looks, because of a lucky algebraic cancellation: expanding the radical, $\sqrt{1-(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c}^2/\energy{E})^2}[1+\frac12(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c}^2/\energy{E})^2]=1-\frac38(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c}^2/\energy{E})^4\cdots$, so the error is of order $(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c}^2/\energy{E})^4$, not $(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c}^2/\energy{E})^2$. As for the *electron* mass terms, these are smaller to begin with, though there is a second-order correction; however, these terms cancel exactly in the calculation of $R$ [eq. (8.7)].

[^ep-8-2-tau]: The $\tau$ lepton decays predominantly into hadrons, and this should add something (less than 1) to $R$, above $1784\unit{\mathrm{MeV}}$. This presumably explains why the experimental numbers are somewhat higher than the “$u+d+s+c$” line in Figure 8.3. The fact that the (unanticipated) $\tau$ threshold comes so soon after the $c$ led to some confusion at first, and the theory seemed to be in trouble, but the discovery of the $\tau$, and the flatness of the graph above the $\Upsilon$ restored confidence in this simple picture.
