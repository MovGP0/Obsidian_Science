> [!TLDR]
> Inclusive inelastic scattering sums over outgoing hadrons and measures two proton structure functions.

<!-- PDF page 274; printed page 266 -->

At modest energies, electron-proton scattering is necessarily elastic ($e+p\to e+p$); the proton recoils, but it's still just a proton. But if the incident electron carries enough energy, all sorts of other stuff may come out—pions, kaons, deltas, you name it. We describe such an inelastic process ($e+p\to e+X$) by a diagram of the form

![[elementary-particles-inelastic-proton-inclusive-diagram.svg|Inclusive inelastic scattering with an unknown photon–proton vertex and outgoing hadrons X.]]

Again, the blob veils our ignorance about the photon-proton vertex; the extra outgoing lines represent the hadronic “shrapnel” from the exploded proton. As before, the *electron* vertex is unaffected by all the excitement at the proton end, and so the (spin-averaged) amplitude, for a given final state $X$, takes the form

$$
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle=\frac{\electric{g_e}^4}{\mechanical{q}^4}
L_{\mathrm{electron}}^{\mu\nu}K_{\mu\nu}(X)
\tag{8.24}
$$

where $K_{\mu\nu}$ is some (unknown) quantity describing the subprocess $\gamma+p\to X$; it depends on $\mechanical{q}\equiv(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3})$, $\mechanical{p}\equiv\mechanical{p_2}$, and the various outgoing momenta $\mechanical{p_4}$, $\mechanical{p_5}$, …, $\mechanical{p_n}$. The scattering cross section is determined by the “Golden Rule” [eq. (6.34)]:

$$
\begin{aligned}
d\mechanical{\sigma}={}&
\frac{\energy{\hbar}^2\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle}
{4\sqrt{(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2})^2
-(\mechanical{m_1}\mechanical{m_2}\mechanical{c}^2)^2}}
\left(\frac{\mechanical{c}\,d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}}{(2\pi)^3\,2\energy{E_3}}\right)
\left(\frac{\mechanical{c}\,d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}}{(2\pi)^3\,2\energy{E_4}}\right)
\cdots\left(\frac{\mechanical{c}\,d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_n}}{(2\pi)^3\,2\energy{E_n}}\right)\\
&\times(2\pi)^4\delta^4(\mechanical{p_1}+\mechanical{p_2}
-\mechanical{p_3}-\mechanical{p_4}-\cdots-\mechanical{p_n})
\end{aligned}
\tag{8.25}
$$

<!-- PDF page 275; printed page 267 -->

However, in a typical experiment only the momentum of the scattered electron ($\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}$) is recorded. What we measure is the so-called “inclusive” cross section, in which *all* accessible final states $X$, and all possible outgoing momenta, are included. To obtain the inclusive cross section from equation (8.25), we sum over $X$ and integrate over $\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}$, $\mechanical{\mathbf p_5}$, …, $\mechanical{\mathbf p_n}$:

$$
d\mechanical{\sigma}=
\frac{\energy{\hbar}^2\electric{g_e}^4L^{\mu\nu}}
{4\mechanical{q}^4\sqrt{(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2})^2
-(\mechanical{m_1}\mechanical{m_2}\mechanical{c}^2)^2}}
\left(\frac{\mechanical{c}\,d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}}{(2\pi)^3\,2\energy{E_3}}\right)
4\pi\mechanical{M}W_{\mu\nu}
\tag{8.26}
$$

where

$$
\begin{aligned}
W_{\mu\nu}\equiv{}&\frac{1}{4\pi\mechanical{M}}\sum_X\int\cdots\int
K_{\mu\nu}(X)
\left(\frac{\mechanical{c}\,d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}}{(2\pi)^3\,2\energy{E_4}}\right)
\cdots\left(\frac{\mechanical{c}\,d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_n}}{(2\pi)^3\,2\energy{E_n}}\right)\\
&\times(2\pi)^4\delta^4(\mechanical{q}+\mechanical{p}-\mechanical{p_4}
-\cdots-\mechanical{p_n})
\end{aligned}
\tag{8.27}
$$

For a massless electron of energy $\energy{E}$ striking a stationary proton of mass $\mechanical{M}$, the square root is just $\mechanical{M}\energy{E}$. Meanwhile, $d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}=|\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}|^2\,d|\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}|\,d\mechanical{\Omega}$, and (again setting $\mechanical{m}=0$) $|\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}|=\energy{E'}/\mechanical{c}$, where $\energy{E'}(=\energy{E_3})$ is the outgoing electron energy. Thus

<!-- PDF page 276; printed page 268 -->

$$
\frac{d\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\energy{E'}\,d\mechanical{\Omega}}
=\left(\frac{\electric{\alpha}\energy{\hbar}}{\mechanical{c}\mechanical{q}^2}\right)^2
\frac{\energy{E'}}{\energy{E}}L^{\mu\nu}W_{\mu\nu}
\tag{8.28}
$$

Notice that $\energy{E'}$ is *not* kinematically determined by $\energy{E}$ and $\mechanical{\theta}$, in the inelastic case, for the outgoing hadrons can soak up a range of energies. [More precisely, the total hadronic momentum, $\mechanical{p_{\mathrm{tot}}}=\mechanical{p_4}+\mechanical{p_5}+\cdots+\mechanical{p_n}$, is no longer constrained by the condition $\mechanical{p_{\mathrm{tot}}}^2=\mechanical{M}^2\mechanical{c}^2$, as it would be for a single proton. Therefore $\mechanical{p_3}=\mechanical{p_1}+\mechanical{p_2}-\mechanical{p_{\mathrm{tot}}}$ is also unconstrained, and equation (8.21) no longer follows.] Accordingly, what we are interested in is the differential cross section for scattering *in a particular energy range* $d\energy{E'}$, and this is what equation (8.28) gives us.

From here on the story is familiar: $W_{\mu\nu}$ is a second-rank tensor, which can only depend on $\mechanical{q}$ and $\mechanical{p}$, all other momenta having been integrated out. As before, it can be written in the form

$$
W^{\mu\nu}=-W_1g^{\mu\nu}
+\frac{W_2}{(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})^2}\mechanical{p^\mu}\mechanical{p^\nu}
+\frac{W_4}{(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})^2}\mechanical{q^\mu}\mechanical{q^\nu}
+\frac{W_5}{(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})^2}
(\mechanical{p^\mu}\mechanical{q^\nu}+\mechanical{p^\nu}\mechanical{q^\mu})
\tag{8.29}
$$

However, this time the $W_i$ are functions of *two* independent scalars, $\mechanical{q}^2$ and $\mechanical{q}\cdot\mechanical{p}$ (because $\mechanical{p_{\mathrm{tot}}}^2$ is no longer constrained, $\mechanical{q}\cdot\mechanical{p}$ is no longer related to $\mechanical{q}^2$). Once again, we have (Problem 8.4)

$$
\mechanical{q_\mu}W^{\mu\nu}=0
\tag{8.30}
$$

from which it follows that (Problem 8.11)

$$
W_4=\frac{(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})^2}{\mechanical{q}^2}W_1
+\left(\frac{\mechanical{q}\cdot\mechanical{p}}{\mechanical{q}^2}\right)^2W_2
\quad\text{and}\quad
W_5=-\frac{(\mechanical{q}\cdot\mechanical{p})}{\mechanical{q}^2}W_2
\tag{8.31}
$$

and therefore $W^{\mu\nu}$ can be expressed in terms of just two “structure functions”:

$$
W^{\mu\nu}=W_1\left(-g^{\mu\nu}
+\frac{\mechanical{q^\mu}\mechanical{q^\nu}}{\mechanical{q}^2}\right)
+\frac{W_2}{(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})^2}
\left[\mechanical{p^\mu}
-\left(\frac{\mechanical{q}\cdot\mechanical{p}}{\mechanical{q}^2}\right)\mechanical{q^\mu}\right]
\left[\mechanical{p^\nu}
-\left(\frac{\mechanical{q}\cdot\mechanical{p}}{\mechanical{q}^2}\right)\mechanical{q^\nu}\right]
\tag{8.32}
$$

Putting equations (8.13) and (8.32) into equation (8.28) (and again setting $\mechanical{m}=0$) we conclude

$$
\frac{d\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\energy{E'}\,d\mechanical{\Omega}}
=\left(\frac{\electric{\alpha}\energy{\hbar}}
{2\energy{E}\sin^2(\mechanical{\theta}/2)}\right)^2
[2W_1\sin^2(\mechanical{\theta}/2)+W_2\cos^2(\mechanical{\theta}/2)]
\tag{8.33}
$$

Equation (8.33) is the fundamental result for inclusive inelastic electron-proton scattering; it is the analog to the Rosenbluth formula (8.23)

$$
\frac{d\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\mechanical{\Omega}}
=\left(\frac{\electric{\alpha}\energy{\hbar}}
{4\mechanical{M}\energy{E}\sin^2(\mechanical{\theta}/2)}\right)^2
\frac{\energy{E'}}{\energy{E}}
[2K_1\sin^2(\mechanical{\theta}/2)+K_2\cos^2(\mechanical{\theta}/2)]
\tag{8.23}
$$

which describes *elastic* scattering. Remember that the structure functions ($W_1$ and $W_2$) depend on *two* independent variables, for a given incident energy ($\energy{E}$). The experimentalist would use $\energy{E'}$ and $\mechanical{\theta}$, whereas the theorist would generally prefer the Lorentz-invariant quantities $\mechanical{q}^2$ and $\mechanical{q}\cdot\mechanical{p}$—or better (for reasons you'll see in the next section)—$-\mechanical{q}^2$ and

$$
x\equiv-\frac{\mechanical{q}^2}{2\mechanical{q}\cdot\mechanical{p}}
\tag{8.34}
$$

<!-- PDF page 277; printed page 269 -->

By contrast, the *elastic* form factors ($K_1$ and $K_2$) depend on only *one* variable ($\mechanical{\theta}$, for the experimentalist, $\mechanical{q}^2$ for the theorist)—in this case $\energy{E'}$ is determined by equation (8.21) and $x$ is fixed ($x=1$). In a formal sense, elastic scattering is a special case of inelastic scattering, in which we impose an extra constraint ($\mechanical{p_{\mathrm{tot}}}^2=\mechanical{M}^2\mechanical{c}^2$) on the outgoing hadron momenta. It should be possible, therefore, to obtain the Rosenbluth formula from equation (8.33), by appropriate choice of the $W$'s. You can check for yourself that

$$
W_{1,2}(\mechanical{q}^2,x)
=-\frac{K_{1,2}(\mechanical{q}^2)}{2\mechanical{M}\mechanical{q}^2}\delta(x-1)
\tag{8.35}
$$

does the job (see Problem 8.12).

I should point out that there is precious little *physics* in all of this; what we have done is to *set the agenda* for a theory of proton structure. A successful theory must enable us to *calculate* the structure functions and form factors, which at this stage are completely arbitrary. The most naive model treats the proton as a simple point charge: in this case (Problem 8.6)

$$
K_1=-\mechanical{q}^2
\quad\text{and}\quad
K_2=(2\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})^2
\tag{8.36}
$$

It's not a bad model at low energies, where only elastic scattering occurs, and the electron never gets close enough to “see” the structure of the proton. But it is grossly inadequate at high energies (see Fig. 8.5). In the next two sections we shall see what the quark model has to say about the high-energy regime.
