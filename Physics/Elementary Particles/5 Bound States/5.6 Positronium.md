> [!TLDR]
> Positronium has equal-mass constituents, modified energy levels, and photon decays that obey charge conjugation symmetry.

<!-- PDF page 167; printed page 159 -->

The theory we have developed here for ordinary hydrogen carries over, with some modifications, to the so-called “exotic” atoms, in which either the proton

<!-- PDF page 168; printed page 160 -->

or the electron is replaced by some other particle. For instance, one can make muonic hydrogen ($p^{+}\mu^{-}$), pionic hydrogen ($p^{+}\pi^{-}$), positronium ($e^{+}e^{-}$), muonium ($\mu^{+}e^{-}$), and so on. Of course, these exotic states are unstable, but many of them last long enough to exhibit a well-defined spectrum. In particular, positronium provides a rich testing ground for quantum electrodynamics. It was analyzed theoretically by Pirenne in 1944, and first produced in the laboratory by Deutsch in 1951.<sup>17</sup> In recent years positronium has assumed a special importance as the model for quarkonium.

The most conspicuous difference between positronium and hydrogen is that we are no longer dealing with a heavy, essentially stationary nucleus, around which the electron orbits, but rather with two particles of *equal mass*, both orbiting around the common center. As in classical mechanics, this two-body problem can be converted into an equivalent one-body problem with the *reduced mass*

$$
\mechanical{m_{\mathrm{red}}}=\frac{\mechanical{m_1}\mechanical{m_2}}{\mechanical{m_1}+\mechanical{m_2}}.
\tag{5.70}
$$

For if the two-body Hamiltonian has the form

$$
\energy{H}=\frac{\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}^{2}}{2\mechanical{m_1}}
+\frac{\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}^{2}}{2\mechanical{m_2}}
+\energy{V}(\mechanical{\mathbf r_1},\mechanical{\mathbf r_2}),
\tag{5.71}
$$

and if the potential depends only on the separation distance, $\mechanical{r}=\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf r_2}-\mechanical{\mathbf r_1}\rvert$, and if we work in the *CM* system, where $\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}=-\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}=\mechanical{\mathbf p}$, then

$$
\energy{H}=\frac{\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{2}}{2\mechanical{m_{\mathrm{red}}}}+\energy{V}(\mechanical{r}),
\tag{5.72}
$$

which is the Hamiltonian for a *single* particle of momentum $\mechanical{\mathbf p}$ and mass $\mechanical{m_{\mathrm{red}}}$, whose “radial” coordinate, $\mechanical{r}$, is the distance between 1 and 2. The unperturbed Hamiltonian for positronium is of the form (5.71), with $\mechanical{m_1}=\mechanical{m_2}=\mechanical{m}$, so that $\mechanical{m_{\mathrm{red}}}=\mechanical{m}/2$ and $\energy{V}=-\electric{e}^{2}/\mechanical{r}$, the same as hydrogen. So we get the unperturbed energy levels for positronium by the simple substitution $\mechanical{m}\to\mechanical{m}/2$ in the Bohr formula (5.26):[^ep-5-6-reduced]

$$
\energy{E_n^{\mathrm{pos}}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\energy{E_n}
=-\electric{\alpha}^{2}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}\frac{1}{4\mechanical{n}^{2}}
\qquad(\mechanical{n}=1,2,3,\ldots).
\tag{5.73}
$$

For example, the ground-state binding energy is $13.6\,\unit{\mathrm{eV}}/2=6.8\,\unit{\mathrm{eV}}$. The unperturbed wave functions are the same as before (5.28), except that the Bohr radius, which goes like $1/\mechanical{m}$ [see eq. (5.29)], is doubled:

$$
\mechanical{a^{\mathrm{pos}}}=2\mechanical{a}=1.06\times10^{-8}\,\unit{\mathrm{cm}}.
\tag{5.74}
$$

The perturbations run much as before, apart from pesky numerical factors. For example, the relativistic correction picks up a factor of 2:

<!-- PDF page 169; printed page 161 -->

$$
\energy{\Delta H_{\mathrm{rel}}}
=-\frac{1}{8\mechanical{m_1}^{3}\mechanical{c}^{2}}\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}^{4}
-\frac{1}{8\mechanical{m_2}^{3}\mechanical{c}^{2}}\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}^{4}
=-\frac{1}{4\mechanical{m}^{3}\mechanical{c}^{2}}\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{4}.
\tag{5.75}
$$

On the other hand, the expectation value of $\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{4}$ in the hydrogenic state $\signal{\psi_{nlm}}$ goes like $(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})^{4}$, so for positronium it is reduced by $(\tfrac{1}{2})^{4}$. All told, then, the relativistic correction for positronium is one eighth that of hydrogen [eq. (5.42)]. More significant is the fact that in positronium the hyperfine splitting is of the same order as the fine structure ($\electric{\alpha}^{4}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}$), since the mass ratio ($\mechanical{m}/\mechanical{m_p}$) which suppresses proton spin effects in hydrogen is *one* in positronium.[^ep-5-6-terminology] Meanwhile, since the “nucleus” ($e^{+}$) is not stationary, we are no longer working with a truly static potential, and there is a new correction due to the finite propagation time for the electromagnetic field. This can be calculated using classical electrodynamics; it has the form

$$
\energy{\Delta H_{\mathrm{ret}}}
=-\frac{\electric{e}^{2}}{2\mechanical{m}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}}
\frac{1}{\mechanical{r}}
[\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{2}+(\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\mechanical{\hat{\mathbf r}})^{2}]
\tag{5.76}
$$

and its contribution is also of order ($\electric{\alpha}^{4}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}$). When all this is put together, the fine structure formula for positronium is found to be<sup>18</sup>

$$
\energy{E_{\mathrm{fs}}^{\mathrm{pos}}}
=\electric{\alpha}^{4}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}\frac{1}{2\mechanical{n}^{3}}
\left[\frac{11}{32\mechanical{n}}-\frac{(1+\tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon)}{(2\mechanical{l}+1)}\right]
\tag{5.77}
$$

where $\epsilon=0$ for the singlet spin combination, whereas for the triplet

$$
\epsilon=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle-\frac{(3\mechanical{l}+4)}{(\mechanical{l}+1)(2\mechanical{l}+3)},&
\text{for }\mechanical{j}=\mechanical{l}+1,\\[6pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mechanical{l}(\mechanical{l}+1)},&
\text{for }\mechanical{j}=\mechanical{l},\\[6pt]
\displaystyle\frac{(3\mechanical{l}-1)}{\mechanical{l}(2\mechanical{l}-1)},&
\text{for }\mechanical{j}=\mechanical{l}-1.
\end{cases}
\tag{5.78}
$$

[In hydrogen, where the proton spin ($\mechanical{\mathbf S_p}$) contributes only at the hyperfine level, we used $\mechanical{\mathbf J}$ for the sum of the electron's spin and orbital angular momentum ($\mechanical{\mathbf J}=\mechanical{\mathbf L}+\mechanical{\mathbf S_e}$); for the *total* angular momentum we needed a new letter: $\mechanical{\mathbf F}=\mechanical{\mathbf J}+\mechanical{\mathbf S_p}=\mechanical{\mathbf L}+\mechanical{\mathbf S_e}+\mechanical{\mathbf S_p}$. In positronium the two spins contribute on an equal footing, and it is customary to combine them first ($\mechanical{\mathbf S}=\mechanical{\mathbf S_1}+\mechanical{\mathbf S_2}$) and use $\mechanical{\mathbf J}$ for the total: $\mechanical{\mathbf J}=\mechanical{\mathbf L}+\mechanical{\mathbf S}=\mechanical{\mathbf L}+\mechanical{\mathbf S_1}+\mechanical{\mathbf S_2}$.]

The Lamb shift, of order $\electric{\alpha}^{5}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}$, makes a smallish correction to this; however, since the “accidental” degeneracy is already broken at the fine structure level in positronium, the Lamb shift loses much of its interest, and I shall not consider it here. There is, however, an entirely new perturbation, with no analog

![[elementary-particles-fig-5-6-pair-annihilation.svg|**Fig. 5.6** Pair annihilation diagram, which affects the spectrum of positronium but does not occur in hydrogen.]]

in hydrogen, resulting from the fact that $e^{+}$ and $e^{-}$ can annihilate temporarily to produce a virtual photon. In the Feynman picture this process is represented by the diagram in Figure 5.6. Because it requires that the electron and positron coincide, this perturbation is proportional to $\lvert\signal{\psi}(0)\rvert^{2}$, and hence occurs only when $\mechanical{l}=0$. [See remarks before eq. (5.67).] Moreover, since the photon carries spin 1, it takes place only in the triplet configuration. We are not in a position yet to calculate this correction, but it raises the energy of the triplet $S$ states by an amount

$$
\energy{\Delta E_{\mathrm{annih.}}}
=\electric{\alpha}^{4}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}\frac{1}{4\mechanical{n}^{3}}
\qquad(\mechanical{l}=0,\ \mechanical{s}=1).
\tag{5.79}
$$

Note that it is of the same order as the fine structure. The complete splitting of the $\mechanical{n}=1$ and $\mechanical{n}=2$ Bohr levels in positronium is indicated on Figure 5.7. [Positronium states are conventionally labeled $n^{(2s+1)}l_j$, with $\mechanical{l}$ given in spectroscopist's notation ($S$ for $\mechanical{l}=0$, $P$ for $\mechanical{l}=1$, $D$ for $\mechanical{l}=2$, etc.), and $\mechanical{s}$ the total spin (0 for the singlet, 1 for the triplet).]

As in the case of hydrogen, positronium can make transitions from one state to another with the emission or absorption of a photon, whose wavelength is determined by the difference in energy between the two levels. *Unlike* hydrogen, positronium can disintegrate completely, the positron annihilating the electron to produce two or more real photons. (Why can't they go to a single real photon?) The charge conjugation number for positronium is $(-1)^{\mechanical{l}+\mechanical{s}}$, while for $n$ photons $C=(-1)^{n}$ (see page 129). Thus charge conjugation invariance prescribes the selection rule

$$
(-1)^{\mechanical{l}+\mechanical{s}}=(-1)^{n}
\tag{5.80}
$$

for the decay of positronium in state $\mechanical{l}$, $\mechanical{s}$ into $n$ photons. Since the positron and electron overlap only when $\mechanical{l}=0$, such decays occur only from $S$ states.[^ep-5-6-higher] Evidently, the singlet ($\mechanical{s}=0$) must go to an even number of photons (typically two), whereas the triplet ($\mechanical{s}=1$) must go to an odd number (typically three). shall see in Chapter 7, the two-photon annihilation cross section[^ep-5-6-anticipation] for low relative velocity $\mechanical{v}$ in the singlet configuration is

$$
\mechanical{\sigma}=4\pi\electric{\alpha}^{2}
\left(\frac{\energy{\hbar}^{2}}{\mechanical{m}^{2}\mechanical{c}\mechanical{v}}\right).
\tag{5.81}
$$

In terms of this cross section, the decay rate is

$$
\mechanical{\Gamma}=\mechanical{\sigma}\mechanical{v}\lvert\signal{\psi}(0)\rvert^{2}.
\tag{5.82}
$$

Using equation (5.61) with the Bohr radius appropriate to positronium [eq. (5.74)], we obtain

$$
\mechanical{\Gamma}=\electric{\alpha}^{5}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}
\frac{1}{2\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{n}^{3}}.
\tag{5.83}
$$

In particular, the lifetime, $\mechanical{\tau}=\mechanical{\Gamma}^{-1}$, of the ground state is

$$
\mechanical{\tau}=\frac{2\energy{\hbar}}{\electric{\alpha}^{5}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}
=1.25\times10^{-10}\,\unit{\mathrm{sec}}.
\tag{5.84}
$$

[^ep-5-6-reduced]: In the case of ordinary hydrogen, the reduced mass differs from the electron mass by only a very small amount, about 0.05%. Nevertheless, technically the $\mechanical{m}$ in the Bohr formula is the reduced mass, and this does lead to observable differences between the spectra of hydrogen and deuterium.
[^ep-5-6-terminology]: This leads to some terminological confusion in the literature. I'll use the words “fine structure” for *all* perturbations of order $\electric{\alpha}^{4}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}$, except the pair annihilation term (see below) including the spin-spin and positron spin-orbit couplings, whose analogs in hydrogen would be called “hyperfine.”
[^ep-5-6-higher]: Actually, positronium can in principle decay directly from a state with $\mechanical{l}>0$ by a higher-order process, but it is much more likely to cascade down to an $S$ state first, and decay from there.
[^ep-5-6-anticipation]: This paragraph anticipates some material on cross sections, decay rates, and lifetimes that will be discussed in Chapter 6. It is included here for completeness.
