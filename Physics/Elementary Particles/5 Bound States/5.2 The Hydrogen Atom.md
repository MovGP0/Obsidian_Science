> [!TLDR]
> The Coulomb potential gives hydrogen wave functions, quantized energies, degeneracies, and the spectral wavelengths described by the Rydberg formula.

<!-- PDF page 156; printed page 148 -->

The hydrogen atom consists, of course, of an electron and a proton. The proton, however, is so heavy (relatively) that it essentially just sits at the origin; the wave function in question is that of the *electron*. Its potential energy, due to the electrical attraction of the nucleus, is (in Gaussian units)

$$
\energy{V}(\mechanical{r})=-\frac{\electric{e}^{2}}{\mechanical{r}}
\tag{5.25}
$$

When this potential is put into the radial equation, it is found (see Problem 5.5) that normalizable solutions occur only when $\energy{E}$ assumes one of the special values

$$
\energy{E_n}=-\frac{\mechanical{m}\electric{e}^{4}}{2\energy{\hbar}^{2}\mechanical{n}^{2}}
=-\electric{\alpha}^{2}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2\mechanical{n}^{2}}\right)
=-13.6\,\unit{\mathrm{eV}}/\mechanical{n}^{2}
\qquad(\mechanical{n}=1,2,3,\ldots)
\tag{5.26}
$$

where

$$
\electric{\alpha}\equiv\frac{\electric{e}^{2}}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}=\frac{1}{137.036}
\tag{5.27}
$$

is the fine structure constant. The corresponding (normalized) wave function is<sup>4</sup>

<!-- PDF page 157; printed page 149 -->

$$
\signal{\psi_{n,l,m_l}}(\mechanical{r},\mechanical{\theta},\mechanical{\phi})
=\left\{
\left(\frac{2}{\mechanical{n}\mechanical{a}}\right)^3
\frac{(\mechanical{n}-\mechanical{l}-1)!}{2\mechanical{n}[(\mechanical{n}+\mechanical{l})!]^3}
\right\}^{1/2}
e^{-\mechanical{r}/\mechanical{n}\mechanical{a}}
\left(\frac{2\mechanical{r}}{\mechanical{n}\mechanical{a}}\right)^{\mechanical{l}}
L_{n-l-1}^{2l+1}\left(\frac{2\mechanical{r}}{\mechanical{n}\mechanical{a}}\right)
Y_l^{m_l}(\mechanical{\theta},\mechanical{\phi})
\tag{5.28}
$$

where

$$
\mechanical{a}\equiv\frac{\energy{\hbar}^{2}}{\mechanical{m}\electric{e}^{2}}
=0.529\times10^{-8}\,\unit{\mathrm{cm}}
\tag{5.29}
$$

is the “Bohr radius” (roughly speaking, the radius of a hydrogen atom) and

$$
L_{q-p}^p(z)\equiv(-1)^p\left(\frac{d}{dz}\right)^p
\left[e^z\left(\frac{d}{dz}\right)^q(e^{-z}z^q)\right]
\tag{5.30}
$$

is an *associated Laguerre polynomial*. Obviously, the wave function itself is a bit of a mess, but that's not really what concerns us. The crucial thing is the formula of the allowed energies, equation (5.26). [This result was first obtained by Bohr in 1913 (more than a decade before the Schrödinger equation was introduced) by a brilliant (although, in retrospect, extraordinarily lucky) amalgam of classical ideas and primitive quantum theory, a blend, as Rabi put it, of “artistry and effrontery.”] Observe that the wave function is labeled by three numbers: $\mechanical{n}$, (the “principal quantum number”), which can be any positive integer—it determines the energy of the state [eq. (5.26)]; $\mechanical{l}$, an integer which ranges from 0 up to $\mechanical{n}-1$ and specifies the total orbital angular momentum [eq. (4.2)]; and $\mechanical{m_l}$, an integer which can assume any value between $-\mechanical{l}$ and $+\mechanical{l}$, giving the $\mechanical{z}$ component of the angular momentum [eq. (4.4)]. Evidently, there are $2\mechanical{l}+1$ different $\mechanical{m_l}$'s, for each $\mechanical{l}$, and $\mechanical{n}$ different $\mechanical{l}$'s, for each $\mechanical{n}$. The total number of distinct states that share the same principal quantum number $\mechanical{n}$, and hence the same energy, is, therefore

$$
\sum_{\mechanical{l}=0}^{\mechanical{n}-1}(2\mechanical{l}+1)=\mechanical{n}^{2}
\tag{5.31}
$$

This is called the *degeneracy* of the $\mechanical{n}$th energy level. Hydrogen is a surprisingly degenerate system; spherical symmetry alone dictates that the $2\mechanical{l}+1$ states with a given value of the total angular momentum should be degenerate, since they differ only in the orientation of $\mechanical{\mathbf L}$, but this suggests a sequence $1,3,5,7,\ldots$, whereas the energy levels of hydrogen have much higher degeneracies: $1,4,9,16,\ldots$. So far, however, we have neglected the electron's spin, as well as a small relativistic effect. Their inclusion, as we shall see in the next section, leads to corrections that “lift” the “extra” degeneracy, splitting the Bohr energies into groups of closely spaced levels.

Before coming to that, however, I should describe how the quantized energy levels of hydrogen reveal themselves in the laboratory, for in practice we do not measure the energies themselves, but rather the wavelength of the light emitted when the electron makes a transition from a higher level to a lower one (or the light absorbed when it goes the other way).<sup>5</sup> The photon carries the *difference* in energy between the initial and final states. According to the Planck formula [eq. (1.1)], then

<!-- PDF page 158; printed page 150 -->

![[elementary-particles-fig-5-1-hydrogen-spectrum.jpg|**Fig. 5.1** The spectrum of hydrogen. When an atom changes from one state to another, the difference in energy appears as a quantum of radiation. The energy of the photon is directly proportional to the frequency of the radiation and inversely proportional to the wavelength. Absorption of radiation stimulates a transition to a state of higher energy; an atom falling to a state of lower energy emits radiation. The spectrum is organized into series of lines that share a common lower level. Wavelengths are given in angstroms; the relative intensity of the lines is indicated by thickness. (From “The Spectrum of Atomic Hydrogen,” by T. W. Hänsch, A. L. Schawlow, and G. W. Series. Copyright © March 1979 by Scientific American, Inc. All rights reserved.)]]

<!-- PDF page 159; printed page 151 -->

$$
\energy{E_{\mathrm{photon}}}=\energy{h}\signal{\nu}
=\energy{E_{\mathrm{initial}}}-\energy{E_{\mathrm{final}}}
=-\frac{\mechanical{m}\electric{e}^{4}}{2\energy{\hbar}^{2}}
\left(\frac{1}{\mechanical{n_i}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\mechanical{n_f}^{2}}\right)
\tag{5.32}
$$

The emitted wavelength, therefore, is given by

$$
\frac{1}{\signal{\lambda}}=\signal{R}\left(\frac{1}{\mechanical{n_f}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\mechanical{n_i}^{2}}\right)
\tag{5.33}
$$

where

$$
\signal{R}\equiv\frac{\mechanical{m}\electric{e}^{4}\mechanical{c}}{4\pi\energy{\hbar}^{3}}
\tag{5.34}
$$

This is the famous *Rydberg formula* for the spectrum of hydrogen. It was discovered experimentally by nineteenth-century spectroscopists, for whom $\signal{R}$ was simply an empirical constant. The greatest triumph of Bohr's theory was its derivation of the Rydberg formula, and its calculation of $\signal{R}$ in terms of the fundamental constants $\mechanical{m},\electric{e},\mechanical{c}$, and $\energy{\hbar}$ (see Fig. 5.1).
