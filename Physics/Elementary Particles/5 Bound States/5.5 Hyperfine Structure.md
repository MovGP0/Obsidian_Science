> [!TLDR]
> Proton spin splits hydrogen energy levels and produces the 21-centimeter microwave transition.

The fine structure and the Lamb shift are minute corrections to the Bohr energy levels, but they are not the end of the story; there is a refinement that is smaller still (by a factor of a thousand), due to the spin of the *nucleus*. The proton, like the electron, constitutes a tiny magnet, but because it is so much heavier, the same angular momentum ($\tfrac{1}{2}\energy{\hbar}$) corresponds to a much smaller dipole moment:[^ep-5-5-ratio]

$$
\magnetic{\boldsymbol{\mu}_p}=\magnetic{\gamma_p}\frac{\electric{e}}{\mechanical{m_p}\mechanical{c}}\mechanical{\mathbf S_p}.
\tag{5.55}
$$

(The proton is a composite object, and its magnetic moment is not simply $\electric{e}\energy{\hbar}/2\mechanical{m_p}\mechanical{c}$, as it would be for a truly elementary particle of spin $\tfrac{1}{2}$. Hence the

<!-- PDF page 165; printed page 157 -->

![[elementary-particles-fig-5-4-magnetic-dipole.svg|**Fig. 5.4** Field of a magnetic dipole. In the plane of the current loop, B points down outside the ring, and up inside the ring.]]

factor $\magnetic{\gamma_p}$, whose experimental value is 2.7928. Later on we shall see how to calculate this quantity in the quark model.) The nuclear spin interacts with the electron's orbital motion by the same mechanism as the spin-orbit contribution to the fine structure, only this time there is no hanky-panky about changing reference systems, and no Thomas precession to worry about. Referring to our earlier calculation [eq. (5.47)], we find that the nuclear spin-orbit interaction is[^ep-5-5-sign]

$$
\energy{\Delta H_{\mathrm{pso}}}
=\frac{\magnetic{\gamma_p}\electric{e}^{2}}{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{m_p}\mechanical{c}^{2}\mechanical{r}^{3}}
(\mechanical{\mathbf L}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf S_p}).
\tag{5.56}
$$

At the same time, the proton spin also interacts directly with the electron spin. In many books on electricity and magnetism<sup>13</sup> it is shown that a magnetic dipole $\magnetic{\boldsymbol{\mu}}$ sets up a field

$$
\magnetic{\mathbf B}(\mechanical{\mathbf r})
=\frac{1}{\mechanical{r}^{3}}
\left[3\frac{(\magnetic{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf r})\mechanical{\mathbf r}}{\mechanical{r}^{2}}
-\magnetic{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\right].
\tag{5.57}
$$

Actually, this formula is not quite right; if we picture the dipole as a tiny current loop, equation (5.57) says that in the plane of the loop (where $\magnetic{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf r}=0$) $\magnetic{\mathbf B}$ always points in the direction opposite to $\magnetic{\boldsymbol{\mu}}$, whereas inside the loop $\magnetic{\mathbf B}$ is in fact *parallel* to $\magnetic{\boldsymbol{\mu}}$ (see Fig. 5.4). It is true that as we shrink the loop down to make a “perfect” point dipole, the region where the field has the “wrong” direction gets smaller and smaller. On the other hand, its *strength* gets larger and larger, since all those field lines must pass through the ring. The correct handling of this “anomalous” field is a rather delicate problem in classical electrodynamics;<sup>14</sup> the conclusion is that

$$
\magnetic{\mathbf B}(\mechanical{\mathbf r})
=\frac{1}{\mechanical{r}^{3}}
\left[3\frac{(\magnetic{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf r})\mechanical{\mathbf r}}{\mechanical{r}^{2}}
-\magnetic{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\right]
+\frac{8\pi}{3}\magnetic{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\delta^{3}(\mechanical{\mathbf r}),
\tag{5.58}
$$

where $\delta^{3}(\mechanical{\mathbf r})$ is the Dirac delta function[^ep-5-5-delta]—infinite at $\mechanical{r}=0$ and zero elsewhere. As long as you stay away from the origin, the “naïve” formula (5.57) is perfectly

<!-- PDF page 166; printed page 158 -->

correct. However, as we shall soon see, the delta function term makes a crucial contribution to hyperfine structure. In the present case the proton sets up the field $\magnetic{\mathbf B}$, and the energy of the electron in its presence is given by equation (5.46). Thus the spin-spin Hamiltonian is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\energy{\Delta H_{\mathrm{ss}}}
={}&\frac{\magnetic{\gamma_p}\electric{e}^{2}}{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{m_p}\mechanical{c}^{2}}
\left\{\frac{1}{\mechanical{r}^{3}}
\left[3(\mechanical{\mathbf S_p}\cdot\mechanical{\hat{\mathbf r}})
(\mechanical{\mathbf S_e}\cdot\mechanical{\hat{\mathbf r}})
-(\mechanical{\mathbf S_p}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf S_e})\right]\right.\\
&\left.\qquad+\frac{8\pi}{3}(\mechanical{\mathbf S_p}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf S_e})
\delta^{3}(\mechanical{\mathbf r})\right\},
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.59}
$$

where $\mechanical{\mathbf S_p}$ and $\mechanical{\mathbf S_e}$ are the spin angular momenta of the proton and electron, respectively.

Together, the nuclear spin-orbit interaction (5.56) and the proton–electron spin-spin coupling (5.59) are responsible for the hyperfine structure of hydrogen. To calculate the resulting energy shifts, we again use the standard formula from perturbation theory (5.35). At this point the treatment is quite different for states with $\mechanical{l}=0$, so we'll consider them first. Since the orbital angular momentum is zero, there is no spin-orbit coupling. Moreover, because the wave function is spherically symmetrical ($\signal{Y_0^0}(\mechanical{\theta},\mechanical{\phi})=1/\sqrt{4\pi}$) the expectation value of the first term in equation (5.59) vanishes (see Problem 5.12). This leaves only the delta function contribution

$$
\energy{\Delta E_{\mathrm{hf}}}
=\frac{8\pi\magnetic{\gamma_p}\electric{e}^{2}}{3\mechanical{m}\mechanical{m_p}\mechanical{c}^{2}}
(\mechanical{\mathbf S_p}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf S_e})
\lvert\signal{\psi_{n00}}(0)\rvert^{2}.
\tag{5.60}
$$

From equation (5.28) we find (Problem 5.13) that

$$
\lvert\signal{\psi_{n00}}(0)\rvert^{2}=\frac{1}{\pi\mechanical{n}^{3}\mechanical{a}^{3}}.
\tag{5.61}
$$

Let

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf F}
=\mechanical{\mathbf L}+\mechanical{\mathbf S_e}+\mechanical{\mathbf S_p}
=\mechanical{\mathbf J}+\mechanical{\mathbf S_p}
\tag{5.62}
$$

be the total angular momentum of the atom, with quantum numbers $\mechanical{f}$ and $\mechanical{m_f}$; in the present case $\mechanical{\mathbf L}=0$, so $\mechanical{F}^{2}=\mechanical{S_e}^{2}+\mechanical{S_p}^{2}+2\mechanical{\mathbf S_e}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf S_p}$, and hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{\mathbf S_p}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf S_e}
&=\frac{\energy{\hbar}^{2}}{2}
[\mechanical{f}(\mechanical{f}+1)-\mechanical{s_p}(\mechanical{s_p}+1)-\mechanical{s_e}(\mechanical{s_e}+1)]\\
&=\frac{\energy{\hbar}^{2}}{2}[\mechanical{f}(\mechanical{f}+1)-\tfrac{3}{2}].
\end{aligned}
\tag{5.63}
$$

Thus

$$
\energy{\Delta E_{\mathrm{hf}}}
=\left(\frac{\mechanical{m}}{\mechanical{m_p}}\right)
\electric{\alpha}^{4}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}
\frac{4\magnetic{\gamma_p}}{3\mechanical{n}^{3}}
[\mechanical{f}(\mechanical{f}+1)-\tfrac{3}{2}].
\tag{5.64}
$$

Comparing the fine structure formula (5.52), we see that the difference in scale is due to the mass ratio ($\mechanical{m}/\mechanical{m_p}$) in front; it follows that hyperfine effects in hydrogen are about one thousand times smaller. Notice that $\mechanical{f}$ can take on two values: zero, in the singlet state (when the spins are oppositely aligned) and one, in the triplet state (when the spins are parallel). Thus each $\mechanical{l}=0$ level divides into two, with the singlet pushed down and the triplet pushed up (Fig. 5.5). For $\mechanical{n}=1$ the energy gap is

$$
\energy{\epsilon}=\energy{E_{\mathrm{triplet}}}-\energy{E_{\mathrm{singlet}}}
=\frac{32\magnetic{\gamma_p}\energy{E_1}^{2}}{3\mechanical{m_p}\mechanical{c}^{2}},
\tag{5.65}
$$

corresponding to a photon of wavelength

$$
\signal{\lambda}=\frac{2\pi\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}{\energy{\epsilon}}
=21.1\,\unit{\mathrm{cm}}.
\tag{5.66}
$$

This is the transition that gives rise to the famous “21-centimeter line” in microwave astronomy.<sup>15</sup>

For $\mechanical{l}=0$, as we have seen, the hyperfine structure comes entirely from the “contact” term in the spin-spin coupling; for $\mechanical{l}\ne0$ it is exactly the reverse. This time the delta function contributes nothing; the wave function (5.28) goes like $\mechanical{r}^{\mechanical{l}}$ at small $\mechanical{r}$, so $\signal{\psi}(0)=0$ when $\mechanical{l}>0$. Physically, the centrifugal (pseudo-)force keeps the electron away from the nucleus. Accordingly, we now have

$$
\energy{\Delta E_{\mathrm{hf}}}
=\frac{\magnetic{\gamma_p}\electric{e}^{2}}{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{m_p}\mechanical{c}^{2}}
\left\langle\frac{1}{\mechanical{r}^{3}}
\left[(\mechanical{\mathbf L}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf S_p})
+3(\mechanical{\mathbf S_p}\cdot\mechanical{\hat{\mathbf r}})
(\mechanical{\mathbf S_e}\cdot\mechanical{\hat{\mathbf r}})
-(\mechanical{\mathbf S_p}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf S_e})\right]\right\rangle.
\tag{5.67}
$$

This expectation value is calculated, for instance, by Bethe and Salpeter;<sup>16</sup> the result is

$$
\energy{\Delta E_{\mathrm{hf}}}
=\left(\frac{\mechanical{m}}{\mechanical{m_p}}\right)
\electric{\alpha}^{4}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}
\frac{\magnetic{\gamma_p}}{2\mechanical{n}^{3}}
\frac{\mechanical{f}(\mechanical{f}+1)-\mechanical{j}(\mechanical{j}+1)-\tfrac{3}{4}}
{\mechanical{j}(\mechanical{j}+1)(\mechanical{l}+\tfrac{1}{2})}.
\tag{5.68}
$$

As it turns out, this formula works for $\mechanical{l}=0$ as well, since in that case $\mechanical{j}=\tfrac{1}{2}$, and we recover equation (5.64). Because the proton carries spin $\tfrac{1}{2}$, $\mechanical{f}$ can only be $\mechanical{j}+\tfrac{1}{2}$ or $\mechanical{j}-\tfrac{1}{2}$; with this in mind, equation (5.68) simplifies slightly:

$$
\energy{\Delta E_{\mathrm{hf}}}
=\left(\frac{\mechanical{m}}{\mechanical{m_p}}\right)
\electric{\alpha}^{4}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}
\frac{\magnetic{\gamma_p}}{2\mechanical{n}^{3}}
\left[\frac{\pm1}{(\mechanical{f}+\tfrac{1}{2})(\mechanical{l}+\tfrac{1}{2})}\right],
\quad\text{for }\mechanical{f}=\mechanical{j}\pm\tfrac{1}{2}.
\tag{5.69}
$$

Each of the levels in Figure 5.2, characterized by particular values of $\mechanical{n}$, $\mechanical{l}$, and $\mechanical{j}$, is split into two—one moving up, and the other down.

[^ep-5-5-ratio]: In fancier language, the proton's gyromagnetic ratio is much smaller than the electron's.
[^ep-5-5-sign]: Note that equation (5.44) picks up a minus sign this time, since we're talking about the field of an orbiting *electron*, not a *proton*.
[^ep-5-5-delta]: The delta function is discussed in Appendix A. For now, all you need to know is that $\int f(\mechanical{\mathbf r})\delta^{3}(\mechanical{\mathbf r})\,d^{3}\mechanical{r}=f(0)$. Technically, the first term in equation (5.58) applies to the region outside a tiny sphere of radius $\epsilon$, and the second term to the region inside. At the end of a calculation $\epsilon$ is set equal to zero.
