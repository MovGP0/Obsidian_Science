Consider now the mesons made entirely out of *light* quarks ($u$, $d$, $s$). These are relativistic systems, remember, so we cannot use the Schrödinger equation, and

<!-- PDF page 177; printed page 169 -->

the theory is rather limited.<sup>23</sup> In particular, we shall not concern ourselves with the spectrum of excited states (Table 4.6), as we did in the case of the heavy quarks, but will confine our attention to the ground state, with $\mechanical{l}=0$. The quark spins can be antiparallel (singlet state, $\mechanical{s}=0$) or parallel (triplet state, $\mechanical{s}=1$); the former configuration yields the pseudoscalar nonet, the latter gives the vector nonet (Fig. 5.11).

To begin with I want to clear up a problem that was not resolved in Chapter 1. We obtained nine mesons simply by combining a quark and an antiquark in all possible combinations (Chap. 1, Sect. 1.8), but this left three neutral states with strangeness 0 ($u\bar u$, $d\bar d$, and $s\bar s$), and it was not clear which of these was the $\pi^{0}$, which the $\eta$, and which the $\eta'$ (or, in the vector case, the $\rho^{0}$, $\omega$, and $\phi$). We are now in a position to resolve the ambiguity. The up and down quarks constitute an isospin doublet:

$$
u=\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\ \tfrac{1}{2}\rangle,\qquad
d=\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\ {-}\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle.
\tag{5.87}
$$

So too do the antiquarks:

$$
\bar d=-\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\ \tfrac{1}{2}\rangle,\qquad
\bar u=\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\ {-}\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle.
\tag{5.88}
$$

(Notice that $\bar d$ carries $I_3=+\tfrac{1}{2}$, and $\bar u$ has $I_3=-\tfrac{1}{2}$; within a multiplet, the particle with the higher charge is assigned the greater $I_3$. The minus sign is a technical detail,<sup>24</sup> which does not affect the argument here in any essential way.) When we combine two particles with $I=\tfrac{1}{2}$, we obtain an isotriplet (eq. 4.15)

**Fig. 5.11** Light-quark mesons with l = 0.

$$
\begin{cases}
\lvert1\ \ 1\rangle=-u\bar d\\
\lvert1\ \ 0\rangle=(u\bar u-d\bar d)/\sqrt{2}\\
\lvert1\ {-}1\rangle=d\bar u
\end{cases}
\tag{5.89}
$$

and an isosinglet (4.16)

$$
\lvert00\rangle=(u\bar u+d\bar d)/\sqrt{2}.
\tag{5.90}
$$

In the case of the pseudoscalar mesons the triplet is the pion; for the vector mesons it is the $\rho$. Evidently the $\pi^{0}$ (or the $\rho^{0}$) is neither $u\bar u$ nor $d\bar d$, but rather the linear combination

$$
\pi^{0},\rho^{0}=(u\bar u-d\bar d)/\sqrt{2}.
\tag{5.91}
$$

If you could pull a $\pi^{0}$ apart, half the time you'd get a $u$ in one hand and a $\bar u$ in the other, and half the time you'd get a $d$ and a $\bar d$.

This leaves two $I=0$ states (the isosinglet combination, equation (5.90), and $s\bar s$) which must represent $\eta$ and $\eta'$ (or $\omega$ and $\phi$). Here the situation is not so clean, for these particles carry identical quantum numbers, and they tend in practice to “mix.” In the case of the pseudoscalars the physical states appear to be

$$
\eta=(u\bar u+d\bar d-2s\bar s)/\sqrt{6}
\tag{5.92}
$$

$$
\eta'=(u\bar u+d\bar d+s\bar s)/\sqrt{3}
\tag{5.93}
$$

whereas for the vector mesons we find

$$
\omega=(u\bar u+d\bar d)/\sqrt{2}
\tag{5.94}
$$

$$
\phi=s\bar s.
\tag{5.95}
$$

To the extent that the Eightfold Way is a good symmetry, the pseudoscalar combinations are more “natural”, since the $\eta'$, which treats $u$, $d$, and $s$ symmetrically, is unaffected by $SU(3)$ transformations; it is a “singlet” under $SU(3)$, in exactly the same sense that the $\pi^{0}$ is a singlet under $SU(2)$ (isospin). The $\eta$, meanwhile, transforms as part of an $SU(3)$ “octet”, whose other members are the three pions and the four $K$'s. (This is, in fact, the original pseudoscalar octet.)

**Fig. 5.12** Quarks and antiquarks.

By contrast, neither the $\phi$ nor the $\omega$ is an $SU(3)$ singlet. They are, you might say, “maximally” mixed, since the strange quark is isolated from the other two. [Incidentally, the other meson nonets seem to follow the $\phi$–$\omega$ mixing pattern, with the possible exception of the $D$ and $E$ (Table 4.6).]

Meanwhile, the strange mesons are constructed by combining an $s$ quark with $u$ or $d$

$$
K^{+}=u\bar s,\qquad K^{0}=d\bar s,\qquad
\bar K^{0}=-s\bar d,\qquad K^{-}=s\bar u.
\tag{5.96}
$$

In the language of group theory, the three light quarks belong to the fundamental representation (denoted $3$) of $SU(3)$, whereas the antiquarks belong to the conjugate representation ($\bar3$) (See Fig. 5.12). What we have done is to combine these representations, obtaining an octet and a singlet:

$$
3\otimes\bar3=8\oplus1
\tag{5.97}
$$

just as in Chapter 4 we combined two two-dimensional (spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$) representations of $SU(2)$ to obtain a triplet and a singlet:[^ep-5-8-representations]

$$
2\otimes\bar2=3\oplus1.
\tag{5.98}
$$

If $SU(3)$ were a perfect symmetry, all the particles in a given supermultiplet would have the same mass. But they obviously do not: the $K$ weighs more than three times the $\pi$, for example. As I indicated in Chapter 4, the breaking of flavor symmetry is due to the fact that the quarks themselves have unequal masses; the $u$ and $d$ quarks weigh about the same (which is why isospin is such a good symmetry) but the $s$ quark is substantially heavier. Roughly speaking, the $K$'s weigh more than the $\pi$'s because they contain an $s$ in place of a $u$ or $d$. But that cannot be the whole story, for if it were, the $\rho$'s would weigh the same as the $\pi$'s; after all, they have the same quark content and are both in the spatial ground state ($\mechanical{n}=1$, $\mechanical{l}=0$). Since the pseudoscalar and vector mesons differ only in the relative orientation of the quark spins, the difference in their masses must be attributed to a spin-spin interaction, the QCD analog to hyperfine splitting in the ground state of hydrogen. The QED formula, remember, is

$$
\energy{\Delta E_{\mathrm{hf}}}
=\frac{8\pi\magnetic{\gamma_p}\electric{e}^{2}}
{3\mechanical{m}\mechanical{m_p}\mechanical{c}^{2}}
(\mechanical{\mathbf S_e}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf S_p})
\lvert\signal{\psi_{100}}(0)\rvert^{2}.
\tag{5.60}
$$

It seems reasonable to suppose that the spin-spin coupling in QCD has a similar structure; that is, it should be proportional to the dot product of the spins and inversely proportional to the product of the constituent masses. On this assumption, we are led to the following meson mass formula:

$$
\mechanical{M}(\text{meson})=\mechanical{m_1}+\mechanical{m_2}
+A\frac{(\mechanical{\mathbf S_1}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf S_2})}{\mechanical{m_1}\mechanical{m_2}}.
\tag{5.99}
$$

The coefficient $A$ is related to $\lvert\signal{\psi}(0)\rvert^{2}$, which we are not in a position to calculate. One assumes it is the same for all the vector and pseudoscalar mesons, since they occupy the same quantum state.[^ep-5-8-assumption] By the usual trick of squaring $\mechanical{\mathbf S}=\mechanical{\mathbf S_1}+\mechanical{\mathbf S_2}$ [see eq. (5.63)], we find that

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf S_1}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf S_2}=
\begin{cases}
\tfrac{1}{4}\energy{\hbar}^{2},&\text{for }\mechanical{s}=1\text{ (vector mesons)}\\
-\tfrac{3}{4}\energy{\hbar}^{2},&\text{for }\mechanical{s}=0\text{ (pseudoscalars)}.
\end{cases}
\tag{5.100}
$$

For constituent masses $\mechanical{m_u}=\mechanical{m_d}=310\,\unit{\mathrm{MeV}}/\mechanical{c}^{2}$, $\mechanical{m_s}=483\,\unit{\mathrm{MeV}}/\mechanical{c}^{2}$, the “best-fit” value of $A$ is $(2\mechanical{m_u}/\energy{\hbar})^{2}160\,\unit{\mathrm{MeV}}/\mechanical{c}^{2}$, and we obtain the results in Table 5.3. Considering its somewhat shaky theoretical foundation, equation (5.99) works surprisingly well, matching seven independent meson masses to an accuracy of about 1%, with three adjustable input parameters. (Notice, however, that the $\eta'$ is not included in the table. See Problem 5.22.)

[^ep-5-8-representations]: Unfortunately (from the point of view of notational consistency) representations of $SU(3)$ are customarily labeled by their dimensionality, whereas representations of $SU(2)$ are more often identified by their spin, so that equation (5.98) would usually be written $\tfrac{1}{2}\otimes\tfrac{1}{2}=1\oplus0$. By the way, it happens that the fundamental representation of $SU(2)$ is equivalent to its conjugate; there's only one kind of spin $\tfrac{1}{2}$. That's why we were able in equation (5.88), to represent $\bar u$ and $\bar d$ in terms of ordinary spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ states. For $SU(3)$ this is no longer the case.
[^ep-5-8-assumption]: In my view this is a questionable assumption: (i) For a Coulombic potential we know [eqs. (5.61 and 5.29)] that $\lvert\signal{\psi}(0)\rvert^{2}$ goes like the *cube* of the reduced mass (for a linear potential it is proportional to $\mechanical{m}$). Why preserve the explicit mass dependence in the denominator of equation (5.99), if we are prepared to ignore it in the numerator? (ii) The central ($I_3=0$) members of each nonet combine a quark with its own antiquark, and hence admit annihilation diagrams, just as positronium does, that are not possible for the other mesons. However, nothing succeeds like success, and equation (5.99) works remarkably well.
