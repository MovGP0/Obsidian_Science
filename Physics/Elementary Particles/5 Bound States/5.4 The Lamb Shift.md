> [!TLDR]
> Quantum electromagnetic corrections produce the Lamb shift and separate hydrogen states that remain degenerate under fine structure alone.

<!-- PDF page 162; printed page 154 -->

A striking feature of the fine structure formula (5.52) is that it depends only on $\mechanical{j}$, not on $\mechanical{l}$; in general, two different values of $\mechanical{l}$ share the same energy. For example, the $2S_{1/2}$ ($\mechanical{n}=2$, $\mechanical{l}=0$; $\mechanical{j}=\tfrac{1}{2}$) and $2P_{1/2}$ ($\mechanical{n}=2$, $\mechanical{l}=1$, $\mechanical{j}=\tfrac{1}{2}$) states should remain perfectly degenerate. In 1947 Lamb and Retherford performed a classic experiment<sup>10</sup> which demonstrated that this is not, in fact, the case; the $S$ state is slightly higher in energy than the $P$ state. The explanation of this *Lamb shift* was provided by Bethe, Feynman, Schwinger, Tomonaga, and others: it is due to the *quantization of the electromagnetic field itself*. Everywhere else in this discussion—in the calculation of the Bohr levels, in the derivation of the fine structure formula, and even in the analysis of hyperfine structure in the next section—the electromagnetic field is treated entirely classically. Coulomb's law is the basis for equation (5.25); the Biot–Savart Law yields equation (5.43); the Lorentz force law is responsible for equation (5.46). The Lamb shift, by contrast, is an example of a *radiative correction* in quantum electrodynamics, to which the semiclassical[^ep-5-4-semiclassical] theory is insensitive. In the Feynman formalism, it results from *loop diagrams*, such as those in Figure 5.3, which we shall discuss quantitatively later on.

<!-- PDF page 163; printed page 155 -->

Qualitatively, the first diagram in Fig. 5.3 describes spontaneous production of electron–positron pairs in the neighborhood of the nucleus, leading to a partial screening of the proton's charge (Fig. 2.1). The second diagram reflects the fact that the ground state of the electromagnetic field is *not* zero:<sup>11</sup> as the electron moves through the “vacuum fluctuations” in the field, it jiggles slightly, and this alters its energy. The third diagram leads to a tiny modification of the electron's magnetic dipole moment; equation (5.45) picks up a factor $(1+\electric{\alpha}/2\pi)=1.00116$. ![[elementary-particles-fig-5-3-lamb-shift-loops.svg|**Fig. 5.3** Some loop diagrams contributing to the Lamb shift.]]

***For l = 0***

$$
\energy{\Delta E_{\mathrm{Lamb}}}
=\electric{\alpha}^{5}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}
\frac{1}{4\mechanical{n}^{3}}\{k(\mechanical{n},0)\}
\tag{5.53}
$$

where $k(\mechanical{n},0)$ is a numerical factor that varies slightly with $\mechanical{n}$, from 12.7 (for $\mechanical{n}=1$) to 13.2 (for $\mechanical{n}\to\infty$).

***For l ≠ 0***

$$
\energy{\Delta E_{\mathrm{Lamb}}}
=\electric{\alpha}^{5}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}
\frac{1}{4\mechanical{n}^{3}}
\left\{k(\mechanical{n},\mechanical{l})
\pm\frac{1}{\pi(\mechanical{j}+\tfrac{1}{2})(\mechanical{l}+\tfrac{1}{2})}\right\},
\quad\text{for }\mechanical{j}=\mechanical{l}\pm\tfrac{1}{2}
\tag{5.54}
$$

where $k(\mechanical{n},\mechanical{l})$ is a very small number (less than 0.05) which varies slightly with $\mechanical{n}$ and $\mechanical{l}$. Evidently the Lamb shift is tiny, except for states with $\mechanical{l}=0$, for which it amounts to about 10% of the fine structure. However, because it depends on $\mechanical{l}$, it lifts the degeneracy of the pairs of states with common $\mechanical{n}$ and $\mechanical{j}$, on Figure 5.2, and in particular it splits the $2S_{1/2}$ and $2P_{1/2}$ levels (See Problem 5.11).
