> [!TLDR]
> The Schrödinger equation separates into time, angular, and radial parts for a central potential; normalizable radial solutions select allowed energies.

<!-- PDF page 151; printed page 143 -->

*Most of this chapter is devoted to the nonrelativistic theory of two-particle bound states, with emphasis on hydrogen ($e^-p^+$), positronium ($e^-e^+$), charmonium ($c\bar c$), and bottomonium ($b\bar b$). This material is not used in subsequent chapters and may be skimmed, saved for later, or skipped entirely. Two tools from elementary quantum mechanics are essential: the Schrödinger equation and perturbation theory; readers unacquainted with these subjects should refer to the appropriate sections of an introductory quantum text (though the essential points are reviewed here, as they arise). The final two sections (5.8 and 5.9) concern relativistic light quark systems—the familiar mesons and baryons—about which far less can be said with confidence. I concentrate on the spin/flavor/color structure of the wave functions and develop a model for estimating masses and magnetic moments. This material does not involve the Schrödinger equation or perturbation theory and can be read independently of Sections 5.1 through 5.7. It will be used briefly later on, in the latter part of Chapter 9.*

Until recently, the theory of bound states played a rather minor role in particle physics. There was, of course, hydrogen (proton plus electron)—but this (the simplest atom) fell in the domain of atomic physics. And there was the deuteron (proton plus neutron)—but this (the simplest nontrivial nucleus) belonged more properly to nuclear physics. The quark model, though, changed everything. Suddenly the hadrons themselves were bound states—all mesons were two-quark systems, and all baryons were three-quark systems. With this discovery the theory of bound states became an important component of elementary particle physics.

<!-- PDF page 152; printed page 144 -->

The analysis of a bound state is simplest when the constituents travel at speeds substantially less than $\mechanical{c}$, for then the apparatus of nonrelativistic quantum mechanics can be brought to bear. Such is the case for hydrogen and for hadrons made out of heavy quarks ($c,b$, and $t$). The more familiar light-quark states (made out of $u,d$, and $s$) are much more difficult to handle, because they are intrinsically relativistic, and quantum field theory (as currently practiced) is not well suited to bound-state problems. (Most of the techniques available start from the premise that the particles are initially free, and free again after some brief interaction (a collision, typically), whereas in a bound state, by its nature, the particles interact continuously over a long period.) At present, therefore, there exists a very rich theory of “charmonium” ($c\bar c$, the $\psi$ meson system) and “bottomonium” ($b\bar b$, the $\Upsilon$ system), but comparatively little can be said about the excited states of $u\bar u$ (say) or $d\bar d$. How can we tell whether a given bound state is relativistic or not? The simplest criterion is the following: If the binding energy is small compared to the rest energies of the constituents, then the system is nonrelativistic.[^ep-5-1-nonrelativistic] For example, the binding energy of hydrogen is $13.6\,\unit{\mathrm{eV}}$, whereas the rest energy of an electron is $511{,}000\,\unit{\mathrm{eV}}$—this is clearly a nonrelativistic system. On the other hand, quark-quark binding energies are on the order of a few hundred $\unit{\mathrm{MeV}}$, which is about the same as the effective rest energy of $u,d$, or $s$ quarks, but substantially less than $c,b$, and $t$ (see Table 4.4). So the light-quark hadrons are relativistic, but the heavy quark systems are not.

For most of this chapter we shall restrict our attention to nonrelativistic bound states of two particles. To establish the framework for this discussion I must first review some basic quantum mechanics.<sup>1</sup> The principal foundation for nonrelativistic quantum theory is Schrödinger's equation. I cannot pretend to *derive* the Schrödinger equation—any more than one can *derive* Newton's laws of motion—it is, after all, an *axiom* of the theory. But I can perhaps make it *plausible*, as follows. In classical mechanics the sum of the kinetic energy ($\tfrac{1}{2}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{v}^{2}=\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{2}/2\mechanical{m}$) and the potential energy ($\energy{V}(\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z})$) is a constant—the *total* energy ($\energy{E}$):[^ep-5-1-dissipative]

$$
\frac{1}{2\mechanical{m}}\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{2}+\energy{V}=\energy{E}
\tag{5.1}
$$

In quantum mechanics the momentum $\mechanical{\mathbf p}$ is replaced by the momentum *operator*

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf p}\to\frac{\energy{\hbar}}{i}\nabla
\quad\left(
\mechanical{p_x}\to\frac{\energy{\hbar}}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mechanical{x}},
\mechanical{p_y}\to\frac{\energy{\hbar}}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mechanical{y}},
\mechanical{p_z}\to\frac{\energy{\hbar}}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mechanical{z}}
\right)
\tag{5.2}
$$

[^ep-5-1-nonrelativistic]: In general, the total energy of a composite system is the sum of three terms: (i) the rest energy of the constituents, (ii) the kinetic energy of the constituents, and (iii) the potential energy of the configuration. The latter two are typically comparable in size (the precise relation is given by the virial theorem, which you can look up in any mechanics or quantum text). If the binding energy is *much less* than the constituent rest energies, so too is their kinetic energy, and hence the system is nonrelativistic. On the other hand, if the mass of the composite structure is substantially different from the sum of the rest masses of the constituents, the kinetic energy is large and the system is relativistic.

[^ep-5-1-dissipative]: In a dissipative system (say, one with friction), the mechanical energy is not conserved. But on the microscopic level there is no such thing as a dissipative force, and what may have looked like nonconservation of energy on the macroscopic scale is simply a conversion of energy into an unseen form (typically heat).

and energy by the operator

$$
\energy{E}\to i\energy{\hbar}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mechanical{t}}
\tag{5.3}
$$

Equation (5.1) becomes a collection of derivatives, which we take to act on the “wave function,” $\signal{\Psi}(\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z},\mechanical{t})$:

$$
\left(-\frac{\energy{\hbar}^{2}}{2\mechanical{m}}\nabla^2+\energy{V}\right)\signal{\Psi}
=i\energy{\hbar}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mechanical{t}}\signal{\Psi}
\tag{5.4}
$$

This is the (time-dependent) Schrödinger equation. The solution ($\signal{\Psi}$) describes a particle of mass $\mechanical{m}$ in the presence of a specified potential energy function $\energy{V}$. Specifically, $\lvert\signal{\Psi}(\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z},\mechanical{t})\rvert^2\,d\mechanical{x}\,d\mechanical{y}\,d\mechanical{z}$ is the probability of finding the particle in the volume element $d^3\mechanical{x}\equiv d\mechanical{x}\,d\mechanical{y}\,d\mechanical{z}$, at time $\mechanical{t}$. Since the particle must be *somewhere*, the integral of $\lvert\signal{\Psi}\rvert^2$ over *all* space has to be 1:

$$
\int\lvert\signal{\Psi}\rvert^2\,d^3\mechanical{x}=1
\tag{5.5}
$$

We say that the wave function is “normalized.”[^ep-5-1-normalization]

The Schrödinger equation can be solved by “separation of variables.” To begin with, we look for solutions that are simple *products* of a function of position, $\signal{\psi}(\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z})$, and a function of time, $\signal{f}(\mechanical{t})$:[^ep-5-1-separation]

$$
\signal{\Psi}(\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z},\mechanical{t})
=\signal{\psi}(\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z})\signal{f}(\mechanical{t})
\tag{5.6}
$$

With this ansatz, equation (5.4) can be written

$$
\frac{1}{\signal{\psi}}\left(-\frac{\energy{\hbar}^{2}}{2\mechanical{m}}\nabla^2+\energy{V}\right)\signal{\psi}
=\frac{i\energy{\hbar}}{\signal{f}}\frac{d\signal{f}}{d\mechanical{t}}
\tag{5.7}
$$

The left side depends only on position, the right side depends only on time; the only way this equation can hold for all $\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z}$, and $\mechanical{t}$ is if both sides are in fact *constant*. Physically, this “separation constant” represents the total energy of the particle, so we call it $\energy{E}$:

$$
\left(-\frac{\energy{\hbar}^{2}}{2\mechanical{m}}\nabla^2+\energy{V}\right)\signal{\psi}
=\energy{E}\signal{\psi}
\tag{5.8}
$$

$$
i\energy{\hbar}\frac{d\signal{f}}{d\mechanical{t}}=\energy{E}\signal{f}
\tag{5.9}
$$

The second of these is easy to solve:[^ep-5-1-time-constant]

[^ep-5-1-normalization]: Notice that a solution to the Schrödinger equation (5.4) can be multiplied by any constant and still remain a solution. In practice, we fix this constant by demanding that equation (5.5) be satisfied; this process is called “normalizing” the wave function.

[^ep-5-1-separation]: Of course, *most* solutions to the Schrödinger equation do *not* have this form. For a justification of the method of separation of variables, see, for example, reference 2, Section 3.3.

[^ep-5-1-time-constant]: The general solution to equation (5.9) includes an overall multiplicative constant. However, since we're going to multiply $\signal{f}$ by $\signal{\psi}$ [eq. (5.6)], we may as well absorb the constant into $\signal{\psi}$ and keep $\signal{f}$ as simple as possible.

<!-- PDF page 154; printed page 146 -->

$$
\signal{f}(\mechanical{t})=e^{-i\energy{E}\mechanical{t}/\energy{\hbar}}
\tag{5.10}
$$

The first [eq. (5.8)] is called the *time-independent* Schrödinger equation. The operator on the left is known as the “Hamiltonian”:

$$
\energy{H}\equiv-\frac{\energy{\hbar}^{2}}{2\mechanical{m}}\nabla^2+\energy{V}
\tag{5.11}
$$

and the (time-independent) Schrödinger equation has the form of an eigenvalue equation:

$$
\energy{H}\signal{\psi}=\energy{E}\signal{\psi}
\tag{5.12}
$$

$\signal{\psi}$ is an eigenfunction of $\energy{H}$, and $\energy{E}$ is the eigenvalue. Evidently, the complete wave function for a particle of mass $\mechanical{m}$ and energy $\energy{E}$, under the influence of a potential energy $\energy{V}(\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z})$, is

$$
\signal{\Psi}(\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z},\mechanical{t})
=\signal{\psi}(\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z})e^{-i\energy{E}\mechanical{t}/\energy{\hbar}}
\tag{5.13}
$$

where $\signal{\psi}$ satisfies equation (5.8).[^ep-5-1-complete]

In the case of a spherically symmetrical (or “central”) potential, $\energy{V}$ is a function only of the distance from the origin, and we adopt the usual spherical coordinates $(\mechanical{r},\mechanical{\theta},\mechanical{\phi})$, in which the *Laplacian*, $\nabla^2\equiv\partial^2/\partial\mechanical{x}^2+\partial^2/\partial\mechanical{y}^2+\partial^2/\partial\mechanical{z}^2$ takes the form<sup>2</sup>

$$
\nabla^2=
\frac{1}{\mechanical{r}^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mechanical{r}}\left(\mechanical{r}^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mechanical{r}}\right)
+\frac{1}{\mechanical{r}^{2}\sin\mechanical{\theta}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mechanical{\theta}}\left(\sin\mechanical{\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mechanical{\theta}}\right)
+\frac{1}{\mechanical{r}^{2}\sin^2\mechanical{\theta}}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\mechanical{\phi}^{2}}
\tag{5.14}
$$

The time-independent Schrödinger equation can now be solved by further separation of variables. Writing

$$
\signal{\psi}(\mechanical{r},\mechanical{\theta},\mechanical{\phi})
=\signal{R}(\mechanical{r})\signal{\Theta}(\mechanical{\theta})\signal{\Phi}(\mechanical{\phi})
\tag{5.15}
$$

equation (5.8) reduces to three (ordinary) differential equations for the functions $\signal{R},\signal{\Theta}$, and $\signal{\Phi}$:

$$
\frac{1}{\mechanical{r}^{2}}\frac{d}{d\mechanical{r}}\left(\mechanical{r}^{2}\frac{d\signal{R}}{d\mechanical{r}}\right)
=\left[\frac{\mechanical{l}(\mechanical{l}+1)}{\mechanical{r}^{2}}
+\frac{2\mechanical{m}}{\energy{\hbar}^{2}}(\energy{V}(\mechanical{r})-\energy{E})\right]\signal{R}
\tag{5.16}
$$

$$
\sin\mechanical{\theta}\frac{d}{d\mechanical{\theta}}\left(\sin\mechanical{\theta}\frac{d\signal{\Theta}}{d\mechanical{\theta}}\right)
=\left[\mechanical{m_l}^{2}-\mechanical{l}(\mechanical{l}+1)\sin^2\mechanical{\theta}\right]\signal{\Theta}
\tag{5.17}
$$

$$
\frac{d^2\signal{\Phi}}{d\mechanical{\phi}^{2}}=-\mechanical{m_l}^{2}\signal{\Phi}
\tag{5.18}
$$

The separation constants, $\mechanical{l}$ and $\mechanical{m_l}$, have been aptly chosen, for they correspond precisely to the orbital angular momentum quantum numbers introduced in Chapter 4. The solution to equation (5.18) is easy:[^ep-5-1-azimuth]

[^ep-5-1-complete]: Notice that $\lvert\signal{\Psi}\rvert^2=\lvert\signal{\psi}\rvert^2$. For most purposes it is only the absolute square of the wave function that matters, and we shall work almost exclusively with $\signal{\psi}$. Casually, we often refer to $\signal{\psi}$ as “the wave function,” but remember that the *complete* wave function carries the exponential time dependence.

[^ep-5-1-azimuth]: The second (linearly independent) solution, $e^{-i\mechanical{m_l}\mechanical{\phi}}$, is covered by letting $\mechanical{m_l}$ run to negative values. We could use $\sin(\mechanical{m_l}\mechanical{\phi})$ and $\cos(\mechanical{m_l}\mechanical{\phi})$ instead, and in electrostatics (where the same problem arises in solving Laplace's equation) we *would*, since the potential must be real. But there is no such constraint on $\signal{\psi}$, and in quantum mechanics the exponential form (5.19) is preferable, because, unlike the sine and cosine, it is an eigenstate of $\mechanical{L_z}$. Notice that $\mechanical{m_l}$ must be an integer, in order that $\signal{\Phi}(\mechanical{\phi}+2\pi)$ will equal $\signal{\Phi}(\mechanical{\phi})$; after all, $\mechanical{\phi}$ and $\mechanical{\phi}+2\pi$ describe the same geometrical point.

<!-- PDF page 155; printed page 147 -->

$$
\signal{\Phi}(\mechanical{\phi})=e^{i\mechanical{m_l}\mechanical{\phi}}
\tag{5.19}
$$

Solutions to equation (5.17) are less familiar; they are the so-called *associated Legendre functions*:<sup>3</sup>

$$
\signal{\Theta}(\mechanical{\theta})=P_l^{m_l}(\cos\mechanical{\theta})
\tag{5.20}
$$

where (in case you're interested)[^ep-5-1-legendre]

$$
P_l^{m_l}(z)=\frac{1}{2^{\mechanical{l}}\mechanical{l}!}
(1-z^2)^{\mechanical{m_l}/2}
\left(\frac{d}{dz}\right)^{(\mechanical{l}+\mechanical{m_l})}(z^2-1)^{\mechanical{l}}
\tag{5.21}
$$

Actually, it is customary to combine $\signal{\Theta}$ and $\signal{\Phi}$, with a conventional normalization factor, to form *spherical harmonics*:[^ep-5-1-negative-m]

$$
Y_l^{m_l}(\mechanical{\theta},\mechanical{\phi})
=\sqrt{\frac{(2\mechanical{l}+1)}{4\pi}\frac{(\mechanical{l}-\mechanical{m_l})!}{(\mechanical{l}+\mechanical{m_l})!}}
(-1)^{\mechanical{m_l}}P_l^{m_l}(\cos\mechanical{\theta})e^{i\mechanical{m_l}\mechanical{\phi}}
\tag{5.22}
$$

Spherical harmonics are tabulated in many places (including the Particle Data Booklet); a few of the more useful ones are given in Table 5.1.

Please don't let the complicated-looking formulas distract you. I include them mainly for completeness, but also to demonstrate that there is nothing *mysterious* about the angular part of the wave function; it's just that it involves functions that are not terribly familiar. Notice, however, that in spite of the fancy *names*, they're only combinations of sines, cosines, and exponentials. Our real concern is not with the angular dependence at all, but rather with equation (5.16), which carries the sole reference to the specific potential. We can simplify the situation slightly by introducing a new function:

$$
\signal{u}(\mechanical{r})\equiv\mechanical{r}\signal{R}(\mechanical{r})
\tag{5.23}
$$

in terms of which equation (5.16) becomes

$$
-\frac{\energy{\hbar}^{2}}{2\mechanical{m}}\frac{d^2\signal{u}}{d\mechanical{r}^{2}}
+\left[\energy{V}(\mechanical{r})+\frac{\energy{\hbar}^{2}}{2\mechanical{m}}
\frac{\mechanical{l}(\mechanical{l}+1)}{\mechanical{r}^{2}}\right]\signal{u}
=\energy{E}\signal{u}
\tag{5.24}
$$

We call this the “radial Schrödinger equation”; curiously enough, it has exactly the same form as equation (5.8) for one dimension, except that the potential is augmented by the so-called “centrifugal barrier,” $(\energy{\hbar}^{2}/2\mechanical{m})\mechanical{l}(\mechanical{l}+1)/\mechanical{r}^{2}$.

Equation (5.24) is about as far as we can pursue the matter in general terms; at this point we have to put in the particular potential $\energy{V}(\mechanical{r})$ for the problem at hand. The strategy then will be to solve the radial equation for $\signal{u}(\mechanical{r})$ and combine the result with the appropriate spherical harmonic to get the full wave function $\signal{\psi}$. In the course of solving the radial equation, however, we shall discover that only certain special values of $\energy{E}$ lead to acceptable results. For most values of $\energy{E}$ the solution to equation (5.24) blows up at large $\mechanical{r}$, and yields a nonnormalizable wave function. Such a solution does not represent a possible physical state. This rather technical detail is the source of the most striking and important feature of quantum mechanics: a bound system cannot have just any old energy (as it could classically); instead, the energy can take on only certain specific values, the so-called “allowed energies” of the system. Indeed, our real concern is not with the wave function itself, but with the *spectrum* of allowed energies.

[^ep-5-1-legendre]: Notice that equation (5.21) makes sense only if $\mechanical{l}$, like $\mechanical{m_l}$, is an integer, and gives zero if $\mechanical{m_l}>\mechanical{l}$. There exist solutions to equation (5.17) for other values of $\mechanical{l}$ and $\mechanical{m_l}$, but they do not yield normalizable wave functions.

[^ep-5-1-negative-m]: This assumes $\mechanical{m_l}\ge0$; for $\mechanical{m_l}<0$, we use $Y_l^{m_l}=(-1)^{\mechanical{m_l}}(Y_l^{-m_l})^*$.
