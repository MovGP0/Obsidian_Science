> [!TLDR]
> Quantum measurements give discrete orbital and spin angular momenta; integer and half-integer spin distinguish bosons from fermions.

<!-- PDF page 115; printed page 107 -->

The earth, in its motion, carries two kinds of angular momentum: *orbital* angular momentum, $\mechanical{r}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{v}$, associated with its annual revolution around the sun, and *spin* angular momentum, $\mechanical{I}\mechanical{\omega}$, associated with its daily rotation about the north–south axis. The same goes for the electron in a hydrogen atom: It too carries both orbital and spin angular momentum. In the macroscopic case the distinction is not terribly profound; after all, the “spin” angular momentum of the earth is nothing but the sum total of the “orbital” angular momenta of all the rocks and dirt clods that make it up, in their daily “orbit” around the axis. In the case of the electron this interpretation is not open to us: The electron, as far as we know, is a true point particle; its spin angular momentum is not attributable to constituent parts revolving about an axis, but is simply an intrinsic property of the particle itself (see Problem 4.8).

<!-- PDF page 116; printed page 108 -->

Classically, we are free to measure all three components of the orbital angular momentum vector, $\mechanical{\mathbf L}=\mechanical{\mathbf r}\times\mechanical{m}\mechanical{\mathbf v}$, to any desired accuracy, and these components can assume any values whatever. In quantum-mechanics, however, it is *impossible in principle* to measure all three components simultaneously; a measurement of $\mechanical{L_x}$, say, inevitably alters the value of $\mechanical{L_y}$, by an unpredictable amount. The best we can do is to measure the *magnitude* of $\mechanical{\mathbf L}$ (or rather, its *square*: $\mechanical{L}^{2}=\mechanical{\mathbf L}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf L}$) together with one component (which we customarily take to be the $\mechanical{z}$ component, $\mechanical{L_z}$). Furthermore, these measurements can only return certain “allowed” values.[^ep-4-2-quantization] Specifically, a measurement of $\mechanical{L}^{2}$ always yields a number of the form

$$
\mechanical{l}(\mechanical{l}+1)\energy{\hbar}^{2}
\tag{4.2}
$$

where $\mechanical{l}$ is a nonnegative integer:

$$
\mechanical{l}=0,1,2,3,\ldots
\tag{4.3}
$$

For a given value of $\mechanical{l}$, a measurement of $\mechanical{L_z}$ always gives a result of the form

$$
\mechanical{m_l}\energy{\hbar}
\tag{4.4}
$$

where $\mechanical{m_l}$ is an integer in the range $[-\mechanical{l},+\mechanical{l}]$:

$$
\mechanical{m_l}=-\mechanical{l},-\mechanical{l}+1,\ldots,-1,0,+1,\ldots,\mechanical{l}-1,\mechanical{l}
\tag{4.5}
$$

[$(2\mechanical{l}+1)$ possibilities in all]. Figure 4.3 may help you to visualize the situation. Here $\mechanical{l}=2$, so the magnitude of $\mechanical{\mathbf L}$ is $\sqrt{6}\energy{\hbar}=2.45\energy{\hbar}$; $\mechanical{L_z}$ can assume the values $2\energy{\hbar}$, $\energy{\hbar}$, $0$, $-\energy{\hbar}$, or $-2\energy{\hbar}$. Notice that the angular momentum vector cannot be oriented purely in the $\mechanical{z}$ direction.

The same goes for spin angular momentum: A measurement of $\mechanical{S}^{2}=\mechanical{\mathbf S}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf S}$ can only return values of the form

$$
\mechanical{s}(\mechanical{s}+1)\energy{\hbar}^{2}
\tag{4.6}
$$

In the case of spin, however, the quantum number $\mechanical{s}$ can be a *half-integer* as well as an integer:

$$
\mechanical{s}=0,\tfrac{1}{2},1,\tfrac{3}{2},2,\tfrac{5}{2},\ldots
\tag{4.7}
$$

[^ep-4-2-quantization]: I am not going to prove the quantization rules for angular momentum, and if this material is new to you, I suggest that you consult a textbook on quantum mechanics. All I propose to do here is summarize the essential results we will need in what follows.

For a given value of $\mechanical{s}$, a measurement of $\mechanical{S_z}$ must yield an answer of the form

$$
\mechanical{m_s}\energy{\hbar}
\tag{4.8}
$$

where $\mechanical{m_s}$ is an integer or half-integer (whichever $\mechanical{s}$ is) in the range $[-\mechanical{s},\mechanical{s}]$:

$$
\mechanical{m_s}=-\mechanical{s},-\mechanical{s}+1,\ldots,\mechanical{s}-1,\mechanical{s}
\tag{4.9}
$$

[$(2\mechanical{s}+1)$ possibilities].

Now, a given particle can be put into any *orbital* angular momentum state you wish, but for each type of particle the value of $\mechanical{s}$ is fixed. Every pion or kaon, for example, has $\mechanical{s}=0$; every electron, proton, neutron, and quark carries $\mechanical{s}=\tfrac{1}{2}$; for the $\rho$, the $\psi$, the photon, and the gluon, $\mechanical{s}=1$; for the $\Delta$'s and the $\Omega^-$, $\mechanical{s}=\tfrac{3}{2}$; and so on. We call $\mechanical{s}$ the “spin” of the particle. Particles with half-integer spin are known as *fermions*—all baryons, leptons, and quarks are fermions; particles with integer spin are known as *bosons*—all mesons and mediators are bosons (see Table 4.3).

**Table 4.3** Classification of particles by spin

| Particle structure | Bosons (integer spin): Spin 0 | Bosons (integer spin): Spin 1 | Fermions (½-integer spin): Spin ½ | Fermions (½-integer spin): Spin ³⁄₂ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Elementary | — | Mediators | Quarks, leptons | — |
| Composite | Pseudo-scalar mesons | Vector mesons | Baryon octet | Baryon decuplet |

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\mechanical{r},\mechanical{\mathbf r}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{m}}$ | Orbital radius and position | The radius and position vector in orbital angular momentum. |
| $\mechanical{m}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{kg}}$ | Mass | The mass in the classical orbital angular momentum. |
| $\mechanical{v},\mechanical{\mathbf v}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}}$ | Orbital velocity | The speed and velocity vector in orbital motion. |
| $\mechanical{I}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{kg\,m^{2}}}$ | Moment of inertia | The inertia used in the classical spin angular momentum. |
| $\mechanical{\omega}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}}$ | Angular velocity | The rotation rate of the earth. |
| $\mechanical{\mathbf L},\mechanical{L_x},\mechanical{L_y},\mechanical{L_z}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{J\,s}}$ | Orbital angular momentum | The angular momentum vector and its components. |

| $\mechanical{\mathbf S},\mechanical{S_z}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{J\,s}}$ | Spin angular momentum | The intrinsic angular momentum and its measured component. |
| $\mechanical{S}^{2}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{J^{2}\,s^{2}}}$ | Squared spin angular momentum | The measurable square of the spin magnitude. |
| $\mechanical{l}$ | $1$ | Orbital quantum number | The nonnegative integer that sets the orbital angular momentum magnitude. |
| $\mechanical{m_l}$ | $1$ | Orbital projection quantum number | The integer that sets the measured orbital component. |
