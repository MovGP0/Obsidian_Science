> [!TLDR]
> Spinors and Pauli matrices describe spin-½ measurements, their probabilities, and the change of spin states under rotations.

The most important spin system is $\mechanical{s}=\tfrac{1}{2}$; the proton, neutron, electron, all quarks, and all leptons carry spin $\tfrac{1}{2}$. Furthermore, once you understand the formalism for $\mechanical{s}=\tfrac{1}{2}$, any other case is a relatively simple matter to work out. So I'll pause here to develop the theory of spin $\tfrac{1}{2}$ in some detail. A particle with spin $\tfrac{1}{2}$ can have $\mechanical{m_s}=\tfrac{1}{2}$ (“spin up”) or $\mechanical{m_s}=-\tfrac{1}{2}$ (“spin down”). Informally, we represent these two states by arrows: $\uparrow$ and $\downarrow$. But a better notation is afforded by two-component column vectors, or *spinors*:

$$
\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;-\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}
\tag{4.18}
$$

It is often said that a particle of spin $\tfrac{1}{2}$ can only exist in one or the other of these two states, but that is quite false. The most general state of a spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ particle is the *linear combination*

$$
\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix}
=\alpha\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}
+\beta\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}
\tag{4.19}
$$

where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are two complex numbers. It is true that a *measurement* of $\mechanical{S_z}$ can only return the value $+\tfrac{1}{2}\energy{\hbar}$ or $-\tfrac{1}{2}\energy{\hbar}$, but the first outcome, say, does not prove that the particle was in the state $\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}$ prior to the measurement. In the general case $\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix}$, $\lvert\alpha\rvert^2$ is the *probability* that a measurement of $\mechanical{S_z}$ would yield the value $+\tfrac{1}{2}\energy{\hbar}$, and $\lvert\beta\rvert^2$ is the probability of getting $-\tfrac{1}{2}\energy{\hbar}$. Since these are the only allowed results, it follows that

$$
\lvert\alpha\rvert^2+\lvert\beta\rvert^2=1
\tag{4.20}
$$

Apart from this “normalization” condition, there is no a priori constraint on the numbers $\alpha$ and $\beta$.

Suppose now that we are to measure $\mechanical{S_x}$ or $\mechanical{S_y}$ on a particle in the state $\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix}$. What results might we get, and what is the probability of each? Symmetry dictates that the allowed values be $\pm\tfrac{1}{2}\energy{\hbar}$; after all, it's perfectly arbitrary which direction we choose to call $\mechanical{z}$ in the first place. But determining the probabilities is not so simple. To each component of $\mechanical{\mathbf S}$ we associate a $2\times2$ matrix:[^ep-4-4-derivation]

[^ep-4-4-derivation]: Again, the *derivation* of these matrices will be found in any quantum-mechanics text. My purpose here is to show you *how* angular momentum is handled in particle physics, not to explain *why* it is done this way.

<!-- PDF page 122; printed page 114 -->

$$
\mechanical{\widehat S_x}=\frac{\energy{\hbar}}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},
\quad
\mechanical{\widehat S_y}=\frac{\energy{\hbar}}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},
\quad
\mechanical{\widehat S_z}=\frac{\energy{\hbar}}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}
\tag{4.21}
$$

The eigenvalues of $\mechanical{\widehat S_x}$ are $\pm\energy{\hbar}/2$, and corresponding normalized eigenvectors are[^ep-4-4-eigenvector]

$$
\chi_\pm=\begin{pmatrix}1/\sqrt2\\\pm1/\sqrt2\end{pmatrix}
\tag{4.22}
$$

(see Problem 4.15). An arbitrary spinor $\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix}$ can be written as a linear combination of these eigenvectors:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix}
=a\begin{pmatrix}1/\sqrt2\\1/\sqrt2\end{pmatrix}
+b\begin{pmatrix}1/\sqrt2\\-1/\sqrt2\end{pmatrix}
\tag{4.23}
$$

where

$$
a=(1/\sqrt2)(\alpha+\beta);\qquad b=(1/\sqrt2)(\alpha-\beta)
\tag{4.24}
$$

The probability that a measurement of $\mechanical{S_x}$ will yield the value $\tfrac{1}{2}\energy{\hbar}$ is $\lvert a\rvert^2$; the probability of getting $-\tfrac{1}{2}\energy{\hbar}$ is $\lvert b\rvert^2$. Evidently, $\lvert a\rvert^2+\lvert b\rvert^2=1$ (see Problem 4.16).

The general procedure, of which this was a particular instance, is as follows:

1. Construct the matrix, $\widehat A$, representing the observable $A$ in question.
2. The allowed values of $A$ are the eigenvalues of $\widehat A$.
3. Write the state of the system as a linear combination of eigenvectors of $\widehat A$; the absolute square of the coefficient of the $i$th eigenvector is the probability that a measurement of $A$ would yield the $i$th eigenvalue.

### Example 4.5

Suppose we measure $\mechanical{S_x}^{2}$ on a particle in the state $\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix}$. What values might we get, and what is the probability of each?

*Solution.* The matrix representing $\mechanical{S_x}^{2}$ would be the square of the matrix representing $\mechanical{S_x}$:

$$
\mechanical{\widehat S_x}^{2}=\frac{\energy{\hbar}^{2}}{4}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
\tag{4.25}
$$

[^ep-4-4-eigenvector]: A nonzero column matrix
    
    $$
    \chi=\begin{pmatrix}a_1\\a_2\\\vdots\\a_n\end{pmatrix}
    $$
    
    is called an *eigenvector* of a given $n\times n$ matrix $M$ if
    
    $$
    M\chi=\lambda\chi
    $$
    
    for some number $\lambda$ (the *eigenvalue*). Notice that any multiple of $\chi$ is still an eigenvector, with the same eigenvalue.

<!-- PDF page 123; printed page 115 -->

Since

$$
\frac{\energy{\hbar}^{2}}{4}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix}
=\frac{\energy{\hbar}^{2}}{4}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix}
$$

*every* spinor is an eigenvector of $\mechanical{\widehat S_x}^{2}$, with eigenvalue $\energy{\hbar}^{2}/4$. Thus we would be *certain* to get $\energy{\hbar}^{2}/4$ (probability 1). The same goes for $\mechanical{\widehat S_y}^{2}$ and $\mechanical{\widehat S_z}^{2}$, so every spinor is an eigenstate of $\mechanical{\widehat S}^{2}=\mechanical{\widehat S_x}^{2}+\mechanical{\widehat S_y}^{2}+\mechanical{\widehat S_z}^{2}$, with eigenvalue $3\energy{\hbar}^{2}/4$. This should come as no surprise—in general, for spin $\mechanical{s}$ we must have $\mechanical{S}^{2}=\mechanical{s}(\mechanical{s}+1)\energy{\hbar}^{2}$.

For mathematical purposes the factor of $\energy{\hbar}/2$ in equation (4.21) is ugly, and it is customary to introduce the *Pauli spin matrices*:

$$
\sigma_x=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},
\quad
\sigma_y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},
\quad
\sigma_z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}
\tag{4.26}
$$

so that $\mechanical{\widehat{\mathbf S}}=(\energy{\hbar}/2)\boldsymbol\sigma$. The Pauli matrices have many interesting properties, some of which are explored in Problems 4.19 and 4.20. We shall encounter them repeatedly in the course of this book.

In a sense, spinors (two-component objects) occupy an intermediate position between scalars (one component) and vectors (three components). Now, when you rotate your coordinate axes, the components of a vector change, in a prescribed manner (see Problem 4.6), and we might inquire how the components of a *spinor* transform, under the same circumstances. The answer<sup>4</sup> is provided by the following rule:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}\alpha'\\\beta'\end{pmatrix}
=U(\mechanical{\boldsymbol\theta})\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix}
\tag{4.27}
$$

where $U(\mechanical{\boldsymbol\theta})$ is the $2\times2$ matrix

$$
U(\mechanical{\boldsymbol\theta})=e^{-i\mechanical{\boldsymbol\theta}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2}
\tag{4.28}
$$

The vector $\mechanical{\boldsymbol\theta}$ points along the axis of rotation, and its magnitude is the angle of rotation, in the right-hand sense, about that axis. Notice that the exponent here is itself a matrix (!). An expression of this form is to be interpreted as shorthand for the power series:

$$
e^A\equiv1+A+\tfrac{1}{2}A^2+\tfrac{1}{3!}A^3+\cdots
\tag{4.29}
$$

(see Problem 4.21).[^ep-4-4-exponential] As you can check for yourself (Problem 4.22), $U(\mechanical{\boldsymbol\theta})$ is a unitary matrix of determinant 1; in fact, the set of all such rotation matrices constitutes the group $SU(2)$. Thus spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ particles transform under rotations according to the fundamental, two-dimensional representation of $SU(2)$. Similarly, particles of spin 1, described by vectors, belong to the three-dimensional representation of $SU(2)$; spin-$\tfrac{3}{2}$ particles, described by a four-component object, transform under the four-dimensional representation of $SU(2)$; and so on. (The construction of these higher-dimensional representations is explored in Problem 4.23.) You're probably wondering what $SU(2)$ has to do with rotations; well, as I mentioned earlier, $SU(2)$ is essentially[^ep-4-4-double-cover] the same group as $SO(3)$, the group of rotations in three dimensions. Particles of different spin, then, belong to different *representations* of the rotation group.

[^ep-4-4-exponential]: *Beware:* For matrices it is not the case that $e^Ae^B=e^{A+B}$ in general. You might want to check this by using the matrices in Problem 4.21. However, the usual rule *does* apply if $A$ and $B$ *commute* (i.e., if $AB=BA$).

<!-- PDF page 124; printed page 116 -->

[^ep-4-4-double-cover]: There is actually a subtle distinction between $SU(2)$ and $SO(3)$. According to Problem 4.21, the matrix $U$ for rotation through an angle of $2\pi$ is $-1$; a spinor *changes sign* under such a rotation. And yet, *geometrically*, a rotation through $2\pi$ is equivalent to no rotation at all. $SU(2)$ is a kind of “double” version of $SO(3)$, in which you don't come back to the beginning until you've turned through $720^\circ$. In this sense spinor representations of $SU(2)$ are not true representations of the rotation group, and that's why they do not appear in classical physics. In quantum mechanics only the *square* of the wave function carries physical significance, and in the squaring the minus sign goes away.
