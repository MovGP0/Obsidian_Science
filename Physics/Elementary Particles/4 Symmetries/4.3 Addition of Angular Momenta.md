> [!TLDR]
> Angular momentum addition gives allowed total spins and Clebsch–Gordan decompositions, including the triplet and singlet states.

<!-- PDF page 117; printed page 109 -->

Angular momentum states are labeled with a “ket”: $\lvert\mechanical{l}\mechanical{m_l}\rangle$ or $\lvert\mechanical{s}\mechanical{m_s}\rangle$. Thus if I say the electron in a hydrogen atom occupies the orbital state $\lvert3\;-1\rangle$ and the spin state $\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle$, I mean that $\mechanical{l}=3$, $\mechanical{m_l}=-1$, $\mechanical{s}=\tfrac{1}{2}$ (which is unnecessary, of course; if it's an electron, $\mechanical{s}$ *must* be $\tfrac{1}{2}$), and $\mechanical{m_s}=\tfrac{1}{2}$. Now, it may happen that we are not interested in the spin and orbital angular momenta separately, but rather in the *total* angular momentum, $\mechanical{\mathbf J}=\mechanical{\mathbf L}+\mechanical{\mathbf S}$. (In the presence of coupling between $\mechanical{\mathbf L}$ and $\mechanical{\mathbf S}$—tidal, if it's the earth–sun system; magnetic, for the electron–proton system—it is $\mechanical{\mathbf J}$, and not $\mechanical{\mathbf L}$ and $\mechanical{\mathbf S}$ individually, that will be conserved.) Or perhaps we are studying the two quarks that go to make a $\psi$ meson; in this case, as we shall see, the *orbital* angular momentum is zero, but we are confronted with the problem of combining the two quark spins to get the total spin of the $\psi$: $\mechanical{\mathbf S}=\mechanical{\mathbf S_1}+\mechanical{\mathbf S_2}$. In either case the question arises: *How do we add two angular momenta*

<!-- PDF page 118; printed page 110 -->

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf J}=\mechanical{\mathbf J_1}+\mechanical{\mathbf J_2}
\tag{4.10}
$$

Classically, of course, we just add the components. But in quantum mechanics we do not have access to all three components; we are obliged to work with one component and the *magnitude*. So the question becomes: If we combine states $\lvert\mechanical{j_1}\mechanical{m_1}\rangle$ and $\lvert\mechanical{j_2}\mechanical{m_2}\rangle$, what *total* angular momentum state(s) $\lvert\mechanical{j}\mechanical{m}\rangle$ do we get? The $\mechanical{z}$ components still add, naturally, so

$$
\mechanical{m}=\mechanical{m_1}+\mechanical{m_2}
\tag{4.11}
$$

but the magnitudes do *not*; it all depends on the relative orientation of $\mechanical{\mathbf J_1}$ and $\mechanical{\mathbf J_2}$ (Fig. 4.4). If they are parallel the magnitudes add, but if they are antiparallel the magnitudes subtract; in general, the magnitude of the vector sum is somewhere between these extremes. As it turns out, we get *every $\mechanical{j}$ from* $(\mechanical{j_1}+\mechanical{j_2})$ down to $\lvert\mechanical{j_1}-\mechanical{j_2}\rvert$, in integer steps:<sup>3</sup>

$$
\mechanical{j}=\lvert\mechanical{j_1}-\mechanical{j_2}\rvert,
\lvert\mechanical{j_1}-\mechanical{j_2}\rvert+1,\ldots,
(\mechanical{j_1}+\mechanical{j_2})-1,(\mechanical{j_1}+\mechanical{j_2})
\tag{4.12}
$$

For instance, a particle of spin 1 in an orbital state $\mechanical{l}=3$ could have total angular momentum $\mechanical{j}=4$ (i.e., $\mechanical{J}^{2}=20\energy{\hbar}^{2}$), or $\mechanical{j}=3$ ($\mechanical{J}^{2}=12\energy{\hbar}^{2}$), or $\mechanical{j}=2$ ($\mechanical{J}^{2}=6\energy{\hbar}^{2}$).

### Example 4.1

A quark and an antiquark are bound together, in a state of zero orbital angular momentum, to form a meson. What are the possible values of the meson's spin?

*Solution.* Quarks carry spin $\tfrac{1}{2}$, so we can get $\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}=1$ or $\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}=0$. The spin-0 combination gives us the “pseudo-scalar” mesons, $\pi$'s, $K$'s, $\eta$, $\eta'$; “scalar” means spin 0; “pseudo-” will be explained shortly. The spin-1 combination gives the “vector” mesons, $\rho$'s, $K^*$'s, $\phi$, $\omega$; “vector” means spin 1.

To add *three* angular momenta, we combine two of them first, using equation (4.12), and then add on the third. Thus if we allow the quarks in Example 4.1 an orbital angular momentum $\mechanical{l}>0$, we get mesons with spin $\mechanical{l}+1$, $\mechanical{l}$, and $\mechanical{l}-1$. Because the orbital quantum number has to be an integer, all mesons carry integer spin (they're bosons). By the same token, all baryons (made up of *three* quarks) must have half-integer spin (they're fermions).

### Example 4.2

Suppose you combine three quarks in a state of zero orbital angular momentum. What are the possible spins of the resulting baryon?

*Solution.* From *two* quarks, each spin $\tfrac{1}{2}$, we get a total angular momentum of $\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}=1$ or $\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}=0$. Adding in the third quark yields $1+\tfrac{1}{2}=\tfrac{3}{2}$ or $1-\tfrac{1}{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}$ (when the first two add to 1), and $0+\tfrac{1}{2}=\tfrac{1}{2}$ (when the first two add to zero). Thus the baryon can have a spin of $\tfrac{3}{2}$ or $\tfrac{1}{2}$, and the latter can be achieved in two different ways. In practice, $\mechanical{s}=\tfrac{3}{2}$ is the decuplet, $\mechanical{s}=\tfrac{1}{2}$ is the octet, and evidently the quark model would allow for another family with $\mechanical{s}=\tfrac{1}{2}$. If we permit the quarks to revolve around one another, throwing in some *orbital* angular momentum, the number of possibilities increases accordingly.

Well, equation (4.12) tells us what total angular momenta $\mechanical{j}$ we can obtain by combining $\mechanical{j_1}$ and $\mechanical{j_2}$, but occasionally we require the explicit decomposition of $\lvert\mechanical{j_1}\mechanical{m_1}\rangle\lvert\mechanical{j_2}\mechanical{m_2}\rangle$ into states of total angular momentum $\lvert\mechanical{j}\mechanical{m}\rangle$:

$$
\lvert\mechanical{j_1}\mechanical{m_1}\rangle\lvert\mechanical{j_2}\mechanical{m_2}\rangle
=\sum_{\mechanical{j}=\lvert\mechanical{j_1}-\mechanical{j_2}\rvert}^{(\mechanical{j_1}+\mechanical{j_2})}
C^{j\;j_1\;j_2}_{m\;m_1\;m_2}\lvert\mechanical{j}\mechanical{m}\rangle,
\qquad\text{with }\mechanical{m}=\mechanical{m_1}+\mechanical{m_2}
\tag{4.13}
$$

The numbers $C^{j\;j_1\;j_2}_{m\;m_1\;m_2}$ are known as *Clebsch–Gordan coefficients*. A book on group theory or advanced quantum-mechanics will explain how to calculate them. In practice, we normally look them up in a table. (There is one in the Particle Data Booklet, and the case $\mechanical{j_1}=2$, $\mechanical{j_2}=\tfrac{1}{2}$ is reproduced in Figure 4.5). The Clebsch–Gordan coefficients give the probability of getting $\mechanical{j}(\mechanical{j}+1)\energy{\hbar}^{2}$, if we measure $\mechanical{J}^{2}$ (the total angular momentum squared) on a system consisting of two angular momentum states $\lvert\mechanical{j_1}\mechanical{m_1}\rangle$ and $\lvert\mechanical{j_2}\mechanical{m_2}\rangle$: The probability is the *square* of the corresponding Clebsch–Gordan coefficient.

### Example 4.3

The electron in a hydrogen atom occupies the orbital state $\lvert2\;-1\rangle$ and the spin state $\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle$. If we measure $\mechanical{J}^{2}$, what values might we get, and what is the probability of each?

*Solution.* The possible values of $\mechanical{j}$ are $\mechanical{l}+\mechanical{s}=2+\tfrac{1}{2}=\tfrac{5}{2}$ and $\mechanical{l}-\mechanical{s}=2-\tfrac{1}{2}=\tfrac{3}{2}$. The $\mechanical{z}$ components add: $\mechanical{m}=-1+\tfrac{1}{2}=-\tfrac{1}{2}$. We go to the Clebsch–Gordan table (Fig. 4.5) labeled $2\times\tfrac{1}{2}$, which indicates that we are combining $\mechanical{j_1}=2$ with $\mechanical{j_2}=\tfrac{1}{2}$, and look for the horizontal row labeled $-1,\tfrac{1}{2}$; these are the values of $\mechanical{m_1}$ and $\mechanical{m_2}$. Reading off the two entries, we find $\lvert2\;-1\rangle\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle=\sqrt{2/5}\lvert5/2\;-1/2\rangle-\sqrt{3/5}\lvert3/2\;-1/2\rangle$. So the probability of getting $\mechanical{j}=\tfrac{5}{2}$ is $\tfrac{2}{5}$, and the probability of getting $\mechanical{j}=\tfrac{3}{2}$ is $\tfrac{3}{5}$. Notice that the probabilities add to 1, as, of course, they *should*.

### Example 4.4

We know from Example 4.1 that two spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ states combine to give spin 1 and spin 0. Find the explicit Clebsch–Gordan decomposition for these states.

*Solution.* Consulting the $\tfrac{1}{2}\times\tfrac{1}{2}$ table, we find

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle&=\lvert1\;1\rangle\\
\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;-\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle&=(1/\sqrt2)\lvert1\;0\rangle+(1/\sqrt2)\lvert0\;0\rangle\\
\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;-\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle&=(1/\sqrt2)\lvert1\;0\rangle-(1/\sqrt2)\lvert0\;0\rangle\\
\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;-\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;-\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle&=\lvert1\;-1\rangle
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.14}
$$

Thus the three spin 1 states are

$$
\left.
\begin{aligned}
\lvert1\;1\rangle&=\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle\\
\lvert1\;0\rangle&=(1/\sqrt2)[\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;-\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle+\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;-\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle]\\
\lvert1\;-1\rangle&=\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;-\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;-\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle
\end{aligned}
\right\}
\tag{4.15}
$$

whereas the spin 0 state is

$$
\lvert0\;0\rangle=(1/\sqrt2)[\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;-\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle-\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;-\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\;\tfrac{1}{2}\rangle]
\tag{4.16}
$$

[By the way, equations (4.15) and (4.16) can be read directly off the Clebsch–Gordan table; the coefficients work both directions:

$$
\lvert\mechanical{j}\mechanical{m}\rangle
=\sum_{j_1,j_2}C^{j\;j_1\;j_2}_{m\;m_1\;m_2}
\lvert\mechanical{j_1}\mechanical{m_1}\rangle\lvert\mechanical{j_2}\mechanical{m_2}\rangle
\tag{4.17}
$$

This time we read down the columns, instead of along the rows.] The spin-1 combination is called the “triplet,” for obvious reasons, and spin 0 is called the “singlet.” For future reference, notice that the triplet is *symmetric* under interchange of the particles, $1\leftrightarrow2$, whereas the singlet is *antisymmetric* (that is, it changes sign). Incidentally, in a singlet state the spins are oppositely aligned (antiparallel); however, it is *not* the case that in a triplet state the spins are necessarily parallel; they are for $\mechanical{m}=1$ and $\mechanical{m}=-1$, but not for $\mechanical{m}=0$.

| $\mechanical{l},\mechanical{m_l}$ | $1$ | Orbital quantum numbers | The orbital magnitude and axial projection labels. |
| $\mechanical{s},\mechanical{m_s}$ | $1$ | Spin quantum numbers | The intrinsic spin and its axial projection label. |
