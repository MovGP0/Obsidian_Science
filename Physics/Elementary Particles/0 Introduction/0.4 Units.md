> [!TLDR]
> Particle physics uses electron volts for energy; this book retains c and ℏ and uses Gaussian units for charge.

<!-- PDF page 16; printed page 8 -->

Elementary particles are small, so for our purposes the *normal* mechanical units—grams, ergs, joules, and so on—are inconveniently large. Atomic physicists introduced the *electron volt*—the energy acquired by an electron when accelerated

<!-- PDF page 17; printed page 9 -->

through a potential difference of 1 volt: $1\,\unit{eV}=1.6\times10^{-19}\,\unit{J}$. For us the eV is inconveniently *small*, but we’re stuck with it. Nuclear physicists use keV ($10^3\,\unit{eV}$); typical energies in particle physics are MeV ($10^6\,\unit{eV}$), GeV ($10^9\,\unit{eV}$), or even TeV ($10^{12}\,\unit{eV}$). Momenta are measured in $\unit{MeV}/c$ (or $\unit{GeV}/c$, or whatever), and masses in $\unit{MeV}/c^2$. Thus the proton weighs $938\,\unit{MeV}/c^2=1.67\times10^{-24}\,\unit{g}$.

Actually, particle theorists are lazy (or clever, depending on your point of view)—they seldom include the $c$’s and $\hbar$’s ($\hbar\equiv h/2\pi$) in their formulas. You’re just supposed to fit them in for yourself at the end, to make the dimensions come out right. As they say in the business, “set $c=\hbar=1$.” This amounts to working in units such that time is measured in centimeters and mass and energy in inverse centimeters; the unit of time is the time it takes light to travel 1 centimeter, and the unit of energy is the energy of a photon whose wavelength is $2\pi$ centimeters. Only at the end of the problem do we revert to conventional units. This makes everything look very elegant, but I thought it would be wiser in this book to keep all the $c$’s and $\hbar$’s where they belong, so that you can check for dimensional consistency as you go along. (If this offends you, remember that it is easier for you to ignore an $\hbar$ you don’t like than for someone else to conjure one up in just the right place.)

Finally, there is the question of what units to use for electric charge. In introductory physics courses most instructors favor the *SI* system, in which charge is measured in *coulombs*, and Coulomb’s law reads

$$
\mechanical{F}=\frac{1}{4\pi\electric{\epsilon_0}}\frac{\electric{q_1}\electric{q_2}}{\mechanical{r}^2}\qquad\text{(SI)}
$$

Most advanced work is done in the *Gaussian* system, in which charge is measured in *electrostatic units* (esu), and Coulomb’s law is written

$$
\mechanical{F}=\frac{\electric{q_1}\electric{q_2}}{\mechanical{r}^2}\qquad\text{(G)}
$$

But elementary particle physicists prefer the *Heaviside–Lorentz* system, in which Coulomb’s law takes the form

$$
\mechanical{F}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{\electric{q_1}\electric{q_2}}{\mechanical{r}^2}\qquad\text{(HL)}
$$

The three units of charge are related as shown:

$$
\electric{q_{\mathrm{HL}}}=\sqrt{4\pi}\,\electric{q_{\mathrm{G}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\electric{\epsilon_0}}}\,\electric{q_{\mathrm{SI}}}
$$

In this book I shall use Gaussian units exclusively, in order to avoid unnecessary confusion in an already difficult subject. Whenever possible I will express results in terms of the *fine structure constant*

$$
\alpha=\frac{\electric{e}^2}{\hbar c}=\frac{1}{137}
$$

where $\electric{e}$ is the charge of the electron in Gaussian units. Most elementary particle

texts write this as $\electric{e}^2/4\pi$, because they are measuring charge in Heaviside–Lorentz units and setting $c=\hbar=1$; but everyone agrees that the *number* is $\frac{1}{137}$.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| — | $\unit{eV}$ | Energy | The energy gained by an electron across a potential difference of 1 volt. |
| — | $\unit{MeV}/c^2$ | Proton mass | The source gives the proton mass as 938 MeV/c². |
| $c$ | $\unit{m\,s^{-1}}$ | Speed of light | The conversion factor retained in the source formulas. |
| $h$ | $\unit{J\,s}$ | Planck constant | The constant used to define ℏ. |
| $\hbar$ | $\unit{J\,s}$ | Reduced Planck constant | Planck’s constant divided by 2π. |
| $\mechanical{F}$ | — | Electrostatic force | The force unit depends on the selected unit system. |
| $\electric{q_1},\electric{q_2}$ | — | Electric charges | The interacting charges in the selected unit system. |
| $\mechanical{r}$ | $\unit{m}$ or $\unit{cm}$ | Separation | The distance between the charges in SI or Gaussian units. |
| $\electric{\epsilon_0}$ | $\unit{F\,m^{-1}}$ | Vacuum permittivity | The constant in the SI form of Coulomb’s law. |
| $\electric{q_{\mathrm{SI}}}$ | $\unit{C}$ | SI charge | The charge value in SI units. |
| $\electric{q_{\mathrm{G}}}$ | $\unit{esu}$ | Gaussian charge | The charge value in Gaussian units. |
| $\electric{q_{\mathrm{HL}}}$ | — | Heaviside–Lorentz charge | The charge value in the rationalized unit system. |
| $\electric{e}$ | $\unit{esu}$ | Electron charge | The electron charge used to form the fine structure constant. |
| $\alpha$ | $1$ | Fine structure constant | The dimensionless electromagnetic coupling constant. |
