> [!TLDR]
> Relativistic field Lagrangians give the Klein–Gordon, Dirac, Proca, and Maxwell equations through the Euler–Lagrange equations.

<!-- PDF page 352; printed page 344 -->

A *particle*, by its nature, is a *localized* entity; in classical particle mechanics we are typically interested in calculating its *position* as a function of time: $\mechanical{x}(\mechanical{t})$, $\mechanical{y}(\mechanical{t})$, $\mechanical{z}(\mechanical{t})$. A *field*, on the other hand, occupies some *region* of space; in field theory our concern is to calculate one or more *functions of position and time*: $\signal{\phi_i}(\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z},\mechanical{t})$. The field variables $\signal{\phi_i}$ might be, for example, the temperature at each point in a room, or the electric potential $\electric{V}$, or the three components of the magnetic field $\magnetic{\mathbf B}$. In particle mechanics we introduced a Lagrangian $\energy{L}$ that was a function of the coordinates $\mechanical{q_i}$ and their time derivatives, $\mechanical{\dot q_i}$; in field theory we start with a Lagrangian (technically, a Lagrangian *density*) $\energy{\mathcal L}$, which is a function of the fields $\signal{\phi_i}$ and their $\mechanical{x}$, $\mechanical{y}$, $\mechanical{z}$, and $\mechanical{t}$ derivatives:

$$
\partial_\mu\signal{\phi_i}\equiv\frac{\partial\signal{\phi_i}}{\partial\mechanical{x^\mu}}
\tag{11.9}
$$

<!-- PDF page 353; printed page 345 -->

In the former case, the left side of the Euler–Lagrange equation (11.6) involves only time derivatives; a *relativistic* theory must treat space and time coordinates on an equal footing, and the Euler–Lagrange equations generalize as you might expect:

$$
\partial_\mu\left(\frac{\partial\energy{\mathcal L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu\signal{\phi_i})}\right)
=\frac{\partial\energy{\mathcal L}}{\partial\signal{\phi_i}}
\qquad(i=1,2,3,\ldots)
\tag{11.10}
$$

## Example 11.1 The Klein–Gordon Lagrangian for a Scalar (Spin-0) Field

Suppose we have a single, scalar field variable $\signal{\phi}$, and the Lagrangian is

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}
=\frac12(\partial_\mu\signal{\phi})(\partial^\mu\signal{\phi})
-\frac12\left(\frac{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}}{\energy{\hbar}}\right)^2\signal{\phi}^2
\tag{11.11}
$$

In this case

$$
\frac{\partial\energy{\mathcal L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu\signal{\phi})}
=\partial^\mu\signal{\phi}
\tag{11.12}
$$

(If this confuses you, write out the Lagrangian “longhand”:

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}
=\frac12[\partial_0\signal{\phi}\,\partial_0\signal{\phi}
-\partial_1\signal{\phi}\,\partial_1\signal{\phi}
-\partial_2\signal{\phi}\,\partial_2\signal{\phi}
-\partial_3\signal{\phi}\,\partial_3\signal{\phi}]
-\frac12\left(\frac{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}}{\energy{\hbar}}\right)^2\signal{\phi}^2
$$

In this form it is clear that

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial(\partial_0\signal{\phi})}
=\partial_0\signal{\phi}=\partial^0\signal{\phi},\qquad
\frac{\partial}{\partial(\partial_1\signal{\phi})}
=-\partial_1\signal{\phi}=\partial^1\signal{\phi},
$$

and so on.) Meanwhile

$$
\frac{\partial\energy{\mathcal L}}{\partial\signal{\phi}}
=-\left(\frac{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}}{\energy{\hbar}}\right)^2\signal{\phi}
$$

and hence the Euler–Lagrange formula requires

$$
\partial_\mu\partial^\mu\signal{\phi}
+\left(\frac{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}}{\energy{\hbar}}\right)^2\signal{\phi}=0
\tag{11.13}
$$

which is the Klein–Gordon equation [eq. (7.9)], describing (in quantum field theory) a particle of spin 0 and mass $\mechanical{m}$.

## Example 11.2 The Dirac Lagrangian for a Spinor (Spin-½) Field

Consider now a spinor field $\signal{\psi}$, and the Lagrangian

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}
=i(\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c})\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\signal{\psi}
-(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2)\signal{\bar\psi}\signal{\psi}
\tag{11.14}
$$

We treat $\signal{\psi}$ and the adjoint spinor $\signal{\bar\psi}$ as independent field variables.[^ep-11-2-independent] Applying the Euler–Lagrange equation to $\signal{\bar\psi}$, we find

[^ep-11-2-independent]: Since $\signal{\psi}$ is a complex spinor, there are actually *eight* independent fields here ($i$ runs from 1 to 8): the real and imaginary parts of each of the four components of $\signal{\psi}$. But in applying the Euler–Lagrange equations any linear combinations of these eight will do just as well, and we choose to use the four components of $\signal{\psi}$ plus the four components of $\signal{\bar\psi}$.

<!-- PDF page 354; printed page 346 -->

$$
\frac{\partial\energy{\mathcal L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu\signal{\bar\psi})}=0,\qquad
\frac{\partial\energy{\mathcal L}}{\partial\signal{\bar\psi}}
=i\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\signal{\psi}
-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2\signal{\psi}
$$

so that

$$
i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\signal{\psi}
-\left(\frac{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}}{\energy{\hbar}}\right)\signal{\psi}=0
\tag{11.15}
$$

This is the Dirac equation [eq. (7.20)], describing (in quantum field theory) a particle of spin $\frac12$ and mass $\mechanical{m}$. Meanwhile, if we apply the Euler–Lagrange equation to $\signal{\psi}$, we obtain

$$
\frac{\partial\energy{\mathcal L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu\signal{\psi})}
=i\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu,\qquad
\frac{\partial\energy{\mathcal L}}{\partial\signal{\psi}}
=-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2\signal{\bar\psi}
$$

and hence

$$
i\partial_\mu\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu
+\left(\frac{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}}{\energy{\hbar}}\right)\signal{\bar\psi}=0
$$

which is the adjoint of the Dirac equation (see Problem 7.13).

## Example 11.3 The Proca Lagrangian for a Vector (Spin-1) Field

Finally, suppose we take a vector field, $\electric{A^\mu}$, with the Lagrangian

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}
=\frac{-1}{16\pi}(\partial^\mu\electric{A^\nu}-\partial^\nu\electric{A^\mu})
(\partial_\mu\electric{A_\nu}-\partial_\nu\electric{A_\mu})
+\frac1{8\pi}\left(\frac{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}}{\energy{\hbar}}\right)^2
\electric{A^\nu}\electric{A_\nu}
\tag{11.16}
$$

Here

$$
\frac{\partial\energy{\mathcal L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu\electric{A_\nu})}
=\frac{-1}{4\pi}(\partial^\mu\electric{A^\nu}-\partial^\nu\electric{A^\mu})
\tag{11.17}
$$

(see Problem 11.2), and

$$
\frac{\partial\energy{\mathcal L}}{\partial\electric{A_\nu}}
=\frac1{4\pi}\left(\frac{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}}{\energy{\hbar}}\right)^2\electric{A^\nu}
\tag{11.18}
$$

so the Euler–Lagrange equation yields

$$
\partial_\mu(\partial^\mu\electric{A^\nu}-\partial^\nu\electric{A^\mu})
+\left(\frac{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}}{\energy{\hbar}}\right)^2\electric{A^\nu}=0
\tag{11.19}
$$

This is called the Proca equation; it describes a particle of spin 1 and mass $\mechanical{m}$. Incidentally, since the combination $(\partial^\mu\electric{A^\nu}-\partial^\nu\electric{A^\mu})$ occurs repeatedly in this theory, it is useful to introduce the shorthand

$$
\electric{F^{\mu\nu}}\equiv\partial^\mu\electric{A^\nu}-\partial^\nu\electric{A^\mu}
\tag{11.20}
$$

Then the Lagrangian reads

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}
=-\frac1{16\pi}\electric{F^{\mu\nu}}\electric{F_{\mu\nu}}
+\frac1{8\pi}\left(\frac{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}}{\energy{\hbar}}\right)^2
\electric{A^\nu}\electric{A_\nu}
\tag{11.21}
$$

and the field equation becomes

$$
\partial_\mu\electric{F^{\mu\nu}}
+\left(\frac{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}}{\energy{\hbar}}\right)^2\electric{A^\nu}=0
\tag{11.22}
$$

<!-- PDF page 355; printed page 347 -->

If the notation is beginning to remind you of electrodynamics, it's no accident, for the electromagnetic field *is* precisely a massless vector field; if you set $\mechanical{m}=0$ in equation (11.22) you're left with Maxwell's equations for empty space.

The Lagrangians in these examples came out of thin air (or rather, they were concocted in such a way as to reproduce the desired field equations). In classical particle mechanics $\energy{L}$ is *derived* $(\energy{L}=\energy{T}-\energy{U})$, but in relativistic field theory $\energy{\mathcal L}$ is usually taken as *axiomatic*—we have to start *somewhere*. The Lagrangian for a particular system is by no means unique: you can always multiply $\energy{\mathcal L}$ by a constant, or add a divergence, $(\partial_\mu M^\mu$, where $M^\mu$ is any function of $\signal{\phi_i}$ and $\partial_\mu\signal{\phi_i})$—such terms cancel out when you apply the Euler–Lagrange equations, so they do not affect the field equations. In this sense the factors of $\frac12$ in the Klein–Gordon Lagrangian, for example, are purely conventional.[^ep-11-2-dimensions] Apart from that, however, what we have here are *the* Lagrangians for spin 0, spin $\frac12$, and spin 1. So far, however, we are talking only of *free* fields, with no sources or interactions.

## Example 11.4 The Maxwell Lagrangian for a Massless Vector Field with Source Jμ

Suppose

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}
=\frac{-1}{16\pi}\electric{F^{\mu\nu}}\electric{F_{\mu\nu}}
-\frac1{\mechanical{c}}\electric{J^\mu}\electric{A_\mu}
\tag{11.23}
$$

where $\electric{F^{\mu\nu}}$ (again) stands for $\partial^\mu\electric{A^\nu}-\partial^\nu\electric{A^\mu}$, and $\electric{J^\mu}$ is some specified function. The Euler–Lagrange equations yield

$$
\partial_\mu\electric{F^{\mu\nu}}=\frac{4\pi}{\mechanical{c}}\electric{J^\nu}
\tag{11.24}
$$

which (as we found in Chapter 7, Section 7.4) is the tensor form of Maxwell's equations, describing the electromagnetic fields produced by a current $\electric{J^\mu}$. Incidentally, it follows from equation (11.24) that

$$
\partial_\nu\electric{J^\nu}=0
\tag{11.25}
$$

That is, the internal consistency of the Maxwell Lagrangian (11.23) requires that the current satisfy the continuity equation (7.74); you can't just put in *any* old function for $\electric{J^\mu}$—it's got to respect conservation of charge.

[^ep-11-2-dimensions]: The Lagrangian $(\energy{L})$ carries units of energy [eq. (11.4)], and the Lagrangian density $(\energy{\mathcal L})$ has the units of *energy per unit volume*. The *fields* carry dimensions as follows:
    
    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    \signal{\phi}\text{ (scalar field): }&\sqrt{ML}/T\\
    \signal{\psi}\text{ (spinor field): }&L^{-3/2}\\
    \electric{A^\mu}\text{ (vector field): }&\sqrt{ML}/T
    \end{aligned}
    $$
    
    These are chosen so that $\signal{\psi}$ will go over to the Schrödinger wave function (in the nonrelativistic limit) and $\electric{A^\mu}$ to the Maxwell vector potential (in the nonquantum limit). By the way, in Heaviside–Lorentz units the Proca and Maxwell Lagrangians would be multiplied by $4\pi$.
