> [!TLDR]
> The Euler–Lagrange equation gives Newton's law for a particle subject to a conservative force.

<!-- PDF page 351; printed page 343 -->

*This chapter introduces the “gauge theories” that are now believed to underlie all elementary particle interactions. I begin with the Lagrangian formulation of classical mechanics, and proceed to Lagrangian field theory, the principle of local gauge invariance, the notion of spontaneous symmetry breaking, and the Higgs mechanism (which accounts for the mass of the $W$'s and the $Z$). This material is quite abstract (in contrast to previous chapters); it concerns the fundamental quantum field theories from which the Feynman rules derive. It will not help you to calculate any cross sections or lifetimes. On the other hand, the ideas discussed here constitute the foundation on which virtually all modern theories are predicated. To understand this chapter it will help to have studied some Lagrangian mechanics, but more essential is the relativistic notation in Chapter 3, the taste of group theory in Chapter 4, the Feynman calculus from Chapter 6, and the Dirac equation from Chapter 7.*

According to Newton's second law of motion, a particle of mass $\mechanical{m}$, subjected to a force $\mechanical{\mathbf F}$, undergoes an acceleration $\mechanical{\mathbf a}$ given by

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf F}=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{\mathbf a}
\tag{11.1}
$$

If the force is *conservative*, it can be expressed as the gradient of a scalar potential energy function $\energy{U}$:

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf F}=-\boldsymbol\nabla\energy{U}
\tag{11.2}
$$

and Newton's law reads

<!-- PDF page 352; printed page 344 -->

$$
\mechanical{m}\frac{d\mechanical{\mathbf v}}{d\mechanical{t}}=-\boldsymbol\nabla\energy{U}
\tag{11.3}
$$

where $\mechanical{\mathbf v}$ is the velocity.<sup>1</sup>

An alternative formulation of classical mechanics begins with the “Lagrangian”

$$
\energy{L}=\energy{T}-\energy{U}
\tag{11.4}
$$

where $\energy{T}$ is the kinetic energy of the particle:

$$
\energy{T}=\tfrac12\mechanical{m}\mechanical{v}^2
\tag{11.5}
$$

The Lagrangian is a function of the coordinates $\mechanical{q_i}$ (say, $\mechanical{q_1}=\mechanical{x}$, $\mechanical{q_2}=\mechanical{y}$, $\mechanical{q_3}=\mechanical{z}$) and their time derivatives $\mechanical{\dot q_i}$ $(\mechanical{\dot q_1}=\mechanical{v_x}$, $\mechanical{\dot q_2}=\mechanical{v_y}$, $\mechanical{\dot q_3}=\mechanical{v_z})$. In the Lagrangian formulation the fundamental law of motion is the Euler–Lagrange equation:<sup>2</sup>

$$
\frac{d}{d\mechanical{t}}\left(\frac{\partial\energy{L}}{\partial\mechanical{\dot q_i}}\right)
=\frac{\partial\energy{L}}{\partial\mechanical{q_i}}
\qquad(i=1,2,3)
\tag{11.6}
$$

Thus in Cartesian coordinates we have

$$
\frac{\partial\energy{L}}{\partial\mechanical{\dot q_1}}
=\frac{\partial\energy{T}}{\partial\mechanical{v_x}}
=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{v_x}
\tag{11.7}
$$

$$
\frac{\partial\energy{L}}{\partial\mechanical{q_1}}
=-\frac{\partial\energy{U}}{\partial\mechanical{x}}
\tag{11.8}
$$

and the Euler–Lagrange equation (for $i=1$) reproduces the $\mechanical{x}$ component of Newton's law, in the form of equation (11.3). The Lagrangian formulation is thus equivalent to Newton's (at least, for conservative systems), but it has certain theoretical advantages, as we shall see in the following sections. (See also Problem 11.1.)

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\mechanical{m}$ | $\unit{g}$ | Particle mass | Mass in Newton's law and kinetic energy. |
| $\mechanical{\mathbf F}$ | $\unit{dyn}$ | Force | The conservative force on the particle. |
| $\mechanical{\mathbf a}$ | $\unit{cm/s^2}$ | Acceleration | The rate of change of velocity. |
| $\mechanical{\mathbf v},\mechanical{v},\mechanical{v_x},\mechanical{v_y},\mechanical{v_z}$ | $\unit{cm/s}$ | Velocity and speed | The velocity vector, its magnitude, and Cartesian components. |
| $\mechanical{t}$ | $\unit{s}$ | Time | The independent variable in the equations of motion. |
| $\mechanical{q_i},\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z}$ | $\unit{cm}$ | Cartesian coordinates | Particle positions used in the stated example. |
| $\mechanical{\dot q_i}$ | $\unit{cm/s}$ | Coordinate rates | Time derivatives of the Cartesian coordinates. |
| $\energy{L}$ | $\unit{erg}$ | Lagrangian | Kinetic energy minus potential energy. |
| $\energy{T},\energy{U}$ | $\unit{erg}$ | Kinetic and potential energies | The two energy functions in the Lagrangian. |
