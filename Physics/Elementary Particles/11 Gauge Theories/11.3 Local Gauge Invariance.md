> [!TLDR]
> Local phase invariance requires a massless electromagnetic gauge field and the covariant derivative.

<!-- PDF page 356; printed page 348 -->

Notice that the Dirac Lagrangian

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}=i\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\signal{\psi}
-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2\signal{\bar\psi}\signal{\psi}
\tag{11.14}
$$

is invariant under the transformation

$$
\signal{\psi}\to e^{i\mechanical{\theta}}\signal{\psi}
\qquad\text{(global gauge transformation)}
\tag{11.26}
$$

(where $\mechanical{\theta}$ is any real number), for then $\signal{\bar\psi}\to e^{-i\mechanical{\theta}}\signal{\bar\psi}$, and in the combination $\signal{\bar\psi}\signal{\psi}$ the exponential factors cancel out. For historical reasons, we call (11.26) a (global) *gauge transformation* (“phase” transformation would be a more sensible term). But what if the phase factor is different at different space-time points; that is, *what if* $\mechanical{\theta}$ *is a function of* $\mechanical{x^\mu}$:

$$
\signal{\psi}\to e^{i\mechanical{\theta}(\mechanical{x})}\signal{\psi}
\qquad\text{(local gauge transformation)}
\tag{11.27}
$$

Is the Lagrangian invariant under such a “*local*” gauge transformation? The answer is *no*, for now we pick up an extra term from the derivative of $\mechanical{\theta}$:

$$
\partial_\mu(e^{i\mechanical{\theta}}\signal{\psi})
=i(\partial_\mu\mechanical{\theta})e^{i\mechanical{\theta}}\signal{\psi}
+e^{i\mechanical{\theta}}\partial_\mu\signal{\psi}
\tag{11.28}
$$

so that

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}\to\energy{\mathcal L}
-\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}(\partial_\mu\mechanical{\theta})
\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\signal{\psi}
\tag{11.29}
$$

Actually, for what follows it is convenient to pull a factor of $-(\electric{q}/\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c})$ out of $\mechanical{\theta}$, letting

$$
\electric{\lambda}(\mechanical{x})
\equiv-\frac{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}{\electric{q}}\mechanical{\theta}(\mechanical{x})
\tag{11.30}
$$

where $\electric{q}$ is the charge of the particle involved. In terms of $\electric{\lambda}$, then,

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}\to\energy{\mathcal L}
+(\electric{q}\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\signal{\psi})\partial_\mu\electric{\lambda}
\tag{11.31}
$$

under the local gauge transformation

$$
\signal{\psi}\to e^{-i\electric{q}\electric{\lambda}(\mechanical{x})/\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}\signal{\psi}
\tag{11.32}
$$

So far, there is nothing particularly new or deep in all this. The crucial point comes when we *demand* that the *complete* Lagrangian be invariant under local gauge transformations.[^ep-11-3-principle] Since the *free* Dirac Lagrangian (11.14) is *not* locally gauge invariant, we are obliged to *add* something, in order to soak up the extra term in equation (11.31). Suppose

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}
=[i\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\signal{\psi}
-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2\signal{\bar\psi}\signal{\psi}]
-(\electric{q}\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\signal{\psi})\electric{A_\mu}
\tag{11.33}
$$

where $\electric{A_\mu}$ is some *new* field (called a “gauge” field) which transforms under local gauge transformations according to the rule

[^ep-11-3-principle]: I know of no compelling physical argument for why a global invariance *should* necessarily hold locally. If you believe that gauge transformations are in some sense “fundamental,” then I suppose one should be able to carry them out independently at spacelike-separated points (which are, after all, out of communication with one another). But I think this begs the question. Better, for the moment at least, to take the requirement of local gauge invariance as a *new* principle of physics in its own right.

<!-- PDF page 357; printed page 349 -->

$$
\electric{A_\mu}\to\electric{A_\mu}+\partial_\mu\electric{\lambda}
\tag{11.34}
$$

This “new, improved” Lagrangian is now invariant under local gauge transformations; the price we had to pay was the introduction of a new vector field that couples to $\signal{\psi}$ through the last term in equation (11.33) (see Problem 11.6). But equation (11.33) isn't the whole story; the *full* Lagrangian must include a “free” term for the gauge field. Since it is a vector field, we look to the Proca Lagrangian

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}
=\frac{-1}{16\pi}\electric{F^{\mu\nu}}\electric{F_{\mu\nu}}
+\frac1{8\pi}\left(\frac{\mechanical{m_A}\mechanical{c}}{\energy{\hbar}}\right)^2
\electric{A^\nu}\electric{A_\nu}
\tag{11.21}
$$

But there is a problem here, for whereas $\electric{F^{\mu\nu}}\equiv\partial^\mu\electric{A^\nu}-\partial^\nu\electric{A^\mu}$ is invariant under (11.34), as you should check for yourself, $\electric{A^\nu}\electric{A_\nu}$ is *not*. *Evidently, the gauge field must be massless* $(\mechanical{m_A}=0)$, otherwise local gauge invariance will be lost.

Conclusion: if we start with the Dirac Lagrangian, and impose local gauge invariance, we are forced to introduce a massless vector field $(\electric{A^\mu})$, and the complete Lagrangian becomes

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}
=[i\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\signal{\psi}
-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2\signal{\bar\psi}\signal{\psi}]
+\left[\frac{-1}{16\pi}\electric{F^{\mu\nu}}\electric{F_{\mu\nu}}\right]
-[(\electric{q}\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\signal{\psi})\electric{A_\mu}]
\tag{11.35}
$$

As you will have guessed, $\electric{A^\mu}$ is precisely the electromagnetic potential; the gauge transformation rule for $\electric{A^\mu}$ (11.34) is just what we found back in Chapter 7 [eq. (7.81)], and the last two terms in equation (11.35) reproduce the Maxwell Lagrangian (11.23), with the current density

$$
\electric{J^\mu}=\mechanical{c}\electric{q}(\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\signal{\psi})
\tag{11.36}
$$

Thus the requirement of local gauge invariance, applied to the free Dirac Lagrangian, generates all of electrodynamics, and specifies the current produced by Dirac particles.

In case the procedure for invoking local gauge invariance seems mysterious, let's review it, and see what was actually involved. The difference between global and local gauge transformations arises when we calculate *derivatives* of the fields [eq. (11.28)]:

$$
\partial_\mu\signal{\psi}
\to e^{-i\electric{q}\electric{\lambda}/\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}
\left[\partial_\mu-i\frac{\electric{q}}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}
(\partial_\mu\electric{\lambda})\right]\signal{\psi}
\tag{11.37}
$$

Instead of a simple phase factor, we pick up an extra term involving $\partial_\mu\electric{\lambda}$. *If in the original (free) Lagrangian we replace every derivative* $(\partial_\mu)$ *by the so-called “covariant derivative”*

$$
\mathcal D_\mu\equiv\partial_\mu+i\frac{\electric{q}}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}\electric{A_\mu}
\tag{11.38}
$$

the transformation of $\electric{A_\mu}$ [eq. (11.34)] will cancel the offending term in equation (11.37)

$$
\mathcal D_\mu\signal{\psi}
\to e^{-i\electric{q}\electric{\lambda}/\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}
\mathcal D_\mu\signal{\psi}
\tag{11.39}
$$

<!-- PDF page 358; printed page 350 -->

and the invariance of $\energy{\mathcal L}$ is restored. The substitution of $\mathcal D_\mu$ for $\partial_\mu$, then, is a simple device for converting a *globally* invariant Lagrangian into a *locally* invariant one; we call this the “*minimal coupling rule*” [it's what I used, in fact, to generate the extra term in eq. (11.33)].[^ep-11-3-minimal] But the covariant derivative introduces a new vector field $(\electric{A_\mu})$, which requires its own *free* Lagrangian; if the latter is not to spoil local gauge invariance, we must take the gauge fields to be massless. This leads to the final expression (11.35), which people in the know would immediately recognize as the Lagrangian for quantum electrodynamics—Dirac fields (electrons and positrons) interacting with Maxwell fields (photons).

The idea of local gauge invariance goes back to the work of Hermann Weyl in 1919.<sup>3</sup> However, its power and generality were not fully appreciated until the early seventies. Our starting point—the global phase transformation (11.26)—may be thought of as multiplication of $\signal{\psi}$ by a unitary $1\times1$ matrix:

$$
\signal{\psi}\to U\signal{\psi},\qquad\text{where }U^\dagger U=1
\tag{11.40}
$$

(Here $U=e^{i\mechanical{\theta}}$). The group of *all* such matrices is $U(1)$ (see Table 4.2), and hence the symmetry involved is called “$U(1)$ gauge invariance.” This terminology is extravagant for the case at hand (a $1\times1$ matrix is a *number*, so why not leave it at that?), but in 1954 Yang and Mills<sup>4</sup> applied the same strategy (insisting that a *global* invariance hold *locally*) to the group $SU(2)$, and later on the idea was extended to color $SU(3)$, producing chromodynamics. In the Standard Model *all* of the fundamental interactions are generated in this way.

[^ep-11-3-minimal]: The minimal coupling rule is much older than the principle of local gauge invariance. In terms of momentum [$\mechanical{p_\mu}\leftrightarrow i\energy{\hbar}\partial_\mu$, see eq. (7.5)] it reads $\mechanical{p_\mu}\to\mechanical{p_\mu}-i(\electric{q}/\mechanical{c})\electric{A_\mu}$, and is a well-known trick in classical electrodynamics for obtaining the equation of motion for a charged particle in the presence of electrodynamic fields. See J. D. Jackson, *Classical Electrodynamics*, 2d Ed. (New York: Wiley, 1975), eq. (12.29). It amounts, in this sense, to a sophisticated formulation of the Lorentz force law. In modern particle theory we prefer to regard local gauge invariance as fundamental, and minimal coupling as a device for achieving it.
