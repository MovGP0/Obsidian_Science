> [!TLDR]
> Local SU(2) invariance couples two equal-mass Dirac fields to three massless gauge fields with non-Abelian interactions.

Suppose now that we have *two* spin-$\frac12$ fields, $\signal{\psi_1}$ and $\signal{\psi_2}$. The Lagrangian, in the absence of any interactions, is

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}
=[i\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}\signal{\bar\psi_1}\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\signal{\psi_1}
-\mechanical{m_1}\mechanical{c}^2\signal{\bar\psi_1}\signal{\psi_1}]
+[i\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}\signal{\bar\psi_2}\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\signal{\psi_2}
-\mechanical{m_2}\mechanical{c}^2\signal{\bar\psi_2}\signal{\psi_2}]
\tag{11.41}
$$

It's just the *sum* of the two Dirac Lagrangians. (Apply the Euler–Lagrange equations to this $\energy{\mathcal L}$, and you'll find that $\signal{\psi_1}$ and $\signal{\psi_2}$ both obey the Dirac equation, with the appropriate mass.) But we can write equation (11.41) more compactly by combining $\signal{\psi_1}$ and $\signal{\psi_2}$ into a two-component column vector:

$$
\signal{\psi}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\signal{\psi_1}\\\signal{\psi_2}\end{pmatrix}
\tag{11.42}
$$

(Of course, $\signal{\psi_1}$ and $\signal{\psi_2}$ are themselves four-component Dirac spinors, and you might prefer a double-index notation: $\signal{\psi_{\alpha,i}}$, where $\alpha=1,2$ identifies the *particle*

<!-- PDF page 359; printed page 351 -->

and $i=1,2,3,4$ labels the spinor component. However, in the present context we are *only* concerned with the particle index, although the Dirac matrices, of course, act on the spinor indices.) The adjoint spinor is

$$
\signal{\bar\psi}=(\signal{\bar\psi_1}\quad\signal{\bar\psi_2})
\tag{11.43}
$$

and the Lagrangian becomes

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}
=i\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\signal{\psi}
-\mechanical{c}^2\signal{\bar\psi}\mechanical{M}\signal{\psi}
\tag{11.44}
$$

where

$$
\mechanical{M}=\begin{pmatrix}\mechanical{m_1}&0\\0&\mechanical{m_2}\end{pmatrix}
\tag{11.45}
$$

is the “mass matrix.” In particular, if the two masses happen to be *equal* equation (11.44) reduces to

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}
=i\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\signal{\psi}
-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2\signal{\bar\psi}\signal{\psi}
\tag{11.46}
$$

This *looks* just like the one-particle Dirac Lagrangian. However, $\signal{\psi}$ is now a two-element column vector, and $\energy{\mathcal L}$ admits a more general global invariance than before:

$$
\signal{\psi}\to U\signal{\psi}
\tag{11.47}
$$

where $U$ is any $2\times2$ unitary matrix

$$
U^\dagger U=1
\tag{11.48}
$$

For under the transformation (11.47),

$$
\signal{\bar\psi}\to\signal{\bar\psi}U^\dagger
\tag{11.49}
$$

and hence the combination $\signal{\bar\psi}\signal{\psi}$ is invariant. Now, just as any complex number of modulus 1 can be written in the form $e^{i\mechanical{\theta}}$, with real $\mechanical{\theta}$, so any unitary matrix can be written in the form<sup>5</sup>

$$
U=e^{iH}
\tag{11.50}
$$

where $H$ is *Hermitian* $(H^\dagger=H)$.[^ep-11-4-hermitian] Moreover, the most general Hermitian $2\times2$ matrix can be expressed in terms of four real numbers, $a_1$, $a_2$, $a_3$, and $\mechanical{\theta}$ (Problem 11.10):

$$
H=\mechanical{\theta}1+\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\mathbf a
\tag{11.51}
$$

where 1 is the $2\times2$ unit matrix, $\tau_1,\tau_2,\tau_3$ are the Pauli matrices (4.26), and the dot product is a convenient shorthand for $\tau_1a_1+\tau_2a_2+\tau_3a_3$. Thus any unitary $2\times2$ matrix can be expressed as a product:

$$
U=e^{i\mechanical{\theta}}e^{i\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\mathbf a}
\tag{11.52}
$$

[^ep-11-4-hermitian]: In matrix theory the natural generalization of complex conjugation $(*)$ is Hermitian conjugation $(\dagger)$—*transpose* conjugation. Of course, there's no distinction in the case of $1\times1$ matrices (complex numbers), but for higher dimensions it is the Hermitian conjugate that shares the most useful properties of ordinary complex conjugation. In this sense the closest analog to a *real* number $(a=a^*)$ is a *Hermitian* matrix $(A=A^\dagger)$, and the analog to a number of modulus 1 $(a^*a=1)$ is a unitary matrix $(A^\dagger A=1)$.

We have already explored the implications of *phase* transformations $(e^{i\mechanical{\theta}})$; in this section we shall concentrate on transformations of the form

$$
\signal{\psi}\to e^{i\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\mathbf a}\signal{\psi}
\qquad[\text{global }SU(2)\text{ transformation}]
\tag{11.53}
$$

The matrix $e^{i\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\mathbf a}$ has determinant 1 [see Problem 4.22], and therefore belongs to the group $SU(2)$. Generalizing the terminology of Section 11.3, we say that Lagrangian (11.46) is invariant under global $SU(2)$ gauge transformations.[^ep-11-4-u2] What Yang and Mills did was to promote this *global* invariance to the status of a *local* invariance.

The inspiration and the strategy were similar to Weyl's, but the implementation is more subtle; in fact, it's quite remarkable that it works at all. The first step is to let the parameters $(\mathbf a)$ be functions of $\mechanical{x^\mu}$ [as before, equation (11.30), I'll let $\boldsymbol\lambda(\mechanical{x})=-(\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}/q)\mathbf a(\mechanical{x})$, where $q$ is a coupling constant analogous to electric charge]:

$$
\signal{\psi}\to S\signal{\psi},\qquad
\text{where }S\equiv e^{-iq\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\boldsymbol\lambda(\mechanical{x})/\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}
\qquad[\text{local }SU(2)\text{ transformation}]
\tag{11.54}
$$

As it stands, $\energy{\mathcal L}$ is *not* invariant under such a transformation, for the derivative picks up an extra term:

$$
\partial_\mu\signal{\psi}\to S\partial_\mu\signal{\psi}+(\partial_\mu S)\signal{\psi}
\tag{11.55}
$$

The remedy, again, is to replace the derivative in $\energy{\mathcal L}$ by a “covariant derivative,” modeled on equation (11.38), but taking into account the structure of equation (11.55):

$$
\mathcal D_\mu\equiv\partial_\mu+i\frac{q}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}
\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\signal{\mathbf A_\mu}
\tag{11.56}
$$

and assign to the gauge fields $\signal{\mathbf A_\mu}$ (it takes *three* of them this time) a transformation rule such that

$$
\mathcal D_\mu\signal{\psi}\to S(\mathcal D_\mu\signal{\psi})
\tag{11.57}
$$

For then the Lagrangian (11.46) will clearly be invariant.

It is not a trivial matter to deduce the transformation rule for $\signal{\mathbf A_\mu}$ from (11.57).<sup>6</sup> I'll leave it for you to show (Problem 11.11) that $\signal{\mathbf A_\mu}\to\signal{\mathbf A'_\mu}$, where $\signal{\mathbf A'_\mu}$ is given by

$$
\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\signal{\mathbf A'_\mu}
=S(\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\signal{\mathbf A_\mu})S^{-1}
+i\left(\frac{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}q\right)(\partial_\mu S)S^{-1}
\tag{11.58}
$$

This much is relatively straightforward. But $S$ and $S^{-1}$ in the first term cannot be brought together, because they do not commute with $\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\signal{\mathbf A_\mu}$. Nor is the gradient of $S$ simply $-i(q\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\partial_\mu\boldsymbol\lambda/\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c})S$, because $S$ does not commute with $\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\partial_\mu\boldsymbol\lambda$. You can work out the exact result (using Problems 4.20 and 4.21), if you have the energy,

[^ep-11-4-u2]: It is *also* invariant under the larger group $U(2)$. But (11.52) shows that any element of $U(2)$ can be expressed as an element of $SU(2)$ times an appropriate phase factor (in group-theoretical language, $U(2)=U(1)\otimes SU(2)$), and since we have already studied $U(1)$ invariance, the only thing *new* here is the $SU(2)$ symmetry.

<!-- PDF page 361; printed page 353 -->

but the answer is not particularly illuminating. For our purposes it will suffice to know the *approximate* transformation rule, in the limiting case of very small $\boldsymbol\lambda$, for which we may expand $S$ and keep only the first-order terms:

$$
S\cong1-\frac{iq}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\boldsymbol\lambda,\quad
S^{-1}\cong1+\frac{iq}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\boldsymbol\lambda,\quad
\partial_\mu S\cong-\frac{iq}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}
\boldsymbol\tau\cdot(\partial_\mu\boldsymbol\lambda)
\tag{11.59}
$$

In this approximation equation (11.58) yields

$$
\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\signal{\mathbf A'_\mu}
\cong\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\signal{\mathbf A_\mu}
+\frac{iq}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}
[\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\signal{\mathbf A_\mu},\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\boldsymbol\lambda]
+\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\partial_\mu\boldsymbol\lambda
\tag{11.60}
$$

and hence (using Problem 4.20, to evaluate the commutator)

$$
\signal{\mathbf A'_\mu}\cong\signal{\mathbf A_\mu}+\partial_\mu\boldsymbol\lambda
+\frac{2q}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}
(\boldsymbol\lambda\times\signal{\mathbf A_\mu})
\tag{11.61}
$$

The resulting Lagrangian

$$
\begin{aligned}
\energy{\mathcal L}
&=i\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\mathcal D_\mu\signal{\psi}
-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2\signal{\bar\psi}\signal{\psi}\\
&=[i\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\signal{\psi}
-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2\signal{\bar\psi}\signal{\psi}]
-(q\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\signal{\psi})\cdot\signal{\mathbf A_\mu}
\end{aligned}
\tag{11.62}
$$

is invariant under local gauge transformations (11.54) and (11.58), but we have been obliged to introduce three new vector fields $\signal{\mathbf A^\mu}=(\signal{A_1^\mu},\signal{A_2^\mu},\signal{A_3^\mu})$, and they will require their own *free* Lagrangian:

$$
\energy{\mathcal L_A}
=-\frac1{16\pi}\signal{F_1^{\mu\nu}}\signal{F_{\mu\nu1}}
-\frac1{16\pi}\signal{F_2^{\mu\nu}}\signal{F_{\mu\nu2}}
-\frac1{16\pi}\signal{F_3^{\mu\nu}}\signal{F_{\mu\nu3}}
=-\frac1{16\pi}\signal{\mathbf F^{\mu\nu}}\cdot\signal{\mathbf F_{\mu\nu}}
\tag{11.63}
$$

(Again, the three-vector notation pertains to the *particle* indices.) The Proca mass term

$$
\frac1{8\pi}\left(\frac{\mechanical{m_A}\mechanical{c}}{\energy{\hbar}}\right)^2
\signal{\mathbf A^\nu}\cdot\signal{\mathbf A_\nu}
\tag{11.64}
$$

is excluded by local gauge invariance; as before, the gauge fields must be massless. But this time the old association $\signal{F^{\mu\nu}}=\partial^\mu\signal{A^\nu}-\partial^\nu\signal{A^\mu}$ must itself be modified, for with this definition the gauge field Lagrangian (11.63) is not invariant *either* (see Problem 11.12). Rather, we take[^ep-11-4-strength]

$$
\signal{\mathbf F^{\mu\nu}}
\equiv\partial^\mu\signal{\mathbf A^\nu}-\partial^\nu\signal{\mathbf A^\mu}
-\frac{2q}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}(\signal{\mathbf A^\mu}\times\signal{\mathbf A^\nu})
\tag{11.65}
$$

Under infinitesimal local gauge transformations (11.61)

$$
\signal{\mathbf F^{\mu\nu}}\to\signal{\mathbf F^{\mu\nu}}
+\frac{2q}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}
(\boldsymbol\lambda\times\signal{\mathbf F^{\mu\nu}})
\tag{11.66}
$$

(Problem 11.13), and hence $\energy{\mathcal L_A}$ is invariant. (See Problem 11.14 for a proof that the invariance extends to *finite* gauge transformations.)

[^ep-11-4-strength]: Definition (11.65) is not as arbitrary as it may seem; the point is that with *three* vector fields there is a second antisymmetric tensor form available, $(\signal{\mathbf A^\mu}\times\signal{\mathbf A^\nu})$, and the coefficient, $-2q/\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}$, is chosen precisely to *make* $\energy{\mathcal L_A}$ invariant. Notice that when the coupling constant $q$ goes to zero we are left with the free Dirac Lagrangian for each spinor field and the free (massless) Proca Lagrangian for each of the three gauge fields.

Conclusion: The complete Yang–Mills Lagrangian is

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}
=[i\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\signal{\psi}
-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2\signal{\bar\psi}\signal{\psi}]
-\frac1{16\pi}\signal{\mathbf F^{\mu\nu}}\cdot\signal{\mathbf F_{\mu\nu}}
-(q\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\boldsymbol\tau\signal{\psi})\cdot\signal{\mathbf A_\mu}
\tag{11.67}
$$

with $\signal{\mathbf F^{\mu\nu}}$ defined by equation (11.65); it is invariant under local $SU(2)$ gauge transformations, (11.54) and (11.58), and describes two equal-mass Dirac fields in interaction with three massless vector gauge fields. It all results from insisting that the *global* $SU(2)$ invariance of the original free Lagrangian (11.46) shall hold locally. Borrowing the language of electrodynamics, we say that the Dirac fields generate three *currents*

$$
\mathbf J^\mu\equiv\mechanical{c}q(\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\boldsymbol\tau\signal{\psi})
\tag{11.68}
$$

which act as *sources* for the gauge fields; the Lagrangian for the gauge fields alone

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}
=-\frac1{16\pi}\signal{\mathbf F^{\mu\nu}}\cdot\signal{\mathbf F_{\mu\nu}}
-\frac1{\mechanical{c}}\mathbf J^\mu\cdot\signal{\mathbf A_\mu}
\tag{11.69}
$$

is reminiscent of the Maxwell Lagrangian (11.23), and gives rise to a rich and interesting classical field theory.<sup>7</sup> (See Problem 11.15.)

Although Yang–Mills theory is inspired by the same *idea* as Weyl's (namely: a global invariance should hold locally), the implementation was more subtle at two points: (1) the local transformation rule for gauge fields, and (2) the expression for $\signal{\mathbf F^{\mu\nu}}$ in terms of $\signal{\mathbf A^\mu}$. Both complications derive from the fact that the symmetry group in question is non-Abelian ($2\times2$ matrices do not commute, whereas $1\times1$ matrices—obviously—do). To emphasize the distinction, we refer to the Weyl case as an *Abelian* gauge theory, and Yang–Mills as a *non-Abelian* gauge theory. In contemporary elementary particle physics many symmetry groups have been explored; we shall encounter a few in the remaining sections of this book. However, the *hard* work is over: Extending non-Abelian gauge theory to higher symmetry groups is a straightforward procedure, once the Yang–Mills model is on the table.

Curiously, though, Yang–Mills theory in its original form turned out to be of little use. After all, it starts from the premise that there exist two elementary spin-$\frac12$ particles of equal mass, and as far as we know there are no such pairs in nature. Yang and Mills themselves had the nucleon system (proton and neutron) in mind, and thought of their model as a way of implementing Heisenberg's isospin invariance in the strong interactions. The small mass difference between proton and neutron, $1.29\,\unit{MeV}/\mechanical{c}^2$, would be attributed to electromagnetic symmetry-breaking. For the theory to succeed there had to exist a massless isotriplet of vector (spin-1) particles. The only candidates in sight are the $\rho$ mesons; but they are hardly *massless* $(\mechanical{M_\rho}=770\,\unit{MeV}/\mechanical{c}^2)$, and this is not a minor discrepancy that can be plausibly blamed on electromagnetic contamination. A number of attempts were made to doctor up Yang–Mills theory to accommodate massive gauge bosons, but by the time they finally bore fruit (through the Higgs mechanism), it was pretty clear that $p$, $n$, and $\rho$ are composite particles anyway, and that isospin is just one component of a larger flavor symmetry that is too

drastically broken to play any fundamental role in the strong interactions. When non-Abelian gauge theory finally came into its own, it was in the context of *color* $(SU(3))$ symmetry in the strong interactions and *weak* isospin-hypercharge $(SU(2)\otimes U(1))$ symmetry in the weak interactions. Meanwhile, for more than a decade after 1954 the Yang–Mills model languished—a lovely idea that nature had evidently chosen not to exploit.
