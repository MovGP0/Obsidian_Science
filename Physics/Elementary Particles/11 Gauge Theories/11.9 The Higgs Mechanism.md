> [!TLDR]
> A scalar vacuum value gives a gauge boson mass, while a gauge choice removes the Goldstone field.

<!-- PDF page 373; printed page 365 -->

The Lagrangian we studied in Section 11.8 can be written more neatly if we combine the two real fields, $\signal{\phi_1}$ and $\signal{\phi_2}$, into a single complex field:

$$
\signal{\phi}\equiv\signal{\phi_1}+i\signal{\phi_2}
\tag{11.123}
$$

so that

$$
\signal{\phi^*}\signal{\phi}=\signal{\phi_1}^2+\signal{\phi_2}^2
\tag{11.124}
$$

In this notation (and it is nothing but notation) the Lagrangian (11.115) reads

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}
=\tfrac12(\partial_\mu\signal{\phi})^*(\partial^\mu\signal{\phi})
+\tfrac12\mechanical{\mu}^2(\signal{\phi^*}\signal{\phi})
-\tfrac14\lambda^2(\signal{\phi^*}\signal{\phi})^2
\tag{11.125}
$$

and the rotational $(SO(2))$ symmetry that was spontaneously broken becomes invariance under $(U(1))$ phase transformations:

$$
\signal{\phi}\to e^{i\mechanical{\theta}}\signal{\phi}
\tag{11.126}
$$

This is precisely the kind of symmetry we considered back in Section 11.3, except that now we are working with scalar fields instead of with spinors. We can make the system invariant under *local* gauge transformations

$$
\signal{\phi}\to e^{i\mechanical{\theta}(\mechanical{x})}\signal{\phi}
\tag{11.127}
$$

by the usual device of introducing a massless gauge field $\signal{A^\mu}$, and replacing the derivatives in equation (11.125) with *covariant* derivatives (11.38):

$$
\mathcal D_\mu\equiv\partial_\mu+i\frac{q}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}\signal{A_\mu}
\tag{11.128}
$$

Thus

$$
\begin{aligned}
\energy{\mathcal L}
&=\frac12\left[\left(\partial_\mu-\frac{iq}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}\signal{A_\mu}\right)
\signal{\phi^*}\right]
\left[\left(\partial^\mu+\frac{iq}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}\signal{A^\mu}\right)
\signal{\phi}\right]\\
&\quad+\frac12\mechanical{\mu}^2(\signal{\phi^*}\signal{\phi})
-\frac14\lambda^2(\signal{\phi^*}\signal{\phi})^2
-\frac1{16\pi}\signal{F^{\mu\nu}}\signal{F_{\mu\nu}}
\end{aligned}
\tag{11.129}
$$

Now we simply retrace our steps in Section 11.8, applying them to the *locally* invariant Lagrangian (11.129). Defining the new fields

$$
\signal{\eta}\equiv\signal{\phi_1}-\mechanical{\mu}/\lambda,\qquad
\signal{\xi}\equiv\signal{\phi_2}
\tag{11.130}
$$

<!-- PDF page 374; printed page 366 -->

[compare eq. (11.119)], the Lagrangian becomes (see Problem 11.25):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\energy{\mathcal L}
&=\left[\frac12(\partial_\mu\signal{\eta})(\partial^\mu\signal{\eta})
-\mechanical{\mu}^2\signal{\eta}^2\right]
+\left[\frac12(\partial_\mu\signal{\xi})(\partial^\mu\signal{\xi})\right]\\
&\quad+\left[-\frac1{16\pi}\signal{F^{\mu\nu}}\signal{F_{\mu\nu}}
+\frac12\left(\frac{q}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}\frac{\mechanical{\mu}}\lambda\right)^2
\signal{A_\mu}\signal{A^\mu}\right]
-2i\left(\frac{\mechanical{\mu}}\lambda\frac{q}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}\right)
(\partial_\mu\signal{\xi})\signal{A^\mu}\\
&\quad+\left\{
\frac{q}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}
[\signal{\eta}(\partial_\mu\signal{\xi})-\signal{\xi}(\partial_\mu\signal{\eta})]\signal{A^\mu}
+\frac{\mechanical{\mu}}\lambda\left(\frac{q}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}\right)^2
\signal{\eta}(\signal{A_\mu}\signal{A^\mu})
+\frac12\left(\frac{q}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}\right)^2
(\signal{\xi}^2+\signal{\eta}^2)(\signal{A_\mu}\signal{A^\mu})\right.\\
&\qquad\left.-\lambda\mechanical{\mu}(\signal{\eta}^3+\signal{\eta}\signal{\xi}^2)
-\frac14\lambda^2(\signal{\eta}^4+2\signal{\eta}^2\signal{\xi}^2+\signal{\xi}^4)
\right\}
+\left(\frac{\mechanical{\mu}^2}{2\lambda}\right)^2
\end{aligned}
\tag{11.131}
$$

The first line is the same as before, equation (11.120); it describes a scalar particle $(\signal{\eta})$ of mass $\sqrt2\mechanical{\mu}\energy{\hbar}/\mechanical{c}$ and a massless Goldstone boson $(\signal{\xi})$. The second line describes the free gauge field $\signal{A^\mu}$, but—*mirabile dictu!*—it has acquired a mass:

$$
\mechanical{m_A}=2\sqrt\pi\left(\frac{q\mechanical{\mu}}{\lambda\mechanical{c}^2}\right)
\tag{11.132}
$$

[compare the Proca Lagrangian (11.21)]. The term in curly brackets specifies various couplings of $\signal{\xi}$, $\signal{\eta}$, and $\signal{A^\mu}$ (see Problem 11.26). It is easy to see where the mass of $\signal{A^\mu}$ came from: The original Lagrangian (11.129) contains a term of the form $\signal{\phi^*}\signal{\phi}\signal{A_\mu}\signal{A^\mu}$, which—absent spontaneous symmetry-breaking—would represent a *coupling*:

![[elementary-particles-qed-chapter11-higgs-scalar-gauge-coupling.svg|Two scalar fields couple to two gauge fields before the vacuum shift.]]

But when the ground state moves “off center,” and the field $\mechanical{\mu}/\lambda$ picks up a constant [(eq. (11.130)], this piece of the Lagrangian takes the form of the Proca mass term.

However, we still have that unwanted Goldstone boson $(\signal{\xi})$. Moreover, there is a suspicious-looking quantity in $\energy{\mathcal L}$:

$$
-2i\left(\frac{\mechanical{\mu}}\lambda\frac{q}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}\right)
(\partial_\mu\signal{\xi})\signal{A^\mu}
\tag{11.133}
$$

What are we to make of this? If we read it as an *interaction*, it leads to a vertex of the form

![[elementary-particles-qed-chapter11-goldstone-gauge-mixing.svg|The bilinear term converts a xi scalar into a gauge field A.]]

in which the $\signal{\xi}$ turns into an $\signal{A}$. Any such term, bilinear in two different fields, indicates that we have incorrectly identified the fundamental particles in the theory (see Problem 11.23). Both difficulties involve the field $\signal{\xi}=\signal{\phi_2}$, and both

<!-- PDF page 375; printed page 367 -->

can be resolved exploiting the local gauge invariance of $\energy{\mathcal L}$ [in the original form (11.129)] to *transform this field away entirely!* Writing equation (11.126) in terms of its real and imaginary parts:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\signal{\phi}\to\signal{\phi'}
&=(\cos\mechanical{\theta}+i\sin\mechanical{\theta})(\signal{\phi_1}+i\signal{\phi_2})\\
&=(\signal{\phi_1}\cos\mechanical{\theta}-\signal{\phi_2}\sin\mechanical{\theta})
+i(\signal{\phi_1}\sin\mechanical{\theta}+\signal{\phi_2}\cos\mechanical{\theta})
\end{aligned}
\tag{11.134}
$$

we see that picking

$$
\mechanical{\theta}=-\tan^{-1}(\signal{\phi_2}/\signal{\phi_1})
\tag{11.135}
$$

will render $\signal{\phi'}$ real, which is to say that $\signal{\phi'_2}=0$. The gauge field $\signal{A^\mu}$ will transform accordingly (11.34), but the Lagrangian will take the same form in terms of the new field variables as it did in terms of the old ones (that's what it means to say that $\energy{\mathcal L}$ is invariant). The only difference is that $\signal{\xi}$ is now zero. *In this particular gauge*, then, the Lagrangian (11.131) reduces to

$$
\begin{aligned}
\energy{\mathcal L}
&=\left[\frac12(\partial_\mu\signal{\eta})(\partial^\mu\signal{\eta})
-\mechanical{\mu}^2\signal{\eta}^2\right]
+\left[-\frac1{16\pi}\signal{F^{\mu\nu}}\signal{F_{\mu\nu}}
+\frac12\left(\frac{q}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}\frac{\mechanical{\mu}}\lambda\right)^2
\signal{A_\mu}\signal{A^\mu}\right]\\
&\quad+\left\{\frac{\mechanical{\mu}}\lambda
\left(\frac{q}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}\right)^2
\signal{\eta}(\signal{A_\mu}\signal{A^\mu})
+\frac12\left(\frac{q}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}\right)^2
\signal{\eta}^2(\signal{A_\mu}\signal{A^\mu})
-\lambda\mechanical{\mu}\signal{\eta}^3-\frac14\lambda^2\signal{\eta}^4\right\}\\
&\quad+\left(\frac{\mechanical{\mu}^2}{2\lambda}\right)^2
\end{aligned}
\tag{11.136}
$$

By an astute choice of gauge, we have eliminated the Goldstone boson and the offending term in $\energy{\mathcal L}$; we are left with a single *massive* scalar $\signal{\eta}$ (the “Higgs” particle) and a *massive* gauge field $\signal{A^\mu}$.

Please understand that Lagrangians (11.129) and (11.136) describe *exactly the same physical system*; all we have done is to select a convenient gauge (11.135) and rewrite the fields in terms of fluctuations about a particular ground state (11.130). We have sacrificed the manifest symmetry of (11.129) in favor of a notation that makes the physical content more transparent, and allows us to extract the Feynman rules more directly. There is an illuminating way to think of this: A *massless* vector field carries two degrees of freedom (transverse polarizations); when $\signal{A^\mu}$ acquires mass, it picks up a third degree of freedom (longitudinal polarization). Where did this extra degree of freedom come from? Answer: *it came from the Goldstone boson, which meanwhile disappeared from the theory*. The gauge field “ate” the Goldstone boson, thereby acquiring both a mass and a third polarization state.[^ep-11-9-ghost] This is the famous *Higgs mechanism*, built on the union of gauge invariance and spontaneous symmetry-breaking.

According to the Standard Model, the Higgs mechanism is responsible for the masses of the weak interaction gauge bosons $(W^\pm$ and $Z^0)$. The details are still matters of speculation—the Higgs particle has never been seen in the laboratory (presumably it is just too heavy to make with any existing accelerator);[^ep-11-9-ssc]

[^ep-11-9-ghost]: We don't *have* to adopt any particular gauge. However, if we do not, the theory will contain a nonphysical “ghost” particle, and it is simplest to eliminate it explicitly from the start.

[^ep-11-9-ssc]: Many particle physicists are presently campaigning for the construction of a Superconducting Supercollider (SSC), whose main purpose would be to search for the Higgs particle.

<!-- PDF page 376; printed page 368 -->

and the Higgs “potential” [$\energy{\mathcal U}(\signal{\phi})$] is completely unknown (I used $\energy{\mathcal U}=-\frac12\mechanical{\mu}^2(\signal{\phi^*}\signal{\phi})+\frac14\lambda^2(\signal{\phi^*}\signal{\phi})^2$ just for the sake of argument). There may in fact be many Higgs particles, or it may be a composite structure, but never mind: The important thing is that we have found a way *in principle* of imparting mass to the gauge fields,[^ep-11-9-yukawa] and that is our license to believe that all the fundamental interactions—weak as well as strong and electromagnetic—can be described by local gauge theories.<sup>11</sup>

[^ep-11-9-yukawa]: In the Standard Model the Higgs particle is *also* responsible for the masses of quarks and leptons; they are initially taken to be massless, but are assumed to have Yukawa couplings (see Problem 11.21) to the Higgs particle. When the latter is “shifted,” by spontaneous symmetry-breaking (11.130), the Yukawa coupling splits into two parts, one of which is a true interaction, and the other a *mass* term for the field $\signal{\psi}$. This is a nice idea, but it does not help us to calculate the fermion masses, since the Yukawa coupling constants themselves are unknown. Only when (and if) the Higgs particle is actually *found* will it be possible to confirm all this empirically.
