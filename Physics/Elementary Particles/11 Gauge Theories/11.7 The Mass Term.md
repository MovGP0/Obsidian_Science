> [!TLDR]
> The particle mass follows from the quadratic field term after expansion about a true potential minimum.

<!-- PDF page 368; printed page 360 -->

The principle of local gauge invariance works beautifully for the strong and electromagnetic interactions. In the first place, it gives us a *machine* for determining the couplings (in the “old days” the construction of $\energy{\mathcal L_{\text{int}}}$ was a purely ad hoc guess). Moreover, as 't Hooft and others proved in the early seventies,<sup>9</sup> gauge theories are automatically renormalizable. But the application to *weak* interactions was stymied by the fact that gauge fields have to be massless. Remember, the mass term in the Proca Lagrangian is not locally gauge invariant, and whereas the photon and the gluons are massless, the $W$'s and the $Z^0$ certainly are *not*. So the question arises, Can we doctor up gauge theory in such a way as to accommodate massive gauge fields? The answer is *yes*, but the procedure—exploiting spontaneous symmetry-breaking and the Higgs mechanism—is diabolically subtle, and it pays to begin by thinking very carefully about how one identifies the mass term in a Lagrangian.

Suppose, for instance, you were given the following Lagrangian for a scalar field $\signal{\phi}$:

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}
=\tfrac12(\partial_\mu\signal{\phi})(\partial^\mu\signal{\phi})+e^{-(\alpha\signal{\phi})^2}
\tag{11.105}
$$

where $\alpha$ is some (real) constant. Where is the mass term here? At first glance there's no sign of one, and you might conclude that this is a *massless* field. But that is incorrect, for if you expand the exponential, $\energy{\mathcal L}$ takes the form

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}
=\tfrac12(\partial_\mu\signal{\phi})(\partial^\mu\signal{\phi})
+1-\alpha^2\signal{\phi}^2+\tfrac12\alpha^4\signal{\phi}^4
-\tfrac16\alpha^6\signal{\phi}^6+\cdots
\tag{11.106}
$$

The 1 is irrelevant (a constant term in $\energy{\mathcal L}$ has no affect on the field equations), but the second term looks just like the mass term in the Klein–Gordon Lagrangian (11.11), with $\alpha^2=\frac12(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}/\energy{\hbar})^2$. Evidently this Lagrangian describes a particle of mass

$$
\mechanical{m}=\sqrt2\alpha\energy{\hbar}/\mechanical{c}
\tag{11.107}
$$

The higher-order terms represent couplings, of the form

and so on. This is not supposed to be a realistic theory, of course—I offer it only as an example of how the mass term in a Lagrangian may be “disguised.” To identify it, we expand $\energy{\mathcal L}$ in powers of $\signal{\phi}$ and pick out the term proportional to $\signal{\phi}^2$ (in general, it's the term of *second order* in the fields—$\signal{\phi}$, $\signal{\psi}$, $\signal{A^\mu}$, or whatever).

But there is a deeper subtlety lurking here, which I illustrate with the following Lagrangian:

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}
=\tfrac12(\partial_\mu\signal{\phi})(\partial^\mu\signal{\phi})
+\tfrac12\mechanical{\mu}^2\signal{\phi}^2-\tfrac14\lambda^2\signal{\phi}^4
\tag{11.108}
$$

Here $\mechanical{\mu}$ and $\lambda$ are (real) constants. The second term looks like a mass, and the third like an interaction. But wait! The *sign* is wrong [compare equation (11.11)]—if that's a mass term, then $\mechanical{m}$ is *imaginary*, which is nonsense. How, then, *should* we interpret this Lagrangian? To answer this question, we must understand that the Feynman calculus is really a perturbation procedure, in which we start from the ground state (the “vacuum”), and treat the fields as fluctuations about that state. For the Lagrangians we have considered so far, the ground state—the field configuration of minimum energy—has always been the trivial one: $\signal{\phi}=0$. But for the Lagrangian (11.108), $\signal{\phi}=0$ is *not* the ground state. To determine the true ground state, we write $\energy{\mathcal L}$ as a “kinetic” term $(\frac12\partial_\mu\signal{\phi}\,\partial^\mu\signal{\phi})$ minus a “potential” term [inspired by the classical Lagrangian (11.4)]:

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}=\energy{\mathcal T}-\energy{\mathcal U}
\tag{11.109}
$$

and look for the minimum of $\energy{\mathcal U}$. In the present case

$$
\energy{\mathcal U}(\signal{\phi})
=-\tfrac12\mechanical{\mu}^2\signal{\phi}^2+\tfrac14\lambda^2\signal{\phi}^4
\tag{11.110}
$$

and the minimum occurs at

$$
\signal{\phi}=\pm\mechanical{\mu}/\lambda
\tag{11.111}
$$

(see Fig. 11.1). For this Lagrangian, the Feynman calculus must be formulated in terms of deviations from one or the other of these ground states. This suggests that we introduce a new field variable, $\signal{\eta}$, defined by

$$
\signal{\eta}\equiv\signal{\phi}\pm\frac{\mechanical{\mu}}\lambda
\tag{11.112}
$$

In terms of $\signal{\eta}$, the Lagrangian reads

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}
=\tfrac12(\partial_\mu\signal{\eta})(\partial^\mu\signal{\eta})
-\mechanical{\mu}^2\signal{\eta}^2
\pm\mechanical{\mu}\lambda\signal{\eta}^3
-\tfrac14\lambda^2\signal{\eta}^4
+\tfrac14(\mechanical{\mu}^2/\lambda)^2
\tag{11.113}
$$

The second quantity is now a mass term with the *correct* sign, and we discover [comparing eq. (11.11)] that the mass of the particle is

$$
\mechanical{m}=\sqrt2\mechanical{\mu}\energy{\hbar}/\mechanical{c}
\tag{11.114}
$$

Meanwhile, the third and fourth terms represent couplings of the form

![[elementary-particles-qed-chapter11-shifted-scalar-vertices.svg|Three-scalar and four-scalar vertices after expansion about a potential minimum.]]

(the last term is a constant, signifying nothing).

I emphasize that Lagrangians (11.108) and (11.113) represent exactly the same physical system; all we have done is to change the *notation* (11.112). But the first version is not suited to the Feynman calculus (technically, a perturbation series in $\signal{\phi}$ would not converge, because it is an expansion about an unstable point); only in the second formulation can we read off the mass and the vertex factors.

*Conclusion:* To identify the mass term in a Lagrangian, we first locate the ground state [the field configuration for which $\energy{\mathcal U}(\signal{\phi})$ is a minimum] and reexpress $\energy{\mathcal L}$ as a function of the deviation, $\signal{\eta}$, from this minimum. Expanding in powers of $\signal{\eta}$, we obtain the mass from the coefficient of the $\signal{\eta}^2$ term.
