> [!TLDR]
> Six Feynman rules assign coupling, propagator, and momentum-conservation factors to diagrams in the scalar ABC toy theory.

<!-- PDF page 209; printed page 201 -->

In Section 6.2 we learned how to calculate decay rates and scattering cross sections, in terms of the amplitude $\signal{\mathcal M}$ for the process in question. Now I'll show you how to determine $\signal{\mathcal M}$ itself, using the “Feynman rules” to evaluate the relevant diagrams. We could go straight to a “real-life” system, such as quantum electrodynamics, with electrons and photons interacting via the primitive vertex:

![[elementary-particles-toy-theory-qed-vertex.svg|Primitive electron–photon vertex.]]

This is the original, the most important, and the best understood application of Feynman's technique. Unfortunately, it involves diverting complications (due to the fact that the electron carries spin $\tfrac{1}{2}$ and the photon carries spin 1) which have nothing to do with the Feynman calculus as such. In Chapter 7 I'll show you how to handle particles with spin, but for the moment I don't want to confuse the issue, so I'm going to introduce a “toy” theory, which does not pretend to represent the real world, but will serve to illustrate the *method*, with a minimum of extraneous baggage.<sup>4</sup>

Imagine a world in which there are just three kinds of particles—call them $A$, $B$, and $C$—with masses $\mechanical{m_A}$, $\mechanical{m_B}$, and $\mechanical{m_C}$. They all have spin 0, and each is its own antiparticle. There is one primitive vertex, by which the three particles interact:

![[elementary-particles-toy-theory-abc-vertex.svg|Primitive ABC interaction vertex.]]

I shall assume that $A$ is the heaviest of the three, and in fact weighs more than $B$ and $C$ combined, so that it can decay into $B+C$. The lowest-order diagram describing this disintegration is:

![[elementary-particles-toy-theory-abc-decay.svg|Lowest-order A → B + C decay diagram.]]

<!-- PDF page 210; printed page 202 -->

to which there are (small) third-order corrections:

![[elementary-particles-toy-theory-third-order-decays.svg|The four third-order corrections to A → B + C shown in the source.]]

and even smaller ones of higher order. Our first project will be to calculate the lifetime of the $A$, to lowest order. After that, we'll look at various scattering processes, such as $A+A\to B+B$:

![[elementary-particles-toy-theory-aa-bb-scattering.svg|Lowest-order A + A → B + B scattering diagram.]]

$A+B\to A+B$:

![[elementary-particles-toy-theory-ab-ab-scattering.svg|The two lowest-order A + B → A + B scattering diagrams.]]

and so on. We shall determine the cross sections for such events. Our problem is to find the amplitude $\signal{\mathcal M}$ associated with a given Feynman diagram. The ritual is as follows:<sup>5</sup>

1. *Notation.* Label the incoming and outgoing four-momenta $\mechanical{p_1}$, $\mechanical{p_2}$, …, $\mechanical{p_n}$ (Fig. 6.5). Label the internal momenta $\mechanical{q_1}$, $\mechanical{q_2}$, …. Put an arrow on each line, to keep track of the “positive” direction (arbitrarily assigned, for the internal lines).[^ep-6-3-arrows]
2. *Coupling Constant.* For each vertex, write down a factor of

$$
-ig
$$

$g$ is called the *coupling constant*; it specifies the strength of the interaction between $A$, $B$, and $C$. In this toy theory $g$ has the dimensions of momentum; in the “real-world” theories we shall encounter later on the coupling constant is always dimensionless.

<!-- PDF page 211; printed page 203 -->

![[elementary-particles-fig-6-5-external-momenta.svg|**Fig. 6.5** A typical Feynman diagram, with external lines labeled (internal lines not shown).]]

3. *Propagator.* For each internal line, write a factor

$$
\frac{i}{\mechanical{q_j}^{2}-\mechanical{m_j}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}}
$$

where $\mechanical{q_j}$ is the four-momentum of the line ($\mechanical{q_j}^{2}\equiv\mechanical{q_j^\mu}\mechanical{q_{j\mu}}$) and $\mechanical{m_j}$ is the mass of the particle the line describes. (Note that $\mechanical{q_j}^{2}\ne\mechanical{m_j}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}$, because a virtual particle does not lie on its mass shell.)

4. *Conservation of Energy and Momentum.* For each vertex, write a delta function of the form

$$
(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{4}(\mechanical{k_1}+\mechanical{k_2}+\mechanical{k_3})
$$

where the $\mechanical{k}$'s are the three four-momenta coming *into* the vertex (if the arrow leads outward, then $\mechanical{k}$ is *minus* the four-momentum of that line). This factor imposes conservation of energy and momentum at each vertex, since the delta function is zero unless the sum of the incoming momenta equals the sum of the outgoing momenta.

5. *Integration over Internal Momenta.* For each internal line, write down a factor

$$
\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{4}}\,d^{4}\mechanical{q_j}
$$

and integrate over all internal momenta.

6. *Cancel the Delta Function.* The result will include a delta function

$$
(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{4}(\mechanical{p_1}+\mechanical{p_2}+\cdots-\mechanical{p_n})
$$

enforcing overall conservation of energy and momentum. Erase this factor, and what remains is $-i\signal{\mathcal M}$.[^ep-6-3-factors]

<!-- PDF page 212; printed page 204 -->

In the following sections we'll see how these rules are used to evaluate some elementary Feynman diagrams in the “*ABC* theory.”

[^ep-6-3-arrows]: Since these particles are their own antiparticles, we do not need the arrows here to keep track of *that* distinction. In later chapters we shall have to be more careful.
[^ep-6-3-factors]: Once you get used to it, steps 4, 5, and 6 can be collapsed into a single rule: “Integrate over all *undetermined* internal momenta.” This is how most books do it, right from the start, but I think the method presented here is clearer, even if it does take a little extra time. By the way, you'll notice that every delta function carries a factor of $(2\pi)^{4}$, and every four-dimensional volume element carries a factor of $(2\pi)^{-4}$. Most of these factors eventually cancel out, and you might wonder if they are really necessary (similar remarks apply to the $i$'s in the propagators and coupling constants). They *are* necessary, and the prescription given here is the most systematic way to keep track of them. Feynman is supposed to have shouted once in exasperation (at a graduate student who “couldn't be bothered with such trivial matters”), “If you can't get the $4\pi$'s in the right place, you don't know *nothing!*”

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\signal{\mathcal M}$ | — | Diagram amplitude | The amplitude calculated with the six Feynman rules. |
| $\mechanical{m_A},\mechanical{m_B},\mechanical{m_C},\mechanical{m_j}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{g}}$ | Particle masses | The toy particle masses and the mass on an internal line. |
| $g$ | $\unit{\mathrm{g\,cm\,s^{-1}}}$ | Toy coupling constant | The interaction strength for each ABC vertex. |
| $\mechanical{p_1},\mechanical{p_2},\mechanical{p_n}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{g\,cm\,s^{-1}}}$ | External four-momenta | The labeled momenta of incoming and outgoing particles. |
| $\mechanical{q_1},\mechanical{q_2},\mechanical{q_j},\mechanical{q_j^\mu},\mechanical{q_{j\mu}}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{g\,cm\,s^{-1}}}$ | Internal four-momenta | The virtual momenta and their components. |
| $\mechanical{k},\mechanical{k_1},\mechanical{k_2},\mechanical{k_3}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{g\,cm\,s^{-1}}}$ | Vertex four-momenta | The signed momenta that enter a vertex. |
| $\mechanical{c}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}}$ | Speed of light | The speed in the propagator mass term. |
