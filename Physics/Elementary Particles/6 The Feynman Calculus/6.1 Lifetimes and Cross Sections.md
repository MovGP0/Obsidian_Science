> [!TLDR]
> Decay rates determine lifetimes and branching ratios, while cross sections relate particle scattering to beam luminosity.

<!-- PDF page 197; printed page 189 -->

*In this chapter we begin the quantitative formulation of elementary particle dynamics, which amounts, in practice, to the calculation of decay rates ($\mechanical{\Gamma}$) and scattering cross sections ($\mechanical{\sigma}$). The procedure involves two distinct parts: (1) evaluation of the relevant Feynman diagrams to determine the “amplitude” ($\signal{\mathcal M}$) for the process in question, and (2) insertion of $\signal{\mathcal M}$ into Fermi's “Golden Rule” to compute $\mechanical{\Gamma}$ or $\mechanical{\sigma}$, as the case may be. To avoid distracting algebraic complications, I introduce here a simplified model. Realistic theories—QED, QCD, and GWS—are developed in succeeding chapters. If you like, Chapter 6 can be read immediately after Chapter 3. Study it with scrupulous care, or what follows will be unintelligible.*

As I mentioned in the Introduction, we have three experimental probes of elementary particle interactions: bound states, decays, and scattering. Nonrelativistic quantum mechanics (in Schrödinger's formulation) is particularly well adapted to handle bound states, which is why we used it, as far as possible, in Chapter 5. By contrast, the relativistic theory (in Feynman's formulation) is especially well suited to describe decays and scattering. In this chapter I'll introduce the basic ideas and strategies of the Feynman “calculus”; in subsequent chapters we will use it to develop the theories of strong, electromagnetic, and weak interactions.

To begin with, we must decide what physical quantities we would like to calculate. In the case of decays, the item of greatest interest is the *lifetime* of the particle in question. What precisely do we mean by the lifetime of, say, the muon? We have in mind, of course, a muon at *rest*; a moving muon lasts longer

<!-- PDF page 198; printed page 190 -->

(from our perspective) because of time dilation. But even stationary muons don't all last the same amount of time, for there is an intrinsically random element in the decay process. We cannot hope to calculate the lifetime of any *particular* muon; rather, what we are after is the *average* (or “mean”) lifetime, $\mechanical{\tau}$, of the muons in any large sample. Now, elementary particles have no memories, so the probability of a given muon decaying in the next microsecond is independent of how long ago that muon was created. (It's quite different in biological systems: An 80-year-old man is much more likely to die in the next year than is a 20-year-old, and his body shows the signs of eight decades of wear and tear. But all muons are identical, regardless of when they were produced; from an actuarial point of view they're all on an equal footing.) The critical parameter, then, is the *decay rate*, $\mechanical{\Gamma}$, the *probability per unit time* that any given muon will disintegrate. If we had a large collection of muons, say, $\mechanical{N}(\mechanical{t})$, at time $\mechanical{t}$, then $\mechanical{N}\mechanical{\Gamma}\,d\mechanical{t}$ of them would decay in the next instant $d\mechanical{t}$. This would, of course, *decrease* the number remaining:

$$
d\mechanical{N}=-\mechanical{\Gamma}\mechanical{N}\,d\mechanical{t}.
\tag{6.1}
$$

It follows that

$$
\mechanical{N}(\mechanical{t})=\mechanical{N}(0)e^{-\mechanical{\Gamma}\mechanical{t}}.
\tag{6.2}
$$

Evidently, the number of particles left decreases exponentially with time. As you can check for yourself (Problem 6.1), the mean lifetime is simply the reciprocal of the decay rate:

$$
\mechanical{\tau}=\frac{1}{\mechanical{\Gamma}}.
\tag{6.3}
$$

Actually, most particles can decay by several different routes. The $\pi^{+}$, for instance, usually decays to $\mu^{+}+\nu_\mu$, but sometimes one goes to $e^{+}+\nu_e$; occasionally, a $\pi^{+}$ decays to $\mu^{+}+\nu_\mu+\gamma$, and they have even been known to go to $e^{+}+\nu_e+\pi^{0}$. In such circumstances the *total* decay rate is the sum of the individual decay rates:

$$
\mechanical{\Gamma_{\mathrm{tot}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mechanical{\Gamma_i}
\tag{6.4}
$$

and the lifetime of the particle is the reciprocal of $\mechanical{\Gamma_{\mathrm{tot}}}$:

$$
\mechanical{\tau}=1/\mechanical{\Gamma_{\mathrm{tot}}}.
\tag{6.5}
$$

In addition to $\mechanical{\tau}$, we want to calculate the various *branching ratios*, that is, the fraction of all particles of the given type that decay by each mode. Branching ratios are determined by the decay rates:

$$
\text{Branching ratio for }i\text{th decay mode}=\mechanical{\Gamma_i}/\mechanical{\Gamma_{\mathrm{tot}}}.
\tag{6.6}
$$

For decays, then, the essential problem is to calculate the decay rate $\mechanical{\Gamma_i}$ for each mode; from there it is an easy matter to obtain the lifetime and branching ratios.

How about scattering? What quantity should the experimentalist measure and the theorist calculate? If we were talking about an *archer* aiming at a “bull's-eye,” the parameter of interest would be the *size of the target*, or more precisely

<!-- PDF page 199; printed page 191 -->

the cross-sectional area it presents to a stream of incoming arrows. In a crude sense, the same goes for elementary particle scattering: If you fire a stream of electrons into a tank of hydrogen (which is essentially a collection of protons) the parameter of interest is the size of the proton—the cross-sectional area $\mechanical{\sigma}$ it presents to the incident beam. The situation is more complicated than in archery, however, for several reasons. First of all the target is “soft”; it's not a simple case of “hit-or-miss,” but rather “the closer you come the greater the deflection.” Nevertheless, it is still possible to define an “effective” cross section; I'll show you how in the next paragraph. Second, the cross section depends on the nature of the “arrow” as well as the structure of the “target.” Electrons scatter off hydrogen more sharply than neutrinos and less so than pions, because different interactions are involved. It depends, too, on the *outgoing* particles; if the energy is high enough we can have not only *elastic* scattering ($e+p\to e+p$), but a variety of *inelastic* processes, such as $e+p\to e+p+\gamma$, or $e+p+\pi^{0}$, or even, in principle, $\nu_e+\Lambda$. Each one of these has its own (“exclusive”) scattering cross section, $\mechanical{\sigma_i}$ (for process $i$). In some experiments, however, the final products are not examined, and we are interested only in the *total* (“inclusive”) cross section.

$$
\mechanical{\sigma_{\mathrm{tot}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mechanical{\sigma_i}.
\tag{6.7}
$$

Finally, each cross section typically depends on the *velocity* of the incident particle. At the most naïve level we might expect the cross section to be proportional to the amount of time the incident particle spends in the vicinity of the target, which is to say that $\mechanical{\sigma}$ should be inversely proportional to $\mechanical{v}$. But this behavior is dramatically altered in the neighborhood of a “resonance”—a special energy at which the particles involved “like” to interact, forming a short-lived semibound state before breaking apart. Such “bumps” in the graph of $\mechanical{\sigma}$ versus $\mechanical{v}$ (or, as it is more commonly plotted, $\mechanical{\sigma}$ versus $\energy{E}$) are in fact the principal means by which short-lived particles are discovered (see Fig. 4.6). So, unlike the archer's target, there's a lot of physics in an elementary particle cross section.

Let's go back, now, to the question of what we mean by a “cross section” when the target is “soft.” Suppose a particle (maybe an electron) comes along, encounters some kind of potential (perhaps the Coulomb potential of a stationary proton), and scatters off at an angle $\mechanical{\theta}$. This *scattering angle* is a function of the *impact parameter* $\mechanical{b}$, the distance by which the incident particle would have missed the scattering center, had it continued on its original trajectory (Fig. 6.1). Ordinarily, the smaller the impact parameter, the larger the deflection, but the actual functional form of $\mechanical{\theta}(\mechanical{b})$ depends on the particular potential involved.

### Example 6.1 — Hard-Sphere Scattering

Suppose the particle bounces elastically off a sphere of radius $\mechanical{R}$. From Figure 6.2, we have

$$
\mechanical{b}=\mechanical{R}\sin\mechanical{\alpha},\qquad
2\mechanical{\alpha}+\mechanical{\theta}=\pi.
$$

Thus

$$
\sin\mechanical{\alpha}=\sin(\pi/2-\mechanical{\theta}/2)=\cos(\mechanical{\theta}/2)
$$

and hence

$$
\mechanical{b}=\mechanical{R}\cos(\mechanical{\theta}/2)
\quad\text{or}\quad
\mechanical{\theta}=2\cos^{-1}(\mechanical{b}/\mechanical{R}).
$$

This is the relation between $\mechanical{\theta}$ and $\mechanical{b}$ for classical hard-sphere scattering.

<!-- PDF page 200; printed page 192 -->

![[elementary-particles-fig-6-1-fixed-potential-scattering.svg|**Fig. 6.1** Scattering from a fixed potential; θ is the scattering angle, b is the impact parameter.]]

If the particle comes in with an impact parameter between $\mechanical{b}$ and $\mechanical{b}+d\mechanical{b}$, it will emerge with a scattering angle between $\mechanical{\theta}$ and $\mechanical{\theta}+d\mechanical{\theta}$. More generally, if it passes through an infinitesimal *area* $d\mechanical{\sigma}$, it will scatter into a corresponding *solid angle* $d\mechanical{\Omega}$ (Fig. 6.3). Naturally, the larger we make $d\mechanical{\sigma}$, the larger $d\mechanical{\Omega}$ will be. The proportionality factor is called the *differential scattering cross section*, $\mechanical{D}$:

$$
d\mechanical{\sigma}=\mechanical{D}(\mechanical{\theta})\,d\mechanical{\Omega}.
\tag{6.8}
$$

In principle, $\mechanical{D}$ might depend on the azimuthal angle $\mechanical{\phi}$; however, most potentials of interest are spherically symmetrical, in which case the differential cross section depends only on $\mechanical{\theta}$ (or, if you prefer, on $\mechanical{b}$). By the way, the notation, $\mechanical{D}$, is my own; most people call it simply $d\mechanical{\sigma}/d\mechanical{\Omega}$, and in the rest of the book I'll revert to the standard terminology. The name “differential cross section” is poorly chosen; it's not a differential at all, in the mathematical sense (the words would apply more naturally to $d\mechanical{\sigma}$ than to $d\mechanical{\sigma}/d\mechanical{\Omega}$).

Now, from Figure 6.3 we see that

$$
d\mechanical{\sigma}=\lvert\mechanical{b}\,d\mechanical{b}\,d\mechanical{\phi}\rvert,\qquad
d\mechanical{\Omega}=\lvert\sin\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\phi}\rvert.
\tag{6.9}
$$

(Areas and solid angles are intrinsically positive, hence the absolute value signs.) Accordingly,

$$
\mechanical{D}(\mechanical{\theta})=\frac{d\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\mechanical{\Omega}}
=\left\lvert\frac{\mechanical{b}}{\sin\mechanical{\theta}}
\left(\frac{d\mechanical{b}}{d\mechanical{\theta}}\right)\right\rvert.
\tag{6.10}
$$

### Example 6.2

In the case of hard-sphere scattering, Example 6.1, we find

$$
\frac{d\mechanical{b}}{d\mechanical{\theta}}
=-\frac{\mechanical{R}}{2}\sin\left(\frac{\mechanical{\theta}}{2}\right)
$$

and hence

$$
\mechanical{D}(\mechanical{\theta})
=\frac{\mechanical{R}\mechanical{b}\sin(\mechanical{\theta}/2)}{2\sin\mechanical{\theta}}
=\frac{\mechanical{R}^{2}}{2}\frac{\cos(\mechanical{\theta}/2)\sin(\mechanical{\theta}/2)}{\sin\mechanical{\theta}}
=\frac{\mechanical{R}^{2}}{4}.
$$

Finally, the *total* cross section is the integral of $d\mechanical{\sigma}$ over all solid angles:

$$
\mechanical{\sigma}=\int d\mechanical{\sigma}
=\int\mechanical{D}(\mechanical{\theta})\,d\mechanical{\Omega}.
\tag{6.11}
$$

### Example 6.3

For hard-sphere scattering

$$
\mechanical{\sigma}=\int\frac{\mechanical{R}^{2}}{4}\,d\mechanical{\Omega}
=\pi\mechanical{R}^{2}
$$

which is, of course, the total cross section the sphere presents to an incoming beam: Any particles *within* this area will scatter, any *outside* will pass by unaffected.

As Example 6.3 indicates, the formalism developed here is consistent with our naïve sense of the term “cross section,” in the case of a “hard” target; its virtue is that it applies as well to “soft” targets, which do not have sharp edges.

### Example 6.4 — Rutherford Scattering

A particle of charge $\electric{q_1}$ scatters off a stationary particle of charge $\electric{q_2}$. In classical mechanics the formula relating the impact parameter to the scattering angle is<sup>1</sup>

$$
\mechanical{b}=\frac{\electric{q_1}\electric{q_2}}{2\energy{E}}\cot(\mechanical{\theta}/2)
$$

<!-- PDF page 202; printed page 194 -->

where $\energy{E}$ is the initial kinetic energy of the incident charge. The differential cross section is therefore

$$
\mechanical{D}(\mechanical{\theta})
=\left(\frac{\electric{q_1}\electric{q_2}}{4\energy{E}\sin^{2}(\mechanical{\theta}/2)}\right)^{2}.
$$

In this case the total cross section is actually *infinite*:[^ep-6-1-range]

$$
\mechanical{\sigma}
=2\pi\left(\frac{\electric{q_1}\electric{q_2}}{4\energy{E}}\right)^{2}
\int_0^\pi\frac{1}{\sin^{4}(\mechanical{\theta}/2)}
\sin\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\theta}=\infty.
$$

Suppose now, that we have a *beam* of incoming particles, with uniform *luminosity* $\mechanical{\mathcal L}$ ($\mechanical{\mathcal L}$ is the number of particles passing down the line per unit time, per unit area). Then $d\mechanical{N}=\mechanical{\mathcal L}\,d\mechanical{\sigma}$ is the number of particles per unit time passing through area $d\mechanical{\sigma}$, and hence also the number per unit time scattered into solid angle $d\mechanical{\Omega}$:

$$
d\mechanical{N}=\mechanical{\mathcal L}\,d\mechanical{\sigma}
=\mechanical{\mathcal L}\mechanical{D}(\mechanical{\theta})\,d\mechanical{\Omega}.
$$

It follows that

$$
\frac{d\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\mechanical{\Omega}}
=\mechanical{D}(\mechanical{\theta})
=\frac{1}{\mechanical{\mathcal L}}\frac{d\mechanical{N}}{d\mechanical{\Omega}}.
\tag{6.12}
$$

This is frequently a more convenient way to think of the differential cross section: It is the number of particles per unit time scattered into solid angle $d\mechanical{\Omega}$, divided by $d\mechanical{\Omega}$ and by the luminosity. (Or, as accelerator physicists like to put it, “the event rate is the cross section times the luminosity”.)[^ep-6-1-target]

[^ep-6-1-range]: This is related to the fact that the Coulomb potential has infinite range (see footnote on p. 17).
[^ep-6-1-target]: In this discussion I have assumed that the target itself is stationary, and that the incident particle is simply *deflected* as it passes through the scattering potential. My purpose was to introduce the essential ideas in the simplest possible context. But in Section 6.2 the formalism is completely general; it includes the recoil of the target, and allows for a change in the identity of the participants during the scattering process (in the reaction $\pi^{-}+p^{+}\to K^{+}+\Sigma^{-}$, for example, $d\mechanical{\Omega}$ might represent the solid angle into which the $K^{+}$ scatters).
