> [!TLDR]
> Loop diagrams produce divergent integrals that require regularization and renormalized masses and couplings.

So far we have looked only at lowest-order (“tree level”) Feynman diagrams; in the case of $A+A\to B+B$, for instance, we considered the graph:

![[elementary-particles-toy-theory-numbered-tree.svg|Tree-level AA to BB diagram with lines numbered 1 through 5.]]

This diagram has two vertices, so that $\signal{\mathcal M}$ is proportional to $g^{2}$. But there are a number of diagrams with *four* vertices which contribute to this process. If the added line starts on line (1), it could terminate also on line (1):

![[elementary-particles-toy-theory-line1-self-loop.svg|Fourth-order diagram with the added line attached twice to line 1.]]

or it could terminate on line (2):

![[elementary-particles-toy-theory-line1-line2-loop.svg|Fourth-order diagram connecting lines 1 and 2.]]

or on line (3):

![[elementary-particles-toy-theory-line1-line3-loop.svg|Fourth-order diagram connecting lines 1 and 3.]]

or on line (4) or line (5). So there are five diagrams in which the added line attaches to line (1). There are five also, of course, in which it attaches to line (2), but we have already counted one of these (the one joining lines (1) and (2)), so there are four *new* diagrams. Likewise, three for line (3), two for line (4), and one for line (5). All told, then, there are

$$
5+4+3+2+1=15
$$

fourth-order diagrams for this process, and another 15 for the “twisted” version. (Disconnected diagrams, such as

![[elementary-particles-toy-theory-disconnected-vacuum.svg|Disconnected tree and vacuum diagrams that are excluded from the count.]]

don't count.)

I am certainly not going to evaluate all these “one-loop” diagrams (or even *think* about two-loop diagrams), but I would like to take a closer look at *one* of them—the one in which line (5) joins onto itself:

![[elementary-particles-toy-theory-internal-line-loop.svg|The internal C-line loop, with momenta q₁ through q₄ and external momenta p₁ through p₄.]]

<!-- PDF page 216; printed page 208 -->

Applying Feynman Rules 1 through 5, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
g^{4}\int
&\frac{\delta^{4}(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{q_1}-\mechanical{p_3})
\delta^{4}(\mechanical{q_1}-\mechanical{q_2}-\mechanical{q_3})
\delta^{4}(\mechanical{q_2}+\mechanical{q_3}-\mechanical{q_4})
\delta^{4}(\mechanical{q_4}+\mechanical{p_2}-\mechanical{p_4})}
{(\mechanical{q_1}^{2}-\mechanical{m_C}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2})
(\mechanical{q_2}^{2}-\mechanical{m_A}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2})
(\mechanical{q_1}^{2}-\mechanical{m_B}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2})
(\mechanical{q_4}^{2}-\mechanical{m_C}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2})}\\
&\qquad\times d^{4}\mechanical{q_1}\,d^{4}\mechanical{q_2}\,d^{4}\mechanical{q_3}\,d^{4}\mechanical{q_4}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.55}
$$

Integration over $\mechanical{q_1}$, using the first delta function, replaces $\mechanical{q_1}$ by $(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3})$; integration over $\mechanical{q_4}$, using the last delta function, replaces $\mechanical{q_4}$ by $(\mechanical{p_4}-\mechanical{p_2})$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{g^{4}}
{[(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3})^{2}-\mechanical{m_C}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}]
[(\mechanical{p_4}-\mechanical{p_2})^{2}-\mechanical{m_C}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}]}\\
&\qquad\times\int
\frac{\delta^{4}(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3}-\mechanical{q_2}-\mechanical{q_3})
\delta^{4}(\mechanical{q_2}+\mechanical{q_3}-\mechanical{p_4}+\mechanical{p_2})}
{(\mechanical{q_2}^{2}-\mechanical{m_A}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2})
(\mechanical{q_3}^{2}-\mechanical{m_B}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2})}
\,d^{4}\mechanical{q_2}\,d^{4}\mechanical{q_3}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.56}
$$

Here the first delta function replaces $\mechanical{q_2}$ by $\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3}-\mechanical{q_3}$, so the second delta function becomes

$$
\delta^{4}(\mechanical{p_1}+\mechanical{p_2}-\mechanical{p_3}-\mechanical{p_4})
$$

which, by rule 6, we erase, leaving

$$
\signal{\mathcal M}
=i\left(\frac{g}{2\pi}\right)^{4}
\frac{1}{[(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3})^{2}-\mechanical{m_C}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}]^{2}}
\int\frac{1}
{[(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3}-\mechanical{q})^{2}-\mechanical{m_A}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}]
(\mechanical{q}^{2}-\mechanical{m_B}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2})}\,d^{4}\mechanical{q}.
\tag{6.57}
$$

(I drop the subscript on $\mechanical{q_3}$ at this point.)

You can try calculating this integral, if you've got the energy, but I'll tell you right now you're going to hit a snag. For the four-dimensional volume element could be written $d^{4}\mechanical{q}=\mechanical{q}^{3}\,d\mechanical{q}\,d\mechanical{\Omega'}$ (where $d\mechanical{\Omega'}$ stands for the angular part) (just as in two-dimensional polar coordinates the element of area is $\mechanical{r}\,d\mechanical{r}\,d\mechanical{\theta}$ and in three-dimensional spherical coordinates the volume element is $\mechanical{r}^{2}\,d\mechanical{r}\sin\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\phi}$). At large $\mechanical{q}$ the integrand is essentially just $1/\mechanical{q}^{4}$, so the $\mechanical{q}$ integral has the form

$$
\int^\infty\frac{1}{\mechanical{q}^{4}}\mechanical{q}^{3}\,d\mechanical{q}
=\ln\mechanical{q}\big\rvert^\infty=\infty.
\tag{6.58}
$$

The integral is logarithmically divergent at large $\mechanical{q}$. This disaster, in one form or another, held up the development of quantum electrodynamics for nearly two decades, until, through the combined efforts of many great physicists—from Dirac, Pauli, Kramers, Weisskopf, and Bethe through Tomonaga, Schwinger, and Feynman—systematic methods were developed for “sweeping the infinities under the rug.” The first step is to *regularize* the integral, using a suitable cutoff procedure that renders it finite without spoiling other desirable features (such as Lorentz invariance). In the case of equation (6.57) this can be accomplished by introducing a factor

$$
\frac{-\mechanical{M}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}}
{(\mechanical{q}^{2}-\mechanical{M}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2})}
\tag{6.59}
$$

<!-- PDF page 217; printed page 209 -->

under the integral sign. The *cutoff mass* $\mechanical{M}$ is assumed to be very large, and will be taken to infinity at the end of the calculation (note that the “fudge factor” (6.59) goes to 1 as $\mechanical{M}\to\infty$).[^ep-6-6-artificial] The integral can now be calculated<sup>6</sup> and separated into two parts: a finite term, independent of $\mechanical{M}$, and a term involving (in this case) the logarithm of $\mechanical{M}$, which blows up as $\mechanical{M}\to\infty$.

At this point a miraculous thing happens: all the divergent, $\mechanical{M}$-dependent terms appear in the final answer in the form of *additions to the masses and the coupling constant*. If we take this seriously, it means that the *physical* masses and couplings are not the $\mechanical{m}$'s and $g$'s that appeared in the original Feynman rules, but rather the “renormalized” ones, containing these extra factors:

$$
\mechanical{m_{\mathrm{physical}}}=\mechanical{m}+\mechanical{\delta m};
\qquad g_{\mathrm{physical}}=g+\delta g.
\tag{6.60}
$$

The fact that $\mechanical{\delta m}$ and $\delta g$ are infinite (in the limit $\mechanical{M}\to\infty$) is disturbing, but not catastrophic, for we never *measure* them anyway; all we ever see in the laboratory are the *physical* values, and these are (obviously) finite (evidently the unmeasurable “bare” masses and couplings, $\mechanical{m}$ and $g$, contain compensating infinities).[^ep-6-6-classical] As a practical matter, we take account of the infinities by using the *physical* values of $\mechanical{m}$ and $g$ in the Feynman rules, and then systematically ignoring the divergent contributions from higher-order diagrams.

Meanwhile, there remain the *finite* ($\mechanical{M}$-independent) contributions from the loop diagrams. They, too, lead to modifications in $\mechanical{m}$ and $g$ (perfectly calculable ones, in this case)—which, however, are functions of the four-momentum of the line in which the loop is inserted ($\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3}$ in the example). This means that the *effective* masses and coupling constants actually depend on the *energies* of the particles involved; we call them “running” masses and “running” coupling constants. The dependence is typically rather slight, at low energies, and can ordinarily be ignored, but it does have observable consequences, in the form of the Lamb shift (in QED) and asymptotic freedom (in QCD).[^ep-6-6-effective]

The procedure I have sketched in the last three paragraphs is called *renormalization*.

<!-- PDF page 218; printed page 210 -->

If *all* the infinities arising from higher-order diagrams can be accommodated in this way, we say that the theory is *renormalizable*. *ABC* theory and quantum electrodynamics are renormalizable. In the early seventies 't Hooft showed that all *gauge* theories, including chromodynamics and the electroweak theory of Glashow, Weinberg, and Salam, are renormalizable. This was a profoundly important discovery, because, beyond lowest-order calculations, a nonrenormalizable theory yields answers that are cutoff-dependent and, therefore, really, quite meaningless.

[^ep-6-6-artificial]: No one would deny that this procedure is artificial. Still, it can be argued that expression (6.59) merely reflects our ignorance of the high-energy (short distance) behavior of quantum field theory. Perhaps the Feynman propagators are not quite right in this régime, and $\mechanical{M}$ is simply a crude way of accounting for the unknown modification. (This would be the case, for example, if the “particles” have substructure that becomes relevant at extremely close range.) Dirac once remarked:

    > It's just a stop-gap procedure. There must be some fundamental change in our ideas, probably a change just as fundamental as the passage from Bohr's orbit theory to quantum mechanics. When you get a number turning out to be infinite which ought to be finite, you should admit that there is something wrong with your equations, and not hope that you can get a good theory just by doctoring up that number. [From P. Buckley and F. D. Peat, *A Question of Physics* (Toronto: University of Toronto Press, 1979), page 39.]

[^ep-6-6-classical]: In case it is some comfort, I should point out that essentially the same thing occurs in *classical* electrodynamics; the electrostatic energy of a point charge is infinite, and makes an infinite contribution (via $\energy{E}=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}$) to the particle's mass. Perhaps this means that there are no true point charges, in classical electrodynamics; perhaps that's what it means in quantum field theory, too. In neither case, however, do we know how to avoid the point particle as a theoretical construct.
[^ep-6-6-effective]: A *physical interpretation* of the running coupling constant in *QED* and *QCD* was suggested in Chapter 2, Section 2.3. A nice explanation of mass renormalization is given by P. Nelson in *American Scientist*, **73**, 66 (1985):

    According to renormalization theory, not only the strengths of the various interactions but the masses of the participating particles appear to vary on differing length scales. To get a feel for this seemingly paradoxical statement, imagine firing a cannon underwater. Even neglecting friction, the trajectory will be very different from the corresponding one on land, since the cannonball must now drag with it a considerable amount of water, modifying its apparent, or “effective,” mass. We can experimentally measure the cannonball's effective mass by shaking it to and fro at a rate $\mechanical{\omega}$, computing the mass from $\mechanical{F}=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{a}$. (This is how astronauts “weigh” themselves in space.) Having found the effective mass, we can now replace the difficult problem of underwater ballistics by a simplified approximation: we ignore the water altogether, but in Newton's equations we simply replace the true cannonball mass by the effective mass. The complicated details of the interaction with the medium are thus reduced to determining one effective parameter.

    A key feature of this approach is that the effective mass so computed depends on $\mechanical{\omega}$, since as $\mechanical{\omega}$ approaches zero, for example, the water has no effect whatever. In other words, the presence of a medium can introduce a scale-dependent effective mass. We say that the effective mass is “renormalized” by the medium. In quantum physics, every particle moves through a “medium” consisting of the quantum fluctuations of all particles present in the theory. We again take into account this medium by ignoring it but changing the values of our parameters to scale-dependent “effective” values.
