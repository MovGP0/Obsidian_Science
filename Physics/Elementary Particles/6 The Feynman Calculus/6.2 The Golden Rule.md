> [!TLDR]
> The Golden Rule combines an interaction amplitude with phase space to give decay rates and scattering cross sections.

<!-- PDF page 202; printed page 194 -->

In Section 6.1 I introduced the basic physical quantities we need to calculate: decay rates and scattering cross sections. In either case there are two ingredients in the recipe: (1) the *amplitude* ($\signal{\mathcal M}$) for the process and (2) the *phase space* available.[^ep-6-2-names] The amplitude contains all the *dynamical* information; we calculate it by evaluating the relevant Feynman diagrams, using the “Feynman rules” appropriate to the interaction in question. The phase space factor contains only *kinematical* information; it depends on the masses, energies, and momenta of the participants, and reflects the fact that a given process is more likely to occur

<!-- PDF page 203; printed page 195 -->

the more “room to maneuver” there is in the final state. For example, the decay of a heavy particle into many light secondaries involves a large phase space factor, for there are many different ways to apportion the available energy. By contrast, the decay of the neutron ($n\to p+e+\bar\nu_e$), in which there is almost no extra mass to spare, is tightly constrained, and the phase space factor is very small.[^ep-6-2-forbidden]

The transition rate for a given process is determined by the amplitude and the phase space according to Fermi's “Golden Rule”:

$$
\text{transition rate}=\frac{2\pi}{\energy{\hbar}}\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}\times(\text{phase space}).
\tag{6.13}
$$

A derivation of the Golden Rule in the nonrelativistic context will be found in any quantum mechanics text;<sup>2</sup> for the relativistic version one must consult a book on quantum field theory.<sup>3</sup> We shall not go into that here; for our purposes it will suffice to quote the quantitative formulation of the Golden Rule in the two cases of interest:

***Golden Rule for Decays.*** Suppose particle 1 decays into several other particles 2, 3, 4, …, $n$:

$$
1\to2+3+4+\cdots+n.
\tag{6.14}
$$

The decay rate is given by the formula[^ep-6-2-grouping]

$$
\begin{aligned}
d\mechanical{\Gamma}
={}&\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}\frac{S}{2\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{m_1}}
\left[
\left(\frac{\mechanical{c}\,d^{3}\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}}{(2\pi)^{3}2\energy{E_2}}\right)
\left(\frac{\mechanical{c}\,d^{3}\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}}{(2\pi)^{3}2\energy{E_3}}\right)\cdots
\left(\frac{\mechanical{c}\,d^{3}\mechanical{\mathbf p_n}}{(2\pi)^{3}2\energy{E_n}}\right)
\right]\\
&\qquad\times(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{4}(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_2}-\mechanical{p_3}-\cdots-\mechanical{p_n})
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.15}
$$

where $\mechanical{p_i}=(\energy{E_i}/\mechanical{c},\mechanical{\mathbf p_i})$ is the four-momentum of the $i$th particle (which carries mass $\mechanical{m_i}$, so that $\energy{E_i}^{2}-\mechanical{\mathbf p_i}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}=\mechanical{m_i}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{4}$).[^ep-6-2-energy] The delta function[^ep-6-2-delta] enforces conservation of energy and momentum; it is zero unless $\mechanical{p_1}=\mechanical{p_2}+\mechanical{p_3}+\cdots+\mechanical{p_n}$. The decaying particle is presumed to be at rest: $\mechanical{p_1}=(\mechanical{m_1}\mechanical{c},\mathbf0)$. $S$ is a product of statistical factors: $1/j!$ for each group of $j$ identical particles in the final state.

Equation (6.15) determines the *differential* rate for a decay in which the three-momentum of particle 2 lies in the range $d^{3}\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}$ about the value $\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}$, that of particle 3 lies in the range $d^{3}\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}$ about $\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}$, and so on. Ordinarily, we are not interested in the individual momenta of the decay products, and so we integrate over all

<!-- PDF page 204; printed page 196 -->

outgoing momenta to get the total decay rate $\mechanical{\Gamma}$ for mode in question (6.14). In particular, if there are only two particles in the final state

$$
\mechanical{\Gamma}=\frac{S}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{m_1}}
\left(\frac{\mechanical{c}}{4\pi}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{2}
\int\frac{\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}}{\energy{E_2}\energy{E_3}}
\delta^{4}(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_2}-\mechanical{p_3})
\,d^{3}\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}\,d^{3}\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}.
\tag{6.16}
$$

In general, the amplitude $\signal{\mathcal M}$ is a function of $\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}$ and $\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}$, and it cannot be taken outside the integral. Nevertheless, for a two-body decay we can carry out the integration explicitly, without knowing the functional form of $\signal{\mathcal M}$, as the following two examples show. Because the general case (Example 6.6) involves some cumbersome algebra, we shall first consider the special case in which the decay products are both massless (Example 6.5).

### Example 6.5

A particle of mass $\mechanical{m}$ decays into two massless secondaries (for instance, $\pi^{0}\to\gamma+\gamma$). If the amplitude for the process is $\signal{\mathcal M}(\mechanical{\mathbf p_2},\mechanical{\mathbf p_3})$, find the decay rate.

*Solution.* First, rewrite the delta function, using the fact that $\energy{E_1}=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}$ and $\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}=\mathbf0$:

$$
\delta^{4}(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_2}-\mechanical{p_3})
=\delta\left(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}-\frac{\energy{E_2}}{\mechanical{c}}-\frac{\energy{E_3}}{\mechanical{c}}\right)
\delta^{3}(-\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}-\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}).
\tag{6.17}
$$

Since $\mechanical{m_2}=\mechanical{m_3}=0$, we have $\energy{E_2}=\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}\rvert\mechanical{c}$, $\energy{E_3}=\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}\rvert\mechanical{c}$. Thus

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{\Gamma}
={}&\frac{S}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{m}}
\left(\frac{1}{4\pi}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{2}
\int\frac{\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}}{\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}\rvert\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}\rvert}\\
&\qquad\times\delta(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}-\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}\rvert-\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}\rvert)
\delta^{3}(-\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}-\mechanical{\mathbf p_3})
\,d^{3}\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}\,d^{3}\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.18}
$$

Next, use $\delta^{3}(-\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}-\mechanical{\mathbf p_3})$ to do the $\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}$ integral. This simply replaces every $\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}$ by $-\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}$, reflecting the conservation of momentum:

$$
\mechanical{\Gamma}=\frac{S}{2(4\pi)^{2}\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{m}}
\int\frac{\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}}{\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}\rvert^{2}}
\delta(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}-2\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}\rvert)\,d^{3}\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}.
\tag{6.19}
$$

At this stage $\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}$ is a function of $\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}$ alone; indeed, since it has to be a *scalar*, it can only depend on $\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}\rvert$.[^ep-6-2-spin] Going to spherical coordinates,

$$
d^{3}\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}=\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}\rvert^{2}\,
d\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}\rvert\sin\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\phi}
\tag{6.20}
$$

and performing the angular integration, $\int\sin\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\phi}=4\pi$, we have

$$
\mechanical{\Gamma}=\frac{S}{8\pi\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{m}}
\int_0^\infty\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}
\delta(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}-2\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}\rvert)\,d\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}\rvert.
\tag{6.21}
$$

<!-- PDF page 205; printed page 197 -->

Now [see eq. (A.9)]

$$
\delta(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}-2\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}\rvert)
=\frac{1}{2}\delta\left(\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}\rvert-\frac{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}}{2}\right)
\tag{6.22}
$$

and we conclude that

$$
\mechanical{\Gamma}=\frac{S}{16\pi\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{m}}\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}
\tag{6.23}
$$

where $\signal{\mathcal M}$ is evaluated at the momenta dictated by the conservation laws, to wit: $\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}=-\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}$ and $\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}\rvert=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}/2$.[^ep-6-2-identical]

### Example 6.6 — Two-Body Decay

Consider now the *general* case of a two-body decay, in which the outgoing particles carry masses $\mechanical{m_2}$ and $\mechanical{m_3}$. Find the decay rate, assuming $\signal{\mathcal M}$ is given.

*Solution.* Again, we begin by rewriting the delta function, as in equation (6.17), and performing the $\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}$ integral; but this time $\energy{E_2}=\mechanical{c}\sqrt{\mechanical{m_2}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}^{2}}$, $\energy{E_3}=\mechanical{c}\sqrt{\mechanical{m_3}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}^{2}}$, so in place of equation (6.19) we have

$$
\mechanical{\Gamma}=\frac{S}{2(4\pi)^{2}\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{m_1}}
\int\frac{\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}
\delta\left(\mechanical{m_1}\mechanical{c}
-\sqrt{\mechanical{m_2}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}^{2}}
-\sqrt{\mechanical{m_3}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}^{2}}\right)}
{\sqrt{\mechanical{m_2}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}^{2}}
\sqrt{\mechanical{m_3}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}^{2}}}\,d^{3}\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}.
\tag{6.24}
$$

As before, $\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}$ is now a function only of $\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}\rvert$, so we can introduce spherical coordinates and do the angular integration:

$$
\mechanical{\Gamma}=\frac{S}{8\pi\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{m_1}}
\int_0^\infty
\frac{\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}
\delta\left(\mechanical{m_1}\mechanical{c}
-\sqrt{\mechanical{m_2}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\rho}^{2}}
-\sqrt{\mechanical{m_3}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\rho}^{2}}\right)}
{\sqrt{\mechanical{m_2}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\rho}^{2}}
\sqrt{\mechanical{m_3}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}^{2}}}
\mechanical{\rho}^{2}\,d\mechanical{\rho}
\tag{6.25}
$$

where $\mechanical{\rho}$ is shorthand for $\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}\rvert$. We could use the general formula (A.13) to reduce the remaining delta function, but it is simpler, and more illuminating, to make a change of variables: Let

$$
\energy{E}=\mechanical{c}\left(
\sqrt{\mechanical{m_2}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\rho}^{2}}
+\sqrt{\mechanical{m_3}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\rho}^{2}}\right).
\tag{6.26}
$$

(Physically, $\energy{E}$ represents the total energy of the outgoing particles—hence the choice of letter.) It follows that

$$
d\energy{E}=\frac{\energy{E}\mechanical{\rho}}
{\sqrt{\mechanical{m_2}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\rho}^{2}}
\sqrt{\mechanical{m_3}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\rho}^{2}}}\,d\mechanical{\rho}
\tag{6.27}
$$

and therefore

$$
\mechanical{\Gamma}=\frac{S}{8\pi\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{m_1}}
\int_{(\mechanical{m_2}+\mechanical{m_3})\mechanical{c}^{2}}^\infty
\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}\frac{\mechanical{\rho}}{\energy{E}}
\delta\left(\mechanical{m_1}\mechanical{c}-\frac{\energy{E}}{\mechanical{c}}\right)\,d\energy{E}.
\tag{6.28}
$$

<!-- PDF page 206; printed page 198 -->

But [eq. (A.9)]

$$
\delta(\mechanical{m_1}\mechanical{c}-\energy{E}/\mechanical{c})
=\mechanical{c}\,\delta(\energy{E}-\mechanical{m_1}\mechanical{c}^{2})
\tag{6.29}
$$

and we conclude that

$$
\mechanical{\Gamma}=\frac{S\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}\mechanical{\rho_0}}
{8\pi\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{m_1}^{2}\mechanical{c}}
\tag{6.30}
$$

provided $\mechanical{m_1}>(\mechanical{m_2}+\mechanical{m_3})$; otherwise the delta function spike is outside the domain of integration and we get $\mechanical{\Gamma}=0$, recording the fact that a particle cannot decay into heavier secondaries. Here $\mechanical{\rho_0}$ is the value of $\mechanical{\rho}$ for which $\energy{E}=\mechanical{m_1}\mechanical{c}^{2}$. Solving equation (6.26), we find (Problem 6.5) that

$$
\mechanical{\rho_0}=\frac{\mechanical{c}}{2\mechanical{m_1}}
\sqrt{\mechanical{m_1}^{4}+\mechanical{m_2}^{4}+\mechanical{m_3}^{4}
-2\mechanical{m_1}^{2}\mechanical{m_2}^{2}
-2\mechanical{m_1}^{2}\mechanical{m_3}^{2}
-2\mechanical{m_2}^{2}\mechanical{m_3}^{2}}.
\tag{6.31}
$$

Remember that $\mechanical{\rho}$ was short for the *variable* $\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}\rvert$; $\mechanical{\rho_0}$ is the *particular value* of $\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}\rvert$ that is consistent with conservation of energy, and equation (6.31) simply reproduces the result we obtained back in Chapter 3 (see Problem 3.16). In more comprehensible notation, then,

$$
\mechanical{\Gamma}=\frac{S\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p}\rvert}
{8\pi\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{m_1}^{2}\mechanical{c}}
\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}
\tag{6.32}
$$

where $\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p}\rvert$ is the magnitude of either outgoing momentum, given in terms of the three masses by equation (6.31), and $\signal{\mathcal M}$ is evaluated at the momenta required by the conservation laws. Notice that if $\mechanical{m_2}=\mechanical{m_3}=0$, then $\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p}\rvert=\mechanical{m_1}\mechanical{c}/2$ and we recover equation (6.23).

The final two-body decay formula (6.32) is surprisingly simple and general. We shall use it frequently in later chapters. Unfortunately, when there are three or more particles in the final state the integrals cannot be done until we know the specific functional form of $\signal{\mathcal M}$ for the process in question. In such cases (of which we shall encounter mercifully few) you have to go back to the Golden Rule, and work it out from scratch.

***Golden Rule for Scattering*** Suppose particles 1 and 2 collide, producing particles 3, 4, …, $n$:

$$
1+2\to3+4+\cdots+n.
\tag{6.33}
$$

The cross section is given by the formula

$$
\begin{aligned}
d\mechanical{\sigma}
={}&\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}
\frac{\energy{\hbar}^{2}S}
{4\sqrt{(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2})^{2}
-(\mechanical{m_1}\mechanical{m_2}\mechanical{c}^{2})^{2}}}
\left[
\left(\frac{\mechanical{c}\,d^{3}\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}}{(2\pi)^{3}2\energy{E_3}}\right)
\left(\frac{\mechanical{c}\,d^{3}\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}}{(2\pi)^{3}2\energy{E_4}}\right)\cdots
\left(\frac{\mechanical{c}\,d^{3}\mechanical{\mathbf p_n}}{(2\pi)^{3}2\energy{E_n}}\right)
\right]\\
&\qquad\times(2\pi)^{4}
\delta^{4}(\mechanical{p_1}+\mechanical{p_2}-\mechanical{p_3}-\mechanical{p_4}-\cdots-\mechanical{p_n}),
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.34}
$$

where, as before, $\mechanical{p_i}=(\energy{E_i}/\mechanical{c},\mechanical{\mathbf p_i})$ is the four-momentum of particle $i$ (mass $\mechanical{m_i}$), $\energy{E_i}=\mechanical{c}\sqrt{\mechanical{m_i}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\mathbf p_i}^{2}}$, and $S$ is a statistical factor ($1/j!$ for each group of $j$ identical

![[elementary-particles-fig-6-4-two-body-cm-scattering.svg|**Fig. 6.4** Two-body scattering in the CM frame.]]

particles in the final state). Note that the delta function enforces conservation of energy and momentum.

Equation (6.34) determines the cross section for a process in which the three-momentum of particle 3 lies in the range $d^{3}\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}$ about the value $\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}$, that of particle 4 falls in the range $d^{3}\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}$ about $\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}$, and so on. In a typical situation we study only the angle at which particle 3 emerges. In that case we integrate over all the *other* momenta ($\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}$, $\mechanical{\mathbf p_5}$, …, $\mechanical{\mathbf p_n}$), and over the magnitude of $\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}$; what's left gives us $d\mechanical{\sigma}/d\mechanical{\Omega}$, the differential cross section for the scattering of particle 3 into solid angle $d\mechanical{\Omega}$.

### Example 6.7 — Two-Body Scattering in the CM Frame

Consider the process

$$
1+2\to3+4
\tag{6.35}
$$

in the *CM* frame (Fig. 6.4). If the amplitude is $\signal{\mathcal M}$, calculate the differential cross section.

*Solution.* In the *CM* frame, $\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}=-\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}$, and hence $\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2}=\energy{E_1}\energy{E_2}/\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}^{2}$. It follows, after some simple algebra (see Problem 6.7), that

$$
\sqrt{(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2})^{2}
-(\mechanical{m_1}\mechanical{m_2}\mechanical{c}^{2})^{2}}
=(\energy{E_1}+\energy{E_2})\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}\rvert/\mechanical{c}.
\tag{6.36}
$$

Thus

$$
d\mechanical{\sigma}
=\left(\frac{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}{8\pi}\right)^{2}
\frac{S\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}\mechanical{c}}
{(\energy{E_1}+\energy{E_2})\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}\rvert}
\frac{d^{3}\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}\,d^{3}\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}}{\energy{E_3}\energy{E_4}}
\delta^{4}(\mechanical{p_1}+\mechanical{p_2}-\mechanical{p_3}-\mechanical{p_4}).
\tag{6.37}
$$

As usual, we begin by rewriting the delta function:[^ep-6-2-fixed]

$$
\delta^{4}(\mechanical{p_1}+\mechanical{p_2}-\mechanical{p_3}-\mechanical{p_4})
=\delta\left(\frac{\energy{E_1}+\energy{E_2}-\energy{E_3}-\energy{E_4}}{\mechanical{c}}\right)
\delta^{3}(-\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}-\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}).
\tag{6.38}
$$

Next we express the outgoing energies in terms of $\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}$ and $\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}$ ($\energy{E_i}=\mechanical{c}\sqrt{\mechanical{m_i}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\mathbf p_i}^{2}}$, and carry out the $\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}$ integral (which sends $\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}\to-\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}$):[^ep-6-2-notation]

<!-- PDF page 208; printed page 200 -->

$$
\begin{aligned}
d\mechanical{\sigma}
={}&\left(\frac{\energy{\hbar}}{8\pi}\right)^{2}
\frac{S\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}\mechanical{c}}
{(\energy{E_1}+\energy{E_2})\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}\rvert}\\
&\qquad\times
\frac{\delta\left((\energy{E_1}+\energy{E_2})/\mechanical{c}
-\sqrt{\mechanical{m_3}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}^{2}}
-\sqrt{\mechanical{m_4}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}^{2}}\right)}
{\sqrt{\mechanical{m_3}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}^{2}}
\sqrt{\mechanical{m_4}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}^{2}}}\,d^{3}\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.39}
$$

This time, however, $\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}$ depends on the *direction* of $\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}$ as well as its magnitude,[^ep-6-2-direction] so we cannot carry out the angular integration. Writing

$$
d^{3}\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}=\mechanical{\rho}^{2}\,d\mechanical{\rho}\,d\mechanical{\Omega}
\tag{6.40}
$$

(where $\mechanical{\rho}$ is shorthand for $\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}\rvert$, and $d\mechanical{\Omega}=\sin\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\phi}$), we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\mechanical{\Omega}}
={}&\left(\frac{\energy{\hbar}}{8\pi}\right)^{2}
\frac{S\mechanical{c}}{(\energy{E_1}+\energy{E_2})\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}\rvert}
\int_0^\infty\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}\\
&\qquad\times
\frac{\delta\left((\energy{E_1}+\energy{E_2})/\mechanical{c}
-\sqrt{\mechanical{m_3}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\rho}^{2}}
-\sqrt{\mechanical{m_4}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\rho}^{2}}\right)}
{\sqrt{\mechanical{m_3}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\rho}^{2}}
\sqrt{\mechanical{m_4}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\rho}^{2}}}
\mechanical{\rho}^{2}\,d\mechanical{\rho}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.41}
$$

The integral over $\mechanical{\rho}$ is the same as in equation (6.25), with $\mechanical{m_2}\to\mechanical{m_4}$ and $\mechanical{m_1}\to(\energy{E_1}+\energy{E_2})/\mechanical{c}^{2}$. Quoting our previous result (6.32), I conclude that

$$
\frac{d\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\mechanical{\Omega}}
=\left(\frac{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}{8\pi}\right)^{2}
\frac{S\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}}{(\energy{E_1}+\energy{E_2})^{2}}
\frac{\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_f}\rvert}{\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_i}\rvert}
\tag{6.42}
$$

where $\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_f}\rvert$ is the magnitude of either outgoing momentum and $\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_i}\rvert$ is the magnitude of either incoming momentum.

As in the case of decays, the two-body final state is peculiarly simple, in the sense that we are able to carry the calculation through to the end *without knowing the explicit functional form of* $\signal{\mathcal M}$. We will be using equation (6.42) frequently in later chapters.

By the way, lifetimes obviously carry the dimensions of time (seconds); decay rates ($\mechanical{\Gamma}=1/\mechanical{\tau}$), therefore, are measured in inverse seconds. Cross sections, have dimensions of *area*—$\unit{\mathrm{cm^{2}}}$, or, more conveniently, “barns”:

$$
1\,\unit{\mathrm{barn}}=10^{-24}\,\unit{\mathrm{cm^{2}}}.
\tag{6.43}
$$

Differential cross sections, $d\mechanical{\sigma}/d\mechanical{\Omega}$, are given in barns per steradian, or simply barns (steradians, like radians, being dimensionless). The amplitude, $\signal{\mathcal M}$, has units which depend on the number of particles involved: If there are $n$ external lines (incoming plus outgoing), the dimensions of $\signal{\mathcal M}$ are those of momentum raised to the power $4-n$:

$$
\text{Dimensions of }\signal{\mathcal M}=(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})^{4-n}.
\tag{6.44}
$$

<!-- PDF page 209; printed page 201 -->

For example, in a three-body process ($A\to B+C$), $\signal{\mathcal M}$ has dimensions of momentum; in a four-body process ($A\to B+C+D$, or $A+B\to C+D$), $\signal{\mathcal M}$ is dimensionless. You can check for yourself that the two Golden Rules then yield the correct units for $\mechanical{\Gamma}$ and $\mechanical{\sigma}$.

[^ep-6-2-identical]: In the specific case $\pi^{0}\to\gamma+\gamma$ there are two identical particles in the final state, so $S=\tfrac{1}{2}$. For $\pi^{0}\to\nu+\bar\nu$, $S=1$.
[^ep-6-2-fixed]: Observe that $\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}$ and $\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}$ are *fixed* vectors (related by your choice of reference frame: $\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}=-\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}$), but at this stage $\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}$ and $\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}$ are integration variables. It is only *after* the $\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}$ integration that they are restricted ($\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}=-\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}$), and after the $\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}\rvert$ integration that they are determined by the scattering angle $\mechanical{\theta}$.
[^ep-6-2-notation]: I follow the standard (sloppy) notation, in which we use the same symbol, $d\mechanical{\sigma}$, before and after integration over $\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}$ (and indeed over $\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}\rvert$ as well, as you'll see in a moment).
[^ep-6-2-direction]: In general, $\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}$ depends on all four momenta. However, in this case $\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}=-\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}$ and $\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}=-\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}$, so it remains a function only of $\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}$ and $\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}$ (assuming again that spin does not come into it). From these vectors we can construct three scalars: $\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}=\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}\rvert^{2}$, $\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}=\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}\rvert^{2}$, and $\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}=\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}\rvert\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}\rvert\cos\mechanical{\theta}$. But $\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}$ is fixed, so the only integration variables on which $\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}$ can depend are $\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}\rvert$ and $\mechanical{\theta}$.
