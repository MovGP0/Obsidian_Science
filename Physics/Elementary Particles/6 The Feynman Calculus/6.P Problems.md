**6.1.** Derive equation (6.3). [*Hint*: What fraction of the original sample decays between $\mechanical{t}$ and $\mechanical{t}+d\mechanical{t}$? What, then, is the probability of any given particle decaying between $\mechanical{t}$ and $\mechanical{t}+d\mechanical{t}$? Take it from there.]

**6.2.** Nuclear physicists traditionally work with “half-life” ($\mechanical{t_{1/2}}$) instead of mean life ($\mechanical{\tau}$); $\mechanical{t_{1/2}}$ is the time it takes for half the members of a large sample to decay. Show that for exponential decay [eq. (6.2)]

$$
\mechanical{t_{1/2}}=\mechanical{\tau}\ln2.
$$

**6.3.** (a) Suppose you started out with a million muons (at rest); how many would still be around $2.2\times10^{-5}\,\unit{\mathrm{sec}}$ later? (b) What is the probability of a $\pi^{-}$ lasting more than $1\,\unit{\mathrm{sec}}$?

**6.4.** A nonrelativistic particle of mass $\mechanical{m}$ scatters from a fixed repulsive potential, $\energy{V}(\mechanical{r})=k/\mechanical{r}^{2}$, where $k$ is a constant.

(a) Find the scattering angle, $\mechanical{\theta}$, as a function of the impact parameter, $\mechanical{b}$.

(b) Determine the differential cross section $d\mechanical{\sigma}/d\mechanical{\Omega}$.

(c) Find the total cross section.

[*References*: Goldstein (ref. 1), p. 108, eq. (3-97), and Becker, *Introduction to Theoretical Mechanics* (New York: McGraw-Hill, 1954), p. 231, Example 10-3.]

**6.5.** Derive equation (6.31), using definition (6.26).

**6.6.** As an application of Example 6.5, consider the decay of $\pi^{0}\to\gamma+\gamma$. Of course, the $\pi^{0}$ is a composite object ($u\bar u$ and $d\bar d$), and so equation (6.23) does not really apply. But let's pretend that the $\pi^{0}$ is a true elementary particle, and see how close we come. Unfortunately, we don't know the amplitude $\signal{\mathcal M}$; however, it must have the dimensions of mass times velocity [eq. (6.44)], and there is only one mass and one velocity available. Moreover, the emission of each photon introduces a factor of $\sqrt{\electric{\alpha}}$ (the fine structure constant) into $\signal{\mathcal M}$, as we shall see in Chapter 7, so the amplitude must be proportional to $\electric{\alpha}$. On this basis, estimate the lifetime of the $\pi^{0}$. Compare the experimental value. [Evidently, the decay of the $\pi^{0}$ is a much more complicated process than this crude model suggests. See C. Quigg, *Gauge Theories of the Strong, Weak, and Electromagnetic Interactions*, Reading, MA: Benjamin/Cummings, 1983, eq. (1.2.25).]

**6.7.** (a) Derive equation (6.36) for scattering of particles 1 and 2 in the *CM*.

(b) Obtain the corresponding formula for the lab frame (particle 2 at rest).

$$
\left[\textit{Answer: }\sqrt{(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2})^{2}
-(\mechanical{m_1}\mechanical{m_2}\mechanical{c}^{2})^{2}}
=\mechanical{m_2}\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}\rvert\mechanical{c}\right]
$$

**6.8.** Consider the case of elastic scattering, $A+B\to A+B$, in the lab frame ($B$ initially at rest) assuming the target is so heavy ($\mechanical{m_B}\mechanical{c}^{2}\gg\energy{E_A}$) that its recoil is negligible. Use equation (6.34) to determine the differential scattering cross section.

$$
\left[\textit{Answer: }(d\mechanical{\sigma}/d\mechanical{\Omega})
=(\energy{\hbar}/8\pi\mechanical{m_B}\mechanical{c})^{2}\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}\right]
$$

**6.9.** Consider the collision $1+2\to3+4$ in the lab frame (2 at rest), with particles 3 and 4 massless. Obtain the formula for the differential cross section.

$$
\left[\textit{Answer: }\frac{d\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\mechanical{\Omega}}
=\left(\frac{\energy{\hbar}}{8\pi}\right)^{2}
\frac{S\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}\rvert}
{\mechanical{m_2}\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}\rvert
(\energy{E_1}+\mechanical{m_2}\mechanical{c}^{2}
-\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}\rvert\mechanical{c}\cos\mechanical{\theta})}\right]
$$

**6.10.** (a) Analyze the problem of elastic scattering ($\mechanical{m_3}=\mechanical{m_1}$, $\mechanical{m_4}=\mechanical{m_2}$) in the lab frame (particle 2 at rest). Derive the formula for the differential cross section.

<!-- PDF page 220; printed page 212 -->

$$
\left[\textit{Answer: }\frac{d\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\mechanical{\Omega}}
=\left(\frac{\energy{\hbar}}{8\pi}\right)^{2}
\frac{\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}^{2}S\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}}
{\mechanical{m_2}\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}\rvert
\left\lvert\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}\rvert
(\energy{E_1}+\mechanical{m_2}\mechanical{c}^{2})
-\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}\rvert\energy{E_3}\cos\mechanical{\theta}\right\rvert}\right]
$$

(b) If the incident particle is massless ($\mechanical{m_1}=0$), show that the result in part (a) simplifies to

$$
\left(\frac{d\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\mechanical{\Omega}}\right)
=S\left(\frac{\energy{\hbar}\energy{E_3}}{8\pi\mechanical{m_2}\mechanical{c}\energy{E_1}}\right)^{2}
\lvert\signal{\mathcal M}\rvert^{2}.
$$
