> Massive weak bosons mediate charged leptonic interactions through a vector-minus-axial-vector vertex.

<!-- PDF page 309; printed page 301 -->

*This chapter surveys the theory of weak interactions. It relies heavily on Chapter 7, but not on Chapters 8 and 9; Section 4.6 of Chapter 4 would be useful background. I begin by stating the Feynman Rules for the coupling of leptons to $W^\pm$, and treat three classic problems in some detail: the beta decays of the muon, the neutron, and the charged pion. Next, we consider the coupling of quarks to $W^\pm$, which brings in the Cabibbo angle, the GIM mechanism, and the Kobayashi-Maskawa matrix. In Section 10.6 I state the Feynman rules for coupling quarks and leptons to the $Z^0$, and the final section (probably the most difficult in this book) shows how all electromagnetic and weak vertex factors can be derived, in the Glashow-Weinberg-Salam electroweak theory.*

The mediators of weak interactions (analogous to photons in QED and gluons in QCD) are the $W$'s $(W^+$ and $W^-)$ and the $Z^0$. Unlike the photon and gluons, which are massless, these “intermediate vector bosons” are extremely heavy—by far the heaviest elementary particles yet detected. Experimentally,

$$
\mechanical{M_W}=82\pm2\,\unit{GeV}/\mechanical{c}^2,\qquad
\mechanical{M_Z}=92\pm2\,\unit{GeV}/\mechanical{c}^2
\tag{10.1}
$$

Now a *massive* particle of spin 1 has *three* allowed polarization states $(m_s=1,0,-1)$, whereas a free *massless* particle has only *two* (if $z$ is the direction of motion, the “longitudinal” polarization $m_s=0$ does not occur). Thus for photons and gluons, we imposed both the Lorentz condition

$$
\epsilon^\mu\mechanical{p_\mu}=0
\tag{10.2}
$$

(reducing the number of independent components in $\epsilon^\mu$ from 4 to 3) and also

<!-- PDF page 310; printed page 302 -->

the Coulomb gauge $(\epsilon^0=0$, so that $\boldsymbol\epsilon\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf p}=0$, which reduces it further from 3 to 2). However, for the $W$'s and the $Z$ the Lorentz condition alone exhausts the gauge freedom, and we do *not* invoke the Coulomb gauge. Moreover, the propagator for massive spin-1 particles is no longer simply $-ig_{\mu\nu}/\mechanical{q}^2$, but rather,[^ep-10-1-massless]

$$
\frac{-i(g_{\mu\nu}-\mechanical{q_\mu}\mechanical{q_\nu}/\mechanical{M}^2\mechanical{c}^2)}
{\mechanical{q}^2-\mechanical{M}^2\mechanical{c}^2}
\qquad\text{(propagator for }W\text{ and }Z\text{)}
\tag{10.3}
$$

where $\mechanical{M}$ is $\mechanical{M_W}$ or $\mechanical{M_Z}$, as the case may be. In practice, $\mechanical{q}^2$ is ordinarily so much smaller than $(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})^2$ that we can safely use

$$
\frac{ig_{\mu\nu}}{(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})^2}
\qquad\text{(propagator for }\mechanical{q}^2\ll(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})^2\text{)}
\tag{10.4}
$$

However, when a process involves energies that are comparable to $\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c}^2$ we must, of course, revert to the exact expression.

The theory of “charged” weak interactions (mediated by the $W$'s) is simpler than that for “neutral” ones (mediated by the $Z$), so for the moment I shall concentrate on the former. In this section we consider the coupling of $W$'s to *leptons*; in the next section we'll discuss their coupling to quarks and hadrons. The fundamental leptonic vertex is

![[elementary-particles-weak-chapter10-leptonic-vertex.svg|The charged leptonic weak vertex converts a charged lepton to its associated neutrino.]]

Here an electron, muon, or tau is converted into the associated neutrino, with emission of a $W^-$ (or absorption of $W^+$). The reverse process $(\nu_l\to l^-+W^+)$ is also possible, of course, as well as the “crossed” reactions involving antileptons. The Feynman rules are the same as for QED (apart from the modifications already mentioned to accommodate the massive mediator), except for the vertex factor

$$
\frac{-ig_w}{2\sqrt2}\gamma^\mu(1-\gamma^5)
\qquad\text{(weak vertex factor)}
\tag{10.5}
$$

The various 2's are purely conventional, and $g_w=\sqrt{4\pi\alpha_w}$ is the “weak coupling constant” (analogous to $\electric{g_e}$ in QED and $g_s$ in QCD). The factor $(1-\gamma^5)$, however, is of profound importance, for $\gamma^\mu$ alone would yield a *vector* coupling (like QED or QCD), whereas $\gamma^\mu\gamma^5$ gives an *axial* vector [see eq. (7.68)]. A theory that adds

The number of degrees of freedom (that is, the number of allowed spin orientations) drops abruptly from $2s+1$ (for $\mechanical{M}\ne0$) to 2 (for $\mechanical{M}=0$). ## Example 10.1 Inverse Muon Decay

Consider the process

$$
\nu_\mu+e^-\to\mu^-+\nu_e
$$

represented (in lowest order) by the diagram

![[elementary-particles-weak-chapter10-inverse-muon-decay.svg|Example 10.1: Inverse muon decay through charged W exchange.]]

Here $\mechanical{q}=\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3}$, and for any experiment likely in the near future $\mechanical{q}^2\ll\mechanical{M_W}^2\mechanical{c}^2$, so we can safely use the simplified propagator (10.4), and the amplitude is

$$
\mathcal M=\frac{g_w^2}{8(\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c})^2}
[\signal{\bar u}(3)\gamma^\mu(1-\gamma^5)\signal{u}(1)]
[\signal{\bar u}(4)\gamma_\mu(1-\gamma^5)\signal{u}(2)]
\tag{10.6}
$$

Applying Casimir's trick (7.123), we find

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{\text{spins}}|\mathcal M|^2
&=\left(\frac{g_w^2}{8(\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c})^2}\right)^2
\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^\mu(1-\gamma^5)
(\not{\mechanical{p_1}}+\mechanical{m_e}\mechanical{c})
\gamma^\nu(1-\gamma^5)\not{\mechanical{p_3}}]\\
&\quad\times\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma_\mu(1-\gamma^5)\not{\mechanical{p_2}}
\gamma_\nu(1-\gamma^5)(\not{\mechanical{p_4}}+\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c})]
\end{aligned}
\tag{10.7}
$$

The trace theorems of Chapter 7, Section 7.7, yield

$$
8[\mechanical{p_1^\mu}\mechanical{p_3^\nu}
+\mechanical{p_1^\nu}\mechanical{p_3^\mu}
-g^{\mu\nu}(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_3})
-i\epsilon^{\mu\nu\lambda\sigma}\mechanical{p_{1\lambda}}\mechanical{p_{3\sigma}}]
\tag{10.8}
$$

for the first trace, and

$$
8[\mechanical{p_{2\mu}}\mechanical{p_{4\nu}}
+\mechanical{p_{2\nu}}\mechanical{p_{4\mu}}
-g_{\mu\nu}(\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_4})
-i\epsilon_{\mu\nu\kappa\tau}\mechanical{p_2^\kappa}\mechanical{p_4^\tau}]
\tag{10.9}
$$

for the second. It follows that[^ep-10-1-epsilon]

$$
\sum_{\text{spins}}|\mathcal M|^2
=4\left(\frac{g_w}{\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c}}\right)^4
(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2})(\mechanical{p_3}\cdot\mechanical{p_4})
\tag{10.10}
$$

Actually, we want the sum over final spins but the *average* over initial

When parity violation was first considered, a factor of the form $(1+\epsilon\gamma^5)$ was used, but experiment soon dictated that $\epsilon=-1$. (See Problem 10.1.) We call it a “$V$–$A$” (“vector minus axial vector”) coupling. Fermi's original theory of beta decay was a pure vector theory (like QED), and although others proposed scalar, pseudoscalar, tensor, or pure axial couplings, it was not until 1956 that anyone seriously contemplated *mixing* terms of different parity.

spins. The electron has two spin states, but the neutrino (as we learned in Chapter 4, Section 4.6) has only *one* (if you like, the incident neutrinos are *always* polarized, since they only come “left-handed”). So

$$
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle
=2\left(\frac{g_w}{\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c}}\right)^4
(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2})(\mechanical{p_3}\cdot\mechanical{p_4})
\tag{10.11}
$$

If we now go to the *CM* frame, and neglect the mass of the electron

$$
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle
=8\left(\frac{g_w\energy{E}}{\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c}^2}\right)^4
\left\{1-\left(\frac{\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}^2}{2\energy{E}}\right)^2\right\}
\tag{10.12}
$$

where $\energy{E}$ is the incident electron (or neutrino) energy. The differential scattering cross section [eq. (6.42)] is isotropic (all scattering angles equally likely)

$$
\frac{d\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\Omega}
=\frac12\left(\frac{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}g_w^2\energy{E}}
{4\pi(\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c}^2)^2}\right)^2
\left\{1-\left(\frac{\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}^2}{2\energy{E}}\right)^2\right\}^2
\tag{10.13}
$$

and the total cross section is

$$
\mechanical{\sigma}
=\frac1{8\pi}\left[\left(\frac{g_w}{\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c}^2}\right)^2
\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}\energy{E}\right]^2
\left\{1-\left(\frac{\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}^2}{2\energy{E}}\right)^2\right\}^2
\tag{10.14}
$$
