> [!TLDR]
> Neutron beta decay requires the electron mass, nucleon weak-charge corrections, and the Cabibbo angle.

<!-- PDF page 317; printed page 309 -->

The success of the muon decay formula (10.35) encourages us to apply the same methods to the decay of the neutron, $n\to p+e+\bar\nu_e$. Of course, the neutron and proton are composite particles, but just as the Mott and Rutherford cross sections (which treat the proton as an elementary “Dirac” particle) give a good account of low-energy electron-proton scattering, so we might hope that the diagram

![[elementary-particles-chapter10-neutron-point-vertex.svg|Neutron beta decay with the neutron–proton weak coupling treated as a point vertex.]]

(the same as for muon decay, only with $n\to p+W^-$ in place of $\mu\to\nu_\mu+W^-$) will afford a reasonable approximation to neutron beta decay. From a calculational point of view the only new feature is that 3 is now a massive particle (a proton, instead of a neutrino). As it happens (Problem 10.4) this does not change the amplitude:

$$
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle
=2\left(\frac{g_w}{\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c}}\right)^4
(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2})(\mechanical{p_3}\cdot\mechanical{p_4})
\tag{10.43}
$$

<!-- PDF page 318; printed page 310 -->

which is the same as (10.16). In the rest frame of the neutron, we find

$$
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle
=\left(\frac{g_w}{\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c}}\right)^4
\mechanical{m_n}\energy{E_2}
[(\mechanical{m_n}^2-\mechanical{m_p}^2-\mechanical{m_e}^2)\mechanical{c}^2
-2\mechanical{m_n}\energy{E_2}]
\tag{10.44}
$$

In this case the electron rest energy is a substantial fraction of the total energy released, $(\mechanical{m_n}-\mechanical{m_p}-\mechanical{m_e})\mechanical{c}^2$, so we *cannot* afford to ignore the electron mass.

The decay rate calculation proceeds as before:

$$
d\mechanical{\Gamma}
=\frac{\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle}{2\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{m_n}}
\left(\frac{\mechanical{c}\,d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}}{(2\pi)^3\,2\energy{E_2}}\right)
\left(\frac{\mechanical{c}\,d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}}{(2\pi)^3\,2\energy{E_3}}\right)
\left(\frac{\mechanical{c}\,d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}}{(2\pi)^3\,2\energy{E_4}}\right)
(2\pi)^4\delta^4(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_2}-\mechanical{p_3}-\mechanical{p_4})
\tag{10.45}
$$

where

$$
\energy{E_2}=\mechanical{c}|\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}|,\quad
\energy{E_3}=\mechanical{c}\sqrt{\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}^2+\mechanical{m_p}^2\mechanical{c}^2},\quad
\energy{E_4}=\mechanical{c}\sqrt{\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}^2+\mechanical{m_e}^2\mechanical{c}^2}
\tag{10.46}
$$

The $\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}$ integral yields

$$
d\mechanical{\Gamma}
=\frac{2\mechanical{c}^3\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle}
{(4\pi)^5\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{m_n}}
\frac{d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}\,d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}}
{\energy{E_2}\energy{E_3}\energy{E_4}}
\delta\left(\mechanical{m_n}\mechanical{c}-\frac{\energy{E_2}}{\mechanical{c}}
-\frac{\energy{E_3}}{\mechanical{c}}-\frac{\energy{E_4}}{\mechanical{c}}\right)
\tag{10.47}
$$

which is the same as equation (10.23), except that this time

$$
\energy{E_3}=\mechanical{c}
\sqrt{(\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}+\mechanical{\mathbf p_4})^2+\mechanical{m_p}^2\mechanical{c}^2}
\tag{10.48}
$$

To carry out the $\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}$ integral, we set

$$
d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}
=|\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}|^2\,d|\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}|
\sin\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\phi}
=\frac1{\mechanical{c}^3}\energy{E_2}^2\,d\energy{E_2}
\sin\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\phi}
\tag{10.49}
$$

and orient the coordinates so that the $z$ axis lies along $\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}$ (which is fixed, for purposes of the $\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}$ integral); then

$$
\energy{E_3}=\mechanical{c}
\sqrt{|\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}|^2+|\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}|^2
+2|\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}||\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}|\cos\mechanical{\theta}
+\mechanical{m_p}^2\mechanical{c}^2}
\equiv\mechanical{c}x
\tag{10.50}
$$

and

$$
\frac{\energy{E_2}\sin\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\theta}}{\energy{E_3}}
=-\frac{dx}{|\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}|}
\tag{10.51}
$$

The $\mechanical{\phi}$ and $\mechanical{\theta}$ (or rather, $x$) integrals yield

$$
d\mechanical{\Gamma}
=\frac{\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle}{(4\pi)^4\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{m_n}}
\frac{d\energy{E_2}\,d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}}{\energy{E_4}|\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}|}I
\tag{10.52}
$$

where

$$
\begin{aligned}
I&\equiv\int_{x_-}^{x_+}
\delta\left(\mechanical{m_n}\mechanical{c}-x-\frac{\energy{E_2}}{\mechanical{c}}
-\frac{\energy{E_4}}{\mechanical{c}}\right)dx\\
&=\begin{cases}
1,&\text{if }x_-<\left(\mechanical{m_n}\mechanical{c}
-\dfrac{\energy{E_2}}{\mechanical{c}}-\dfrac{\energy{E_4}}{\mechanical{c}}\right)<x_+\\
0,&\text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
\tag{10.53}
$$

and the limits [from (10.50)] are

$$
x_\pm=\sqrt{(|\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}|\pm|\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}|)^2
+\mechanical{m_p}^2\mechanical{c}^2}
\tag{10.54}
$$

As before, equation (10.53) defines the range of the $\energy{E_2}$ integral; I'll let you work out the algebra (Problem 10.5):

<!-- PDF page 319; printed page 311 -->

$$
\energy{E_\pm}
=\frac{\tfrac12(\mechanical{m_n}^2-\mechanical{m_p}^2+\mechanical{m_e}^2)\mechanical{c}^2
-\mechanical{m_n}\energy{E_4}}
{\mechanical{m_n}-\energy{E_4}/\mechanical{c}^2\mp|\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}|/\mechanical{c}}
\tag{10.55}
$$

The $\energy{E_2}$ integral is thus

$$
\int_{\energy{E_-}}^{\energy{E_+}}\energy{E_2}
[(\mechanical{m_n}^2-\mechanical{m_p}^2-\mechanical{m_e}^2)\mechanical{c}^2
-2\mechanical{m_n}\energy{E_2}]\,d\energy{E_2}
\equiv J(\energy{E_4})
\tag{10.56}
$$

and since

$$
d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}=4\pi|\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}|^2d|\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}|
=\frac{4\pi}{\mechanical{c}^2}|\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}|\energy{E_4}\,d\energy{E_4}
\tag{10.57}
$$

we conclude that

$$
\frac{d\mechanical{\Gamma}}{d\energy{E}}
=\frac1{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}^2(4\pi)^3}
\left(\frac{g_w}{\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c}}\right)^4J(\energy{E})
\tag{10.58}
$$

(since there is no further occasion for ambiguity, I'm eliminating the subscript on $\energy{E_4}$; from now on $\energy{E}$ is the electron energy).

Equation (10.58) is *exact* (use it, if you like, to rederive equation (10.35), setting $\mechanical{m_n}\to\mechanical{m_\mu}$ and $\mechanical{m_p},\mechanical{m_e}\to0$), but $J(\energy{E})$ is a rather cumbersome function. From the definition (10.56):

$$
J(\energy{E})=\frac12(\mechanical{m_n}^2-\mechanical{m_p}^2-\mechanical{m_e}^2)\mechanical{c}^2
(\energy{E_+}^2-\energy{E_-}^2)
-\frac{2\mechanical{m_n}}3(\energy{E_+}^3-\energy{E_-}^3)
\tag{10.59}
$$

where $\energy{E_\pm}$ are given by equation (10.55). It pays to approximate, at this stage, recognizing that there are four small numbers here:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\epsilon&\equiv\frac{\mechanical{m_n}-\mechanical{m_p}}{\mechanical{m_n}}=0.0014,
&\delta&\equiv\frac{\mechanical{m_e}}{\mechanical{m_n}}=0.0005,\\
\eta&\equiv\frac{\energy{E_4}}{\mechanical{m_n}\mechanical{c}^2}\quad(\delta<\eta<\epsilon),
&\phi&\equiv\frac{|\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}|}{\mechanical{m_n}\mechanical{c}}\quad(0<\phi<\eta)
\end{aligned}
\tag{10.60}
$$

(The last of these is not independent, of course: $\phi^2=\eta^2-\delta^2$.) Expanding to lowest order (Problem 10.5), we obtain

$$
J\cong4\mechanical{m_n}^4\mechanical{c}^6\eta\phi(\epsilon-\eta)^2
=\frac4{\mechanical{c}^2}\energy{E}
\sqrt{\energy{E}^2-\mechanical{m_e}^2\mechanical{c}^4}
[(\mechanical{m_n}-\mechanical{m_p})\mechanical{c}^2-\energy{E}]^2
\tag{10.61}
$$

So the distribution of electron energies is given by

$$
\frac{d\mechanical{\Gamma}}{d\energy{E}}
=\frac1{\pi^3\energy{\hbar}}
\left(\frac{g_w}{2\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c}^2}\right)^4
\energy{E}\sqrt{\energy{E}^2-\mechanical{m_e}^2\mechanical{c}^4}
[(\mechanical{m_n}-\mechanical{m_p})\mechanical{c}^2-\energy{E}]^2
\tag{10.62}
$$

The experimental results are shown in Figure 10.2. The electron energies range from $\mechanical{m_e}\mechanical{c}^2$ up to about $(\mechanical{m_n}-\mechanical{m_p})\mechanical{c}^2$ (Problem 10.6). Integrating over $\energy{E}$, we get the total decay rate (Problem 10.7):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{\Gamma}
&=\frac1{4\pi^3\energy{\hbar}}\left(\frac{g_w}{2\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c}^2}\right)^4
(\mechanical{m_e}\mechanical{c}^2)^5\\
&\quad\times\left[\frac1{15}(2a^4-9a^2-8)\sqrt{a^2-1}
+a\ln(a+\sqrt{a^2-1})\right]
\end{aligned}
\tag{10.63}
$$

<!-- PDF page 320; printed page 312 -->

![[elementary-particles-figure-10-2-neutron-spectrum.png|**Fig. 10.2** Electron energy distribution from neutron beta decay. (Solid line is the theoretical curve; dots are experimental data.) &#91;Source: C. J. Christensen et al., Phys. Rev. D5, 1628 (1972), Figure 4.&#93;]]

where

$$
a\equiv\frac{\mechanical{m_n}-\mechanical{m_p}}{\mechanical{m_e}}
\tag{10.64}
$$

Putting in the numbers, we find (Problem 10.8)

$$
\mechanical{\tau}=\frac1{\mechanical{\Gamma}}=1316\,\unit{sec}
\tag{10.65}
$$

This is in the right ball park, as they say: The experimental neutron lifetime[^ep-10-3-lifetime] is $898\pm16\,\unit{sec}$, and given that weak decays range from 15 $\unit{min}$ down to $10^{-13}\,\unit{sec}$, perhaps we should be pleased to get the right order of magnitude. But why isn't the agreement *perfect*?

The main problem is that we have treated the proton and neutron as though they were simple point particles, interacting with the $W$ in exactly the same way as leptons do. To be honest about it, we should go back to the beginning, and admit that we do not really know how the $W$ couples to composite structures, draw in a blob on the Feynman diagram (to symbolize our ignorance)

![[elementary-particles-chapter10-neutron-composite-vertex.svg|Neutron beta decay with an unknown composite nucleon weak vertex.]]

and express the amplitude in terms of various unknown “form factors,” whose

[^ep-10-3-lifetime]: The number is taken from the Particle Data Booklet. Free neutrons are hard to work with, and the “official” neutron lifetime has changed substantially over time, dropping by more than 10% in the last 15 years. Note also that nuclear physicists tend to list the *half-life* $(\mechanical{t_{1/2}}=\mechanical{\tau}\ln2)$, and beta-decay specialists often quote the “comparative half-life”—the so-called “$f$” value—which has certain kinematic and Coulombic factors removed. (For the neutron the correction factor $f$ is about 1.7.) All this is just to warn you that the numbers quoted in the literature for the neutron “lifetime” are scattered all over the map, and it pays to read the fine print and check the date.

structure is limited only by Lorentz covariance—just as we did in Chapter 8 for the proton-*photon* vertex. Only when a mature QCD can provide us with the detailed structure of the nucleons will we be in a position to perfect the neutron lifetime calculation.

And yet, the Mott formula works well for low-energy electron-proton scattering: Why does essentially the same procedure give us the right answer in electrodynamics, but not in the weak interactions? In both cases the wavelength of the “probe” $(\gamma$ or $W$, as the case may be) is much larger than the diameter of the “target” $(p$ or $n)$ (see Problem 10.9); the nucleon's internal structure is not “resolved,” and it behaves as a point particle. The *crucial* question, though, is: *What is the net coupling strength of this object?* Of course, the net *charge* of the proton is simply $\electric{e}$ (the same as for the $\mu^+$, say). It doesn't *matter* what complicated processes are going on inside—valence quarks emitting virtual gluons, gluons producing quark-antiquark pairs, these “sea” quarks recombining, and so on—because *all this frenzied activity conserves charge*. From the perspective of a long wavelength photon it just looks like a point, and the net charge of the composite nucleon is just the sum of the charges of the valence quarks. But there is no a priori reason to suppose that the same applies to the *weak* coupling; when a gluon splits into a quark-antiquark pair, the net contribution of this pair to the weak coupling may *not* be zero—who knows? To account for this, we make the following replacement in the $n\to p+W$ vertex factor:

$$
(1-\gamma^5)\to(c_V-c_A\gamma^5)
\tag{10.66}
$$

where $c_V$ is the correction to the vector “weak charge,” and $c_A$ is the correction to the axial vector “weak charge.”[^ep-10-3-formfactors] Now, the same basic process, $n\to p+e+\bar\nu_e$, occurs not only for the free neutron, but also within radioactive nuclei, so we have in principle many independent opportunities to measure $c_V$ and $c_A$.[^ep-10-3-oxygen] The experimental results are as follows:

$$
c_V=1.000\pm0.003,\qquad c_A=1.26\pm0.02
\tag{10.67}
$$

Surprisingly, the vector weak charge is *not* modified by the strong interactions within the nucleon. Presumably, like electric charge, it is “protected” by a conservation law; we call this the “Conserved Vector Current” (CVC) hypothesis. Even the axial term is not altered much; evidently, it is “almost” conserved. We call this the “Partially Conserved Axial Current” (PCAC) hypothesis.

The effect of the substitution (10.66) on the neutron lifetime is something you can calculate for yourself, if you have the stamina; to good approximation, the decay rate is increased by a factor of

$$
\tfrac14(c_V^2+3c_A^2)=1.44
\tag{10.68}
$$

and the lifetime is decreased in the same ratio:

[^ep-10-3-formfactors]: $c_V$ and $c_A$ are related to the $\mechanical{q}^2=0$ limits of the corresponding weak form factors.

[^ep-10-3-oxygen]: A particular favorite is $^{14}\mathrm O\to{}^{14}\mathrm N$, which is known (from the observed spin and parity of the initial and final states) to involve only vector coupling. It affords a direct and relatively precise measure of $c_V$.

$$
\mechanical{\tau}=\frac{1316\,\unit{sec}}{1.44}=914\,\unit{sec}
\tag{10.69}
$$

This is now within the error bars of the experimental value. Unfortunately, the agreement is deceptive, for there is yet *another* correction to be made. The underlying quark process here is $d\to u+W$ (with two spectators):

![[elementary-particles-chapter10-neutron-quark-vertex.svg|Quark-level neutron beta decay: One down quark becomes an up quark, and the other two quarks are spectators.]]

and this quark vertex carries a factor of $\cos\mechanical{\theta_C}$, where

$$
\mechanical{\theta_C}=13.1^\circ
\tag{10.70}
$$

is the Cabibbo angle. I'll have more to say about this in the next section, but the essential point for now is that our theoretical value for the neutron lifetime, corrected for nonconservation of the axial charge and modified by the Cabibbo angle, is

$$
\mechanical{\tau}=\frac{914\,\unit{sec}}{\cos^2\mechanical{\theta_C}}=963\,\unit{sec}
\tag{10.71}
$$

[^ep-10-3-corrections]: This isn't the end of the story; there is a small Coulomb correction (due to the attraction of the electron and proton in the final state); there is presumably some $\mechanical{q}^2$ dependence in the form factors even near $\mechanical{q}^2=0$; and there may yet be inaccuracies in the experimental data. In particular, the neutron lifetime is very sensitive to uncertainties in $c_A$. But we are within 6% of the experimental result, and it is time to move on.
