All the cards are now on the table;[^ep-10-7-1-bosons] it remains only to explain where the GWS parameters in Table 10.1 and equations (10.94) and (10.98) come from. Glashow's original aim was to *unify* the weak and electromagnetic interactions, to combine

them into a single theoretical system, in which they would appear not as unrelated phenomena, but rather as different manifestations of one fundamental “electroweak” interaction. This was a bold proposition, in 1961.<sup>17</sup> In the first place, there was the enormous disparity in strength between weak and electromagnetic forces. However, as Glashow and others recognized, this could be accounted for if the weak interactions were mediated by extremely massive particles. Of course, this immediately begs the second question: If it's really all one basic interaction, how come the electromagnetic mediator $(\gamma)$ is massless, when the weak mediators $(W^\pm$ and $Z^0)$ are so heavy? Glashow had no particularly good answer (“It is a stumbling block we must overlook,” he said coyly). The solution was provided by Weinberg and Salam, in 1967 (see refs. 8 and 9) in the form of the “Higgs mechanism” (Chapter 11). Finally, there is a structural difference between the electromagnetic and weak vertex factors, which at first glance would seem to preclude any possibility of unification: The former are purely vectorial $(\gamma^\mu)$, whereas the latter contain vector and axial vector parts. In particular, the $W^\pm$ coupling is “maximally” mixed $V$–$A$ in character $(\gamma^\mu(1-\gamma^5))$.

This last difficulty is overcome by the ingenious device of absorbing the matrix $(1-\gamma^5)$ into the particle spinor itself. Specifically, we define

$$
\signal{u_L}(\mechanical{p})\equiv\frac{(1-\gamma^5)}2\signal{u}(\mechanical{p})
\tag{10.119}
$$

The subscript $(L)$ stands for “left-handed,” and is supposed to make you think “helicity $-1$.” However, this is somewhat misleading, since $\signal{u_L}$ is not, in general, a helicity eigenstate. In fact, as you can easily show (see Problem 10.23)

$$
\gamma^5\signal{u}(\mechanical{p})
=\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{\mechanical{c}(\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)}
{\energy{E}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2}&0\\
0&\dfrac{\mechanical{c}(\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)}
{\energy{E}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2}
\end{pmatrix}\signal{u}(\mechanical{p})
\tag{10.120}
$$

*If the particle in question is massless*, then $\energy{E}=|\mechanical{\mathbf p}|\mechanical{c}$, and

$$
\gamma^5\signal{u}(\mechanical{p})
=(\mechanical{\hat{\mathbf p}}\cdot\boldsymbol\Sigma)\signal{u}(\mechanical{p})
\tag{10.121}
$$

where

$$
\boldsymbol\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol\sigma&0\\0&\boldsymbol\sigma\end{pmatrix}
\tag{10.122}
$$

as before. Recall [eq. (7.48)] that $\energy{\hbar}/2\,\boldsymbol\Sigma$ is the spin matrix for a Dirac particle, and hence $(\mechanical{\hat{\mathbf p}}\cdot\boldsymbol\Sigma)$ is the helicity, with eigenvalues $\pm1$. Accordingly

$$
\tfrac12(1-\gamma^5)\signal{u}(\mechanical{p})
=\begin{cases}
0,&\text{if }\signal{u}(\mechanical{p})\text{ carries helicity }+1\\
\signal{u}(\mechanical{p}),&\text{if }\signal{u}(\mechanical{p})\text{ carries helicity }-1
\end{cases}
\qquad(\text{for }\mechanical{m}=0\text{ only})
\tag{10.123}
$$

More generally, $\tfrac12(1-\gamma^5)$ functions as a “projection operator,” picking out the helicity $-1$ component of $\signal{u}(\mechanical{p})$. On the other hand, *if the particle is not massless*, it is only in the ultrarelativistic regime $(\energy{E}\gg\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2)$ that equation (10.121) holds (approximately), and hence only in this limit that $\signal{u_L}$ (as defined by equation (10.119)) carries helicity $-1$. Nevertheless, everybody calls $\signal{u_L}$ a “left-handed”

state, and I shall stick to the customary language.[^ep-10-7-1-name] Meanwhile, for an *antiparticle* equation (10.121) reads

$$
\gamma^5\signal{v}(\mechanical{p})
=-(\mechanical{\hat{\mathbf p}}\cdot\boldsymbol\Sigma)\signal{v}(\mechanical{p})
\qquad(\text{if }\mechanical{m}=0)
\tag{10.124}
$$

(see Problem 10.23), and for this reason we define

$$
\signal{v_L}(\mechanical{p})\equiv\frac{(1+\gamma^5)}2\signal{v}(\mechanical{p})
\tag{10.125}
$$

The corresponding “right-handed” spinors are

$$
\signal{u_R}(\mechanical{p})\equiv\frac{(1+\gamma^5)}2\signal{u}(\mechanical{p}),\qquad
\signal{v_R}(\mechanical{p})\equiv\frac{(1-\gamma^5)}2\signal{v}(\mechanical{p})
\tag{10.126}
$$

As for the adjoint spinors, we have

$$
\signal{\bar u_L}
=\signal{u_L^\dagger}\gamma^0
=\signal{u^\dagger}\frac{(1-\gamma^5)}2\gamma^0
=\signal{u^\dagger}\gamma^0\frac{(1+\gamma^5)}2
=\signal{\bar u}\frac{(1+\gamma^5)}2
\tag{10.127}
$$

[Recall that $\gamma^5$ is Hermitian $(\gamma^{5\dagger}=\gamma^5)$, and it anticommutes with $\gamma^\mu$ $(\gamma^\mu\gamma^5=-\gamma^5\gamma^\mu)$.] Similarly

$$
\signal{\bar v_L}=\signal{\bar v}\frac{(1-\gamma^5)}2,\quad
\signal{\bar u_R}=\signal{\bar u}\frac{(1-\gamma^5)}2,\quad
\signal{\bar v_R}=\signal{\bar v}\frac{(1+\gamma^5)}2
\tag{10.128}
$$

We call these various spinors (summarized in Table 10.2) “chiral” fermion states (from the Greek word for “hand”—same root as “chiropractor”).

I emphasize that all this is only notation and terminology; it is *useful* because it allows us to recast the weak and electromagnetic interactions in a form that facilitates their unification. Consider, to begin with, the coupling of an electron and a neutrino to the $W^-$ (as it occurs, say, in inverse beta decay, Example 10.1):

The contribution to $\mathcal M$ from this vertex is given by

$$
j_\mu^-=\signal{\bar\nu}\gamma_\mu\left(\frac{1-\gamma^5}2\right)\signal{e}
\tag{10.129}
$$

(here $\signal{e}$ and $\signal{\nu}$ stand for the particle spinors; for a while we need to keep careful track of the different particle species, and $\signal{u_e}$, $\signal{u_{\nu_e}}$, etc. just gets too cumbersome). This quantity is called the (negatively charged) weak “current”; as we shall see, it plays a role somewhat analogous to the electric current in QED. Now

$$
\left(\frac{1-\gamma^5}2\right)^2
=\frac14[1-2\gamma^5+(\gamma^5)^2]
=\left(\frac{1-\gamma^5}2\right)
\tag{10.130}
$$

and

$$
\gamma_\mu\left(\frac{1-\gamma^5}2\right)
=\left(\frac{1+\gamma^5}2\right)\gamma_\mu
\tag{10.131}
$$

so

$$
\gamma_\mu\left(\frac{1-\gamma^5}2\right)
=\left(\frac{1+\gamma^5}2\right)\gamma_\mu\left(\frac{1-\gamma^5}2\right)
\tag{10.132}
$$

This may not look like much of an improvement, but it enables us to rewrite equation (10.129) more neatly, in terms of the chiral spinors:

$$
j_\mu^-=\signal{\bar\nu_L}\gamma_\mu\signal{e_L}
\tag{10.133}
$$

The weak vertex factor is now *purely vectorial*—but it couples only *left-handed* electrons to *left-handed* neutrinos. In the latter sense it is still structurally different from the fundamental vertex in QED; however, we can play a similar game there, too. Notice that

$$
\signal{u}=\left(\frac{1-\gamma^5}2\right)\signal{u}
+\left(\frac{1+\gamma^5}2\right)\signal{u}
=\signal{u_L}+\signal{u_R}
\tag{10.134}
$$

(similarly $\signal{\bar u}=\signal{\bar u_L}+\signal{\bar u_R}$), so the electromagnetic “current” can itself be written in terms of chiral spinors:

$$
\electric{j_\mu^{em}}
=-\signal{\bar e}\gamma_\mu\signal{e}
=-(\signal{\bar e_L}+\signal{\bar e_R})\gamma_\mu(\signal{e_L}+\signal{e_R})
=-\signal{\bar e_L}\gamma_\mu\signal{e_L}-\signal{\bar e_R}\gamma_\mu\signal{e_R}
\tag{10.135}
$$

(For future purposes it is best to build in a factor of $-1$, to account for the negative charge of the electron). Observe that the “cross terms” vanish:

$$
\signal{\bar e_L}\gamma_\mu\signal{e_R}
=\signal{\bar e}\left(\frac{1+\gamma^5}2\right)\gamma_\mu
\left(\frac{1+\gamma^5}2\right)\signal{e}
=\signal{\bar e}\gamma_\mu
\left(\frac{1-\gamma^5}2\right)\left(\frac{1+\gamma^5}2\right)\signal{e}
\tag{10.136}
$$

but

$$
(1-\gamma^5)(1+\gamma^5)=1-(\gamma^5)^2=0
\tag{10.137}
$$

Equations (10.133) and (10.135) are beginning to look like the stuff of which one might build a unified theory. It is true that the weak current only couples left-handed states, whereas the electromagnetic current couples both types, but apart from that they are strikingly similar. So attractive is this formulation that physicists have come to regard left- and right-handed fermions almost as different

particles.[^ep-10-7-1-propagation] In this view, the factor $(1-\gamma^5)/2$ in the charged weak coupling characterizes the participating particles, rather than the interaction itself; the latter is vectorial in *all* cases—strong, electromagnetic, and weak alike.

[^ep-10-7-1-propagation]: There is a danger in carrying this too far. You may find yourself wondering, for example, whether the left-handed electron necessarily has the same *mass* as the right-handed electron; or, noting that no vector interaction can couple a left-handed particle to a right-handed one [see eqs. (10.136) and (10.137)], you may ask how the two “worlds” communicate at all. Both questions are based on a misunderstanding of $\signal{u_L}$ and $\signal{u_R}$. The problem is that, useful as it is in describing particle interactions, handedness is not conserved in the propagation of a free particle (unless its mass is zero). (Formally, $\gamma^5$ does not commute with the free particle Hamiltonian.) In fact, $\signal{u_L}$ and $\signal{u_R}$ do not satisfy the Dirac equation (see Problem 10.24). A particle that starts out left-handed will soon evolve a right-handed component. (By contrast, *helicity is conserved* in free-particle propagation.) Only for *massless* fermions can left- and right-handed species be considered distinct particles in the full sense of the word; and, of course, left- and right-handed *neutrinos* are distinct: as far as we know right-handed neutrinos do not exist at all.
