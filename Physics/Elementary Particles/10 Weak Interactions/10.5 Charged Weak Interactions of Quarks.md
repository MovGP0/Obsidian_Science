> [!TLDR]
> Charged weak quark interactions mix generations through the Cabibbo angle and the Kobayashi–Maskawa matrix.

<!-- PDF page 325; printed page 317 -->

In the case of leptons, the coupling to $W^\pm$ takes place strictly *within a particular generation*:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}\nu_e\\e\end{pmatrix},\quad
\begin{pmatrix}\nu_\mu\\\mu\end{pmatrix},\quad
\begin{pmatrix}\nu_\tau\\\tau\end{pmatrix}
\qquad\text{(lepton generations)}
$$

That is, $e^-\to\nu_e+W^-$, $\mu^-\to\nu_\mu+W^-$, $\tau^-\to\nu_\tau+W^-$, but there is no cross-generational coupling, of the form $e^-\to\nu_\mu+W^-$, for example. This observation, in fact, is enshrined in the laws of conservation of electron number, muon number, and tau number. The coupling of $W$ to *quarks* is not quite so simple, for although the generation structure is similar

$$
\begin{pmatrix}u\\d\end{pmatrix},\quad
\begin{pmatrix}c\\s\end{pmatrix},\quad
\begin{pmatrix}t\\b\end{pmatrix}
\qquad\text{(quark generations)}
$$

the weak interactions do not strictly respect their separate identities. There are, to be sure, interactions of the form $d\to u+W^-$ (the process that underlies neutron decay, $n\to p+e+\bar\nu_e$), but there exist as well cross-generational couplings, such as $s\to u+W^-$ (seen, for example, in the decay $\Lambda\to p+e+\bar\nu_e$). Indeed, if this were *not* the case, we would have three absolute “flavor-conservation” laws: conservation of “upness-plus-downness,” “charm-plus-strangeness,” and “truth-plus-beauty”—analogous to the three lepton number conservation laws. As a result, the lightest strange particle $(K^-)$ would be absolutely stable, and so would the $B$ meson (the lightest beautiful particle); our world would be a quite different place.

In 1963 (when $u$, $d$, and $s$ were the only quarks known) Cabibbo<sup>1</sup> suggested that the $d\to u+W^-$ vertex carries a factor of $\cos\mechanical{\theta_C}$, whereas $s\to u+W^-$ carries a factor of $\sin\mechanical{\theta_C}$; apart from that they are identical to the leptonic couplings [eq. (10.5)]:

![[elementary-particles-chapter10-cabibbo-vertices.svg|The down-to-up and strange-to-up charged weak vertices carry cosine and sine Cabibbo factors.]]

$$
\frac{-ig_w}{2\sqrt2}\gamma^\mu(1-\gamma^5)\cos\mechanical{\theta_C},
\qquad
\frac{-ig_w}{2\sqrt2}\gamma^\mu(1-\gamma^5)\sin\mechanical{\theta_C}
\tag{10.82}
$$

<!-- PDF page 326; printed page 318 -->

The strangeness-changing process $(s\to u+W^-)$ is conspicuously weaker than the strangeness-conserving one $(d\to u+W^-)$, so evidently the “Cabibbo angle” $\mechanical{\theta_C}$ is rather small. Experimentally

$$
\mechanical{\theta_C}=13.1^\circ
\tag{10.83}
$$

The weak interactions *almost* respect quark generations . . . but not *quite*.

## Example 10.2 Leptonic Decays

Consider the decay $K^-\to l^-+\bar\nu_l$, where $l$ is an electron or a muon. This is the analog to $\pi^-$ decay (Sect. 10.4), but now the quark vertex is $s+\bar u\to W^-$, instead of $d+\bar u\to W^-$. From equation (10.80) we have

$$
\mechanical{\Gamma}
=\frac{f_K^2}{\pi\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{m_K}^3}
\left(\frac{g_w}{4\mechanical{M_W}}\right)^4
\mechanical{m_l}^2(\mechanical{m_K}^2-\mechanical{m_l}^2)^2
$$

The coupling strength is presumably the same as before, except that where $f_\pi$ contained a factor of $\cos\mechanical{\theta_C}$, $f_K$ carries a factor of $\sin\mechanical{\theta_C}$. Accordingly,

$$
\frac{\mechanical{\Gamma}(K^-\to l^-+\bar\nu_l)}
{\mechanical{\Gamma}(\pi^-\to l^-+\bar\nu_l)}
=\tan^2\mechanical{\theta_C}
\left(\frac{\mechanical{m_\pi}}{\mechanical{m_K}}\right)^3
\left(\frac{\mechanical{m_K}^2-\mechanical{m_l}^2}
{\mechanical{m_\pi}^2-\mechanical{m_l}^2}\right)^2
\tag{10.84}
$$

Putting in the appropriate numbers, we get 0.96 for the muon mode $(l=\mu)$ and 0.19 for the electron mode $(l=e)$. [The experimental ratios are 1.34 and 0.26, respectively, corresponding to a Cabibbo angle of $15.4^\circ$. These decays are pure axial-vector, and as we discovered earlier—see eq. (10.67)—perfect agreement is not to be expected.]

For obvious reasons, processes of the kind considered in Example 10.2 are called *leptonic* decays. There also exist *semileptonic* decays, such as $\pi^-\to\pi^0+e^-+\bar\nu_e$, $\bar K^0\to\pi^++\mu^-+\bar\nu_\mu$ (Fig. 10.3a), or for that matter the beta decay of the neutron: $n\to p^++e^-+\bar\nu_e$. Finally, there are *nonleptonic* weak interactions, such as $K^-\to\pi^0+\pi^-$ or $\Lambda\to p^++\pi^-$ (Fig. 10.3b). Generally speaking, the latter are the hardest to analyze, because there is strong interaction contamination at *both* ends of the $W$ line. We shall not consider nonleptonic weak processes in this book.<sup>2</sup>

## Example 10.3 Semileptonic Decays

In the case of neutron decay $(n\to p+e+\bar\nu_e)$ the basic quark process is $d\to u+W^-$ (with two spectators). However, there are *two* $d$ quarks in the neutron, and *either* one could couple to the $W$; the net amplitude for the process is the *sum*. The simplest way to keep track of the numbers is to use the quark wave functions of Chapter 5, Section 5.9; the flavor states $\signal{\psi_{12}}$, for instance, give $n=(ud-du)d/\sqrt2$, from which (with $d\to u$) we get $[(uu-uu)d+(ud-du)u]/\sqrt2=(ud-du)u/\sqrt2=p$. The overall coefficient is then simply $\cos\mechanical{\theta_C}$ (as I claimed at the end of Section 10.3).

![[elementary-particles-figure-10-3a-semileptonic-decay.svg|**Fig. 10.3a** A typical semileptonic decay $(\bar K^0\to\pi^++\mu^-+\bar\nu_\mu)$.]]

![[elementary-particles-figure-10-3b-nonleptonic-decay.svg|**Fig. 10.3b** A typical nonleptonic weak decay $(\Lambda\to p^++\pi^-)$.]]

By contrast, in the decay $\Sigma^0\to\Sigma^++e+\bar\nu_e$, the quark process is still $d\to u$, but here $\Sigma^0=[(us-su)d+(ds-sd)u]/2\to[(us-su)u+(us-su)u]/2=(us-su)u=\sqrt2\Sigma^+$, and hence the amplitude carries a factor of $\sqrt2\cos\mechanical{\theta_C}$.[^ep-10-5-spin] The decay rate is given by equation (10.63), which reduces (in the case $a\gg1$) to the form

$$
\mechanical{\Gamma}
=\frac1{30\pi^3\energy{\hbar}}
\left(\frac{g_w}{2\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c}^2}\right)^4
(\mechanical{\Delta m}\mechanical{c}^2)^5X^2
$$

where $\mechanical{\Delta m}$ is the baryon mass decrease and $X$ is the Cabibbo factor $(\cos\mechanical{\theta_C}$, for neutron decay; $\sqrt2\cos\mechanical{\theta_C}$, for $\Sigma^0\to\Sigma^++e+\bar\nu_e$; etc.). I'll let you work out the numbers for yourself (Problem 10.13).[^ep-10-5-pcac]

Cabibbo's theory was very successful in correlating dozens of decay rates, but there remained a disturbing problem: this picture allowed the $K^0$ to decay into a $\mu^+\mu^-$ pair (see Fig. 10.4). The amplitude should be proportional to $\sin\mechanical{\theta_C}\cos\mechanical{\theta_C}$, but the calculated rate is far greater than the experimental limits allow. A solution to this paradox was proposed in 1970 by Glashow, Iliopoulos, and Maiani (GIM).<sup>3</sup> They introduced a fourth quark $(c)$—remember, this was four years before the “November Revolution” produced the first direct experimental evidence for charm—whose couplings to $s$ and $d$ carry factors of $\cos\mechanical{\theta_C}$ and $-\sin\mechanical{\theta_C}$, respectively:

![[elementary-particles-chapter10-gim-vertices.svg|Charm weak vertices carry minus-sine and cosine Cabibbo factors for down and strange quarks.]]

$$
\frac{-ig_w}{2\sqrt2}\gamma^\mu(1-\gamma^5)(-\sin\mechanical{\theta_C}),
\qquad
\frac{-ig_w}{2\sqrt2}\gamma^\mu(1-\gamma^5)\cos\mechanical{\theta_C}
\tag{10.85}
$$

[^ep-10-5-spin]: Actually, there is a technical difference here: The active quark is bound to the spectator in a *spin singlet* state. Fortunately, this does not affect the lifetime.

[^ep-10-5-pcac]: This procedure includes only the valence quarks, and hence is insensitive to the nonconservation of the axial coupling. As we found in equation (10.68), PCAC can lead to a correction of nearly 50%, so one does not expect fine precision in the lifetimes. Cabibbo's theory included a way of calculating the axial couplings, but I shall not go into that here.

In the “GIM mechanism,” the diagram in Fig. 10.4 is canceled by the corresponding diagram with $c$ in place of $u$ (Fig. 10.5), for this time the amplitude is proportional to $-\sin\mechanical{\theta_C}\cos\mechanical{\theta_C}$.[^ep-10-5-cancellation]

The Cabibbo–GIM scheme invites a simple interpretation: Instead of the physical quarks $d$ and $s$, the “correct” states to use in the weak interactions are $d'$ and $s'$, given by

$$
d'=d\cos\mechanical{\theta_C}+s\sin\mechanical{\theta_C},\qquad
s'=-d\sin\mechanical{\theta_C}+s\cos\mechanical{\theta_C}
\tag{10.86}
$$

or, in matrix form

$$
\begin{pmatrix}d'\\s'\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\mechanical{\theta_C}&\sin\mechanical{\theta_C}\\
-\sin\mechanical{\theta_C}&\cos\mechanical{\theta_C}
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}d\\s\end{pmatrix}
\tag{10.87}
$$

The $W$'s couple to the “Cabibbo-rotated” states

$$
\begin{pmatrix}u\\d'\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\begin{pmatrix}c\\s'\end{pmatrix}
$$

in exactly the same way that they couple to lepton pairs, $\begin{pmatrix}\nu_e\\e\end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix}\nu_\mu\\\mu\end{pmatrix}$; their couplings to the *physical* particles (states of specific flavor) are then given by

$$
\begin{pmatrix}u\\d'\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}u\\d\cos\mechanical{\theta_C}+s\sin\mechanical{\theta_C}\end{pmatrix},
\quad
\begin{pmatrix}c\\s'\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}c\\-d\sin\mechanical{\theta_C}+s\cos\mechanical{\theta_C}\end{pmatrix}
$$

That is, $d\to u+W^-$ carries a factor $\cos\mechanical{\theta_C}$, $s\to u+W^-$ a factor $\sin\mechanical{\theta_C}$, and so on.[^ep-10-5-rotation]

[^ep-10-5-cancellation]: The cancellation is not *perfect*, because the mass of the $c$ is not the same as the mass of the $u$. However, these virtual particles are so far off the mass shell that both propagators are essentially just $i\not{\mechanical{q}}/\mechanical{q}^2$. (In calculating $\mathcal M$ we shall be integrating over the one remaining internal momentum which is not fixed by the conservation laws. This is essentially the momentum “circulating around the loop.” Because of the two $W$ propagators, the main contribution will come in the region of the $W$ mass, which is so much greater than the $c$ or $u$ mass that the latter can, to good approximation, be neglected. Actually, the decay *does* occur, it's just extremely slow, and if you include the effects of $u/c$ mass difference, the calculation is consistent with the observed rate.)

[^ep-10-5-rotation]: It is purely conventional that we “rotate” $d$ and $s$, rather than $u$ and $c$; we could accomplish the same purpose by introducing $u'=u\cos\mechanical{\theta_C}-c\sin\mechanical{\theta_C}$ and $c'=u\sin\mechanical{\theta_C}+c\cos\mechanical{\theta_C}$. Incidentally, you might ask why a similar rotation does not occur in the *lepton* sector. The answer is, it already *has*—or rather, we'd never notice it if it did. The point is that all neutrinos are massless, and any linear combination of them is *still* massless. So there is no “tag” to identify the “unrotated” states, and what we call $\nu_e$, for example, is “the neutrino paired with the $e$ in weak interactions,” just as $d'$ is “the quark paired with $u$ in weak interactions.”

At the time, the GIM mechanism seemed a little extravagant—introducing a new quark just to fix a rather esoteric technical defect in a largely untested theory. But the skeptics were silenced by the discovery of the $\psi(c\bar c)$ in 1974. Meanwhile, Kobayashi and Maskawa<sup>4</sup> had generalized the Cabibbo–GIM scheme to handle *three* generations of quarks.[^ep-10-5-generations] The “weak interaction generations,”

$$
\begin{pmatrix}u\\d'\end{pmatrix},\quad
\begin{pmatrix}c\\s'\end{pmatrix},\quad
\begin{pmatrix}t\\b'\end{pmatrix}
\qquad\text{(weak interaction quark generations)}
\tag{10.88}
$$

are related to the physical quark states by the *Kobayashi–Maskawa matrix*:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}d'\\s'\\b'\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}
U_{ud}&U_{us}&U_{ub}\\
U_{cd}&U_{cs}&U_{cb}\\
U_{td}&U_{ts}&U_{tb}
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}d\\s\\b\end{pmatrix}
\tag{10.89}
$$

where $U_{ud}$, for example, specifies the coupling of $u$ to $d(d\to u+W^-)$. There are nine entries in the KM matrix, but they are not all independent (see Problem 10.14). $U$ can be reduced to a kind of “canonical form,” in which there remain just three “generalized Cabibbo angles,” $(\mechanical{\theta_1},\mechanical{\theta_2},\mechanical{\theta_3})$ and one phase factor $(\mechanical{\delta})$:<sup>5</sup>

$$
U=\begin{pmatrix}
c_1&s_1c_3&s_1s_3\\
-s_1c_2&c_1c_2c_3-s_2s_3e^{i\mechanical{\delta}}&c_1c_2s_3+s_2c_3e^{i\mechanical{\delta}}\\
-s_1s_2&c_1s_2c_3+c_2s_3e^{i\mechanical{\delta}}&c_1s_2s_3-c_2c_3e^{i\mechanical{\delta}}
\end{pmatrix}
\tag{10.90}
$$

Here $c_i$ stands for $\cos\mechanical{\theta_i}$, and $s_i$ for $\sin\mechanical{\theta_i}$. If $\mechanical{\theta_2}=\mechanical{\theta_3}=0$, the third generation does not mix with the other two, and we recover the original Cabibbo–GIM picture, with $\mechanical{\theta_1}=\mechanical{\theta_C}$. However, there is compelling evidence (namely, the observed

The Standard Model offers no insight into the KM matrix (indeed, this is one of the most conspicuous weaknesses of the Standard Model); for the moment, we simply take the values of the matrix elements from experiment. There is a small industry devoted to the accurate measurement of these parameters. So far, only their *magnitudes* are known with any precision.<sup>6</sup>

$$
|U_{ij}|=\begin{pmatrix}

The third generation mixing (the off-diagonal elements in the third row and column) turns out to be very small indeed, as we learned from the surprisingly long lifetime of the $B$ meson $(10^{-12}\,\unit{sec})$.
