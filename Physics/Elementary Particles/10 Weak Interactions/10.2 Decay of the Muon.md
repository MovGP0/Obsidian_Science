> [!TLDR]
> The muon decay spectrum and lifetime determine the Fermi coupling constant and the weak coupling strength.

<!-- PDF page 312; printed page 304 -->

Electron-neutrino scattering is not the easiest thing in the world to study experimentally, but the closely related process, muon decay $(\mu\to e+\nu_\mu+\bar\nu_e)$, is the cleanest of all weak interaction phenomena, theoretically and experimentally. The Feynman diagram

![[elementary-particles-weak-chapter10-muon-decay.svg|Muon decay: The muon emits a muon neutrino and a W, which produces an electron and an electron antineutrino.]]

leads to the amplitude

$$
\mathcal M=\frac{g_w^2}{8(\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c})^2}
[\signal{\bar u}(3)\gamma^\mu(1-\gamma^5)\signal{u}(1)]
[\signal{\bar u}(4)\gamma_\mu(1-\gamma^5)\signal{v}(2)]
\tag{10.15}
$$

from which we obtain, as before,

$$
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle
=2\left(\frac{g_w}{\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c}}\right)^4
(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2})(\mechanical{p_3}\cdot\mechanical{p_4})
\tag{10.16}
$$

In the muon rest frame, $\mechanical{p_1}=(\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c},\mathbf0)$, we have

<!-- PDF page 313; printed page 305 -->

$$
\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2}=\mechanical{m_\mu}\energy{E_2}
\tag{10.17}
$$

and since $\mechanical{p_1}=\mechanical{p_2}+\mechanical{p_3}+\mechanical{p_4}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\mechanical{p_3}+\mechanical{p_4})^2
&=\mechanical{p_3}^2+\mechanical{p_4}^2+2\mechanical{p_3}\cdot\mechanical{p_4}
=\mechanical{m_e}^2\mechanical{c}^2+2\mechanical{p_3}\cdot\mechanical{p_4}\\
&=(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_2})^2
=\mechanical{p_1}^2+\mechanical{p_2}^2-2\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2}
=\mechanical{m_\mu}^2\mechanical{c}^2-2\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2}
\end{aligned}
\tag{10.18}
$$

from which it follows that

$$
\mechanical{p_3}\cdot\mechanical{p_4}
=\frac{(\mechanical{m_\mu}^2-\mechanical{m_e}^2)\mechanical{c}^2}{2}
-\mechanical{m_\mu}\energy{E_2}
\tag{10.19}
$$

The algebra will be simpler later on, at no significant cost in accuracy, if we set $\mechanical{m_e}=0$, so that

$$
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle
=\left(\frac{g_w}{\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c}}\right)^4
\mechanical{m_\mu}^2\energy{E_2}(\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}^2-2\energy{E_2})
\tag{10.20}
$$

Now, the decay rate is given by the Golden Rule (6.15):[^ep-10-2-threebody]

$$
d\mechanical{\Gamma}
=\frac{\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle}{2\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{m_\mu}}
\left(\frac{\mechanical{c}\,d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}}{(2\pi)^3\,2\energy{E_2}}\right)
\left(\frac{\mechanical{c}\,d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}}{(2\pi)^3\,2\energy{E_3}}\right)
\left(\frac{\mechanical{c}\,d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}}{(2\pi)^3\,2\energy{E_4}}\right)
(2\pi)^4\delta^4(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_2}-\mechanical{p_3}-\mechanical{p_4})
\tag{10.21}
$$

where $\energy{E_2}=|\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}|\mechanical{c}$, $\energy{E_3}=|\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}|\mechanical{c}$, and $\energy{E_4}=|\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}|\mechanical{c}$. To begin with, we peel apart the delta function:

$$
\delta^4(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_2}-\mechanical{p_3}-\mechanical{p_4})
=\delta\left(\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}
-\frac{\energy{E_2}}{\mechanical{c}}-\frac{\energy{E_3}}{\mechanical{c}}
-\frac{\energy{E_4}}{\mechanical{c}}\right)
\delta^3(\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}+\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}+\mechanical{\mathbf p_4})
\tag{10.22}
$$

and perform the $\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}$ integral:

$$
d\mechanical{\Gamma}
=\frac{\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle\mechanical{c}^3}
{16(2\pi)^5\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{m_\mu}}
\frac{(d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_2})(d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_4})}
{\energy{E_2}\energy{E_3}\energy{E_4}}
\delta\left(\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}-\frac{\energy{E_2}}{\mechanical{c}}
-\frac{\energy{E_3}}{\mechanical{c}}-\frac{\energy{E_4}}{\mechanical{c}}\right)
\tag{10.23}
$$

where $\energy{E_3}$ now stands for $|\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}+\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}|\mechanical{c}$. Next we'll do the $\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}$ integral. Setting the polar axis along $\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}$ (which is *fixed*, for the purposes of the $\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}$ integration), we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left(\frac{\energy{E_3}}{\mechanical{c}}\right)^2
&=|\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}+\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}|^2
=\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}^2+\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}^2
+2\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}\\
&=\frac1{\mechanical{c}^2}(\energy{E_2}^2+\energy{E_4}^2
+2\energy{E_2}\energy{E_4}\cos\mechanical{\theta})
\end{aligned}
\tag{10.24}
$$

and

$$
d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}
=\left(\frac{\energy{E_2}}{\mechanical{c}}\right)^2
\frac{d\energy{E_2}}{\mechanical{c}}\sin\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\phi}
\tag{10.25}
$$

The $\mechanical{\phi}$ integral is trivial $(\int d\mechanical{\phi}=2\pi)$; to carry out the $\mechanical{\theta}$ integration, let

$$
x\equiv\frac1{\mechanical{c}}
\sqrt{\energy{E_2}^2+\energy{E_4}^2+2\energy{E_2}\energy{E_4}\cos\mechanical{\theta}}
=\frac{\energy{E_3}}{\mechanical{c}}
\tag{10.26}
$$

[^ep-10-2-threebody]: Note that this is a *three* body decay, so we have to go all the way back to the Golden Rule.

<!-- PDF page 314; printed page 306 -->

so that

$$
dx=-\frac{\energy{E_2}\energy{E_4}\sin\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\theta}}
{\mechanical{c}\energy{E_3}}
\tag{10.27}
$$

Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\int_0^\pi\frac{\sin\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\theta}}{\energy{E_3}}
\delta\left(\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}-\frac{\energy{E_2}}{\mechanical{c}}
-\frac{\energy{E_3}}{\mechanical{c}}-\frac{\energy{E_4}}{\mechanical{c}}\right)\\
&\quad=\frac{\mechanical{c}}{\energy{E_2}\energy{E_4}}
\int_{x_-}^{x_+}\delta\left(\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}
-x-\frac{\energy{E_2}}{\mechanical{c}}-\frac{\energy{E_4}}{\mechanical{c}}\right)dx\\
&\quad=\begin{cases}
\dfrac{\mechanical{c}}{\energy{E_2}\energy{E_4}},&
\text{if }x_-<\left(\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}
-\dfrac{\energy{E_2}}{\mechanical{c}}-\dfrac{\energy{E_4}}{\mechanical{c}}\right)<x_+\\
0,&\text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
\tag{10.28}
$$

where

$$
x_\pm\equiv\frac1{\mechanical{c}}
\sqrt{\energy{E_2}^2+\energy{E_4}^2\pm2\energy{E_2}\energy{E_4}}
=\frac1{\mechanical{c}}|\energy{E_2}\pm\energy{E_4}|
\tag{10.29}
$$

The inequality in equation (10.28) can be expressed more neatly:

$$
|\energy{E_2}-\energy{E_4}|
<(\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}^2-\energy{E_2}-\energy{E_4})
<\energy{E_2}+\energy{E_4}
\tag{10.30}
$$

or, adding $(\energy{E_2}+\energy{E_4})$ and dividing through by 2:

$$
\frac12\{|\energy{E_2}-\energy{E_4}|+\energy{E_2}+\energy{E_4}\}
<\frac12\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}^2
<(\energy{E_2}+\energy{E_4})
\tag{10.31}
$$

The term on the left is simply the larger of $\energy{E_2}$ and $\energy{E_4}$; the other one is necessarily even smaller, so expression (10.31) is equivalent to *three* inequalities:

$$
\left\{\begin{aligned}
\energy{E_2}&<\tfrac12\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}^2\\
\energy{E_4}&<\tfrac12\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}^2\\
(\energy{E_2}+\energy{E_4})&>\tfrac12\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}^2
\end{aligned}\right\}
\tag{10.32}
$$

[These constraints make good sense kinematically: Particle 2, for example, gets the maximum possible energy when 3 and 4 emerge diametrically opposite to it:

![[elementary-particles-weak-chapter10-muon-decay-energy-limit.svg|Maximum-energy configuration: Particle 2 moves opposite to particles 3 and 4.]]

In this case 2 picks up half the available energy $(\tfrac12\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}^2)$, while 3 and 4 share the other half. If there is a nonzero angle between 3 and 4, 2 gets less, and 3 plus 4 get correspondingly more. Thus $\tfrac12\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}^2$ is the maximum energy for any *individual* outgoing particle, and the *minimum* total for any *pair*.]

The inequalities (10.32) specify the limits on the $\energy{E_2}$ and $\energy{E_4}$ integrals: $\energy{E_2}$ runs from $\tfrac12\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}^2-\energy{E_4}$ up to $\tfrac12\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}^2$, and $\energy{E_4}$ will go from 0 to $\tfrac12\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}^2$. The $\mechanical{\theta}$ and $\mechanical{\phi}$ integrals leave us with

$$
d\mechanical{\Gamma}
=\frac{\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle\mechanical{c}}{(4\pi)^4\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{m_\mu}}
\,d\energy{E_2}\,\frac{d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}}{\energy{E_4}^2}
\tag{10.33}
$$

Putting in equation (10.20) and carrying out the $\energy{E_2}$ integral, we have

<!-- PDF page 315; printed page 307 -->

$$
\begin{aligned}
d\mechanical{\Gamma}
&=\left(\frac{g_w}{4\pi\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c}}\right)^4
\frac{\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}}{\energy{\hbar}}
\frac{d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}}{\energy{E_4}^2}
\int_{\tfrac12\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}^2-\energy{E_4}}^{\tfrac12\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}^2}
\energy{E_2}(\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}^2-2\energy{E_2})\,d\energy{E_2}\\
&=\left(\frac{g_w}{4\pi\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c}}\right)^4
\frac{\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}}{\energy{\hbar}}
\left(\frac{\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}^2}{2}-\frac23\energy{E_4}\right)
d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}
\end{aligned}
\tag{10.34}
$$

Finally, writing

$$
d^3\mechanical{\mathbf p_4}=4\pi\left(\frac{\energy{E_4}}{\mechanical{c}}\right)^2
\frac{d\energy{E_4}}{\mechanical{c}}
$$

and dropping the subscript $(\energy{E}\equiv\energy{E_4}$ is the electron energy), we obtain

$$
\frac{d\mechanical{\Gamma}}{d\energy{E}}
=\left(\frac{g_w}{\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c}}\right)^4
\frac{\mechanical{m_\mu}^2\energy{E}^2}{2\energy{\hbar}(4\pi)^3}
\left(1-\frac{4\energy{E}}{3\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}^2}\right)
\tag{10.35}
$$

This tells us the energy distribution of the electrons emitted in muon decay. It fits the experimental spectrum perfectly (Fig. 10.1). The total decay rate is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{\Gamma}
&=\left(\frac{g_w}{\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c}}\right)^4
\frac{\mechanical{m_\mu}^2}{2\energy{\hbar}(4\pi)^3}
\int_0^{\tfrac12\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}^2}
\energy{E}^2\left(1-\frac{4\energy{E}}{3\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}^2}\right)d\energy{E}\\
&=\left(\frac{\mechanical{m_\mu}g_w}{\mechanical{M_W}}\right)^4
\frac{\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}^2}{12\energy{\hbar}(8\pi)^3}
\end{aligned}
\tag{10.36}
$$

and hence the lifetime of the muon is

$$
\mechanical{\tau}=\frac1{\mechanical{\Gamma}}
=\left(\frac{\mechanical{M_W}}{\mechanical{m_\mu}g_w}\right)^4
\frac{12\energy{\hbar}(8\pi)^3}{\mechanical{m_\mu}\mechanical{c}^2}
\tag{10.37}
$$

Notice that $g_w$ and $\mechanical{M_W}$ do not appear *separately*, either in the muon lifetime formula or in the electron-neutrino scattering cross section; only their *ratio* occurs. It is traditional, in fact, to express weak interaction formulas in terms of the “Fermi coupling constant”

$$
G_F\equiv\frac{\sqrt2}{8}
\left(\frac{g_w}{\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c}^2}\right)^2
(\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c})^3
\tag{10.38}
$$

Thus the muon lifetime is written

$$
\mechanical{\tau}=\frac{192\pi^3\energy{\hbar}^7}
{G^2\mechanical{m_\mu}^5\mechanical{c}^4}
\tag{10.39}
$$

In Fermi's original theory of beta decay (1933) there was no $W$; the interaction was supposed to be a direct four-particle coupling, represented in the Feynman language by a diagram of the form

![[elementary-particles-weak-chapter10-fermi-contact.svg|Fermi's original theory uses one four-particle contact vertex.]]

<!-- PDF page 316; printed page 308 -->

![[elementary-particles-figure-10-1-muon-spectrum.png|**Fig. 10.1** Experimental spectrum of positrons in $\mu^+\to e^++\nu_e+\bar\nu_\mu$. The solid line is the theoretically predicted spectrum, based on equation (10.35), corrected for electromagnetic effects. (Source: M. Bardon et al., Phys. Rev. Lett. 14, 449 (1965).)]]

From the modern perspective, Fermi's theory combined the $W$ propagator with the two vertex factors, in the diagram

![[elementary-particles-weak-chapter10-fermi-mediator.svg|The W propagator and two weak vertices form an effective four-particle interaction at low energy.]]

to make an effective four-particle coupling constant $G_F$. It worked, but only because the $W$ is so heavy that expression (10.4) is a good approximation to the true propagator (10.3),[^ep-10-2-fermi] and in fact it was recognized already in the fifties that Fermi's theory could not be valid at high energies. The idea of a weak mediator (analogous to the photon) was suggested by O. Klein as far back as 1938.

[^ep-10-2-fermi]: Fermi also thought the coupling was pure vector, as I mentioned in the footnote (*) on p. 303. Despite these defects (for which Fermi could scarcely be blamed; after all, he invented the theory at a time when the neutrino was a wild speculation and the Dirac equation itself was quite new) Fermi's theory was astonishingly prescient, and all subsequent developments have been relatively small adjustments to it.

<!-- PDF page 317; printed page 309 -->

If we put in the observed muon lifetime and mass, we find that

$$
G_F/(\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c})^3
=\frac{\sqrt2}{8}\left(\frac{g_w}{\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c}^2}\right)^2
=1.166\times10^{-5}/\unit{GeV}^2
\tag{10.40}
$$

The corresponding value of $g_w$ (less accurately known, at present, because of the experimental uncertainty in $\mechanical{M_W}$) is

$$
g_w=0.66
\tag{10.41}
$$

and hence the “weak fine structure constant” is

$$
\alpha_w=\frac{g_w^2}{4\pi}=\frac1{29}
\tag{10.42}
$$

This number should come as something of a shock: it is *larger* than the electromagnetic fine structure constant $(\electric{\alpha}=\frac1{137})$, by a factor of nearly five! Weak interactions are feeble *not* because the *intrinsic* coupling is small (it *isn't*), but because the *mediators* are so *massive*—or, more precisely, because we typically work at energies so far below the $W$ mass that the denominator in the propagator $(\mechanical{q}^2-\mechanical{M_W}^2\mechanical{c}^2)$ is extremely large. New machines are presently under construction that will run at energies close to $\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c}^2$, and in this regime the “weak” interactions will far surpass the electromagnetic ones in strength.
