In 1958, Bludman<sup>7</sup> suggested that there might exist *neutral* weak interactions, mediated by an uncharged partner of the $W$'s—the $Z^0$:

![[elementary-particles-chapter10-neutral-fermion-vertex.svg|The neutral weak vertex couples a fermion to the Z boson.]]

Here $f$ stands for any lepton or any quark. Notice, however, that the *same* Fermion comes out as went in (just as in QED and QCD). We do not allow couplings of the form $\mu^-\to e^-+Z^0$, for example (this would violate conservation of muon and electron number), nor of the form $s\to d+Z^0$ (such a strangeness-changing neutral process would lead to $K^0\to\mu^++\mu^-$, which, as I have already remarked, is strongly suppressed).[^ep-10-6-basis] In 1961, Glashow<sup>8</sup> published the first paper on unification of weak and electromagnetic interactions; his theory required the existence

    ![[elementary-particles-chapter10-neutral-rotated-vertices.svg|Neutral vertices for the Cabibbo-rotated down and strange states.]]
    
    The $d'$ diagram gives
    
    $$
    \mathcal M\sim\bar d'd'
    =\bar dd\cos^2\mechanical{\theta_C}+\bar ss\sin^2\mechanical{\theta_C}
    +(\bar ds+\bar sd)\sin\mechanical{\theta_C}\cos\mechanical{\theta_C}
    $$
    
    The $s'$ diagram gives
    
    $$
    \mathcal M\sim\bar s's'
    =\bar dd\sin^2\mechanical{\theta_C}+\bar ss\cos^2\mechanical{\theta_C}
    -(\bar ds+\bar sd)\sin\mechanical{\theta_C}\cos\mechanical{\theta_C}
    $$
    
    So the sum of the two is $\mathcal M_{\text{tot}}\sim\bar d'd'+\bar s's'=\bar dd+\bar ss$. Thus the *net* amplitude, once both diagrams are combined, is the same whichever states we use. (The same argument generalizes to three generations, as long as the KM matrix is unitary.)

In 1967, Weinberg and Salam<sup>9</sup> formulated Glashow's model as a “spontaneously broken gauge theory,” and in 1971, 't Hooft<sup>10</sup> demonstrated that the Glashow–Weinberg–Salam scheme is renormalizable. Thus there were increasingly persuasive theoretical reasons for thinking that neutral weak interactions occur in nature, but for a long time there were no *experimental* data to support this hope. Finally, in 1973,<sup>11</sup> a bubble chamber photograph at CERN (Fig. 10.6) revealed the first convincing evidence for the reaction

$$
\bar\nu_\mu+e\to\bar\nu_\mu+e
$$

suggesting mediation by the $Z^0$.

The same series of experiments also witnessed the corresponding neutrino-*quark* process, in the form of inclusive neutrino-nucleon scattering:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bar\nu_\mu+N&\to\bar\nu_\mu+X\\
\nu_\mu+N&\to\nu_\mu+X
\end{aligned}
$$

Their cross sections were about a third as large as those of the related *charged* events $(\bar\nu_\mu+N\to\mu^++X$ and $\nu_\mu+N\to\mu^-+X)$, indicating that this was indeed a new kind of weak interaction, and not simply a higher-order process

(which would yield a far smaller cross section). The CERN results came as welcome encouragement to electroweak theorists, who had been out on a limb now for several years. Meanwhile a series of deep inelastic neutrino scattering experiments was conducted (also at CERN) that confirmed not only the basic structure of charged and neutral weak interactions, but also the quark-parton model itself—which carries over directly from *electron*-nucleon scattering, (an electromagnetic process) to *neutrino*-nucleon scattering (a weak process).<sup>12</sup>

As we have seen, the coupling of quarks and leptons to $W^\pm$ is a universal “$V$–$A$” form; the vertex factor is always

$$
\frac{-ig_w}{2\sqrt2}\gamma^\mu(1-\gamma^5)
\qquad(W^\pm\text{ vertex factor})
\tag{10.92}
$$

(It is true that the axial coupling to *composite* structures, such as the proton, is modified, but that is a result of strong interaction contamination—the underlying quark process is pure $V$–$A$). The coupling of the $Z^0$ is not so simple:

$$
\frac{-ig_z}{2}\gamma^\mu(c_V^f-c_A^f\gamma^5)
\qquad(Z^0\text{ vertex factor})
\tag{10.93}
$$

where $g_z$ is the neutral coupling constant, and the coefficients $c_V^f$ and $c_A^f$ depend on the particular quark or lepton $(f)$ involved. In the GWS model, *all* these numbers are determined by a single fundamental parameter $\mechanical{\theta_w}$, called the “weak mixing angle” (or “Weinberg angle”). See Table 10.1. Moreover, the weak coupling constants are related to the basic unit of *electric* charge:

$$
g_w=\frac{\electric{g_e}}{\sin\mechanical{\theta_w}},\qquad
g_z=\frac{\electric{g_e}}{\sin\mechanical{\theta_w}\cos\mechanical{\theta_w}}
\tag{10.94}
$$

where $\electric{g_e}$ is the electromagnetic coupling constant (in appropriate units, the charge

$$
\mechanical{\theta_w}=28.7^\circ
\qquad(\sin^2\mechanical{\theta_w}=0.23)
\tag{10.95}

Derivation of $\mechanical{\theta_w}$ stands as a major challenge for any theory going beyond the Standard Model. Meanwhile, the $Z^0$ propagator is [eq. (10.3)]

$$
\frac{-i(g_{\mu\nu}-\mechanical{q_\mu}\mechanical{q_\nu}/\mechanical{M_Z}^2\mechanical{c}^2)}
{\mechanical{q}^2-\mechanical{M_Z}^2\mechanical{c}^2}
\tag{10.96}
$$

In the typical case $\mechanical{q}^2\ll\mechanical{M_Z}^2\mechanical{c}^2$, it reduces to

$$
\frac{ig_{\mu\nu}}{(\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c})^2}
\tag{10.97}
$$

Finally, the $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$ masses are related by

$$
\mechanical{M_W}=\mechanical{M_Z}\cos\mechanical{\theta_w}
\tag{10.98}
$$

Equations (10.93)–(10.98) are the basic predictions of the GWS model. Given the weak mixing angle, we can now *calculate* the $W$ and $Z$ masses (see Problem 10.17). Their discovery by Rubbia at CERN in 1983, at $\mechanical{M_W}=82\,\unit{GeV}/\mechanical{c}^2$ and $\mechanical{M_Z}=92\,\unit{GeV}/\mechanical{c}^2$ (as predicted) was persuasive evidence for the GWS model.<sup>13</sup>

## Example 10.4 Elastic Neutrino-Electron Scattering

In Example 10.1 we calculated the cross section for the $W$-mediated process $\nu_\mu+e\to\nu_e+\mu$. We now consider the related $Z^0$-mediated reaction $\nu_\mu+e\to\nu_\mu+e$:

![[elementary-particles-chapter10-neutral-neutrino-electron.svg|Example 10.4: Elastic neutrino–electron scattering through a neutral Z boson.]]

The amplitude is

$$
\mathcal M=\frac{g_z^2}{8(\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c})^2}
[\signal{\bar u}(3)\gamma^\mu(1-\gamma^5)\signal{u}(1)]
[\signal{\bar u}(4)\gamma_\mu(c_V-c_A\gamma^5)\signal{u}(2)]
\tag{10.99}
$$

and hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle
&=2\left(\frac{g_z}{4\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c}}\right)^4
\operatorname{Tr}\{\gamma^\mu(1-\gamma^5)\not{\mechanical{p_1}}
\gamma^\nu(1-\gamma^5)\not{\mechanical{p_3}}\}\\
&\quad\times\operatorname{Tr}\{\gamma_\mu(c_V-c_A\gamma^5)
(\not{\mechanical{p_2}}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})
\gamma_\nu(c_V-c_A\gamma^5)(\not{\mechanical{p_4}}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})\}\\
&=\frac12\left(\frac{g_z}{\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c}}\right)^4
\{(c_V+c_A)^2(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2})(\mechanical{p_3}\cdot\mechanical{p_4})\\
&\qquad+(c_V-c_A)^2(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_4})(\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_3})
-(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})^2(c_V^2-c_A^2)(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_3})\}
\end{aligned}
\tag{10.100}
$$

<!-- PDF page 334; printed page 326 -->

![[elementary-particles-figure-10-7-neutrino-cm.svg|**Fig. 10.7** Elastic neutrino-electron scattering in the CM.]]

where $\mechanical{m}$ is the mass of the electron, and $c_V$ and $c_A$ are the neutral weak couplings for the electron. If we now go to the CM frame, and ignore the electron mass (i.e., set $\mechanical{m}\to0$), we find

$$
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle
=2\left(\frac{g_z\energy{E}}{\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c}^2}\right)^4
\left[(c_V+c_A)^2+(c_V-c_A)^2\cos^4\frac{\mechanical{\theta}}2\right]
\tag{10.101}
$$

where $\energy{E}$ is the electron (or neutrino) energy, and $\mechanical{\theta}$ is the scattering angle (Fig. 10.7). The differential scattering cross section [eq. (6.42)] is

$$
\frac{d\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\Omega}
=2\left(\frac{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}\pi\right)^2
\left(\frac{g_z}{4\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c}^2}\right)^4
\energy{E}^2
\left[(c_V+c_A)^2+(c_V-c_A)^2\cos^4\frac{\mechanical{\theta}}2\right]
\tag{10.102}
$$

and the total cross section (integrating over all angles) is

$$
\mechanical{\sigma}
=\frac2{3\pi}(\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c})^2
\left(\frac{g_z}{2\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c}^2}\right)^4
\energy{E}^2(c_V^2+c_A^2+c_Vc_A)
\tag{10.103}
$$

Putting in the GWS values for $c_V$ and $c_A$ (from Table 10.1), and comparing the result of Example 10.1 [eq. (10.14)], we find that for energies substantially above the muon mass

$$
\frac{\mechanical{\sigma}(\nu_\mu+e^-\to\nu_\mu+e^-)}
{\mechanical{\sigma}(\nu_\mu+e^-\to\nu_e+\mu^-)}
=\frac14-\sin^2\mechanical{\theta_w}
+\frac43\sin^4\mechanical{\theta_w}=0.09
\tag{10.104}
$$

The current experimental value<sup>14</sup> is 0.08, which, given the 10% uncertainties in the measurements, as well as the (somewhat smaller) uncertainty in $\mechanical{\theta_w}$, is excellent agreement.

You might well ask why it took so long for neutral weak interactions to be detected in the laboratory; after all, 15 years separate Bludman's original speculations from the definitive experiments at CERN. The reason is that *most* neutral processes are “masked” by competing electromagnetic ones. For example, $e^++e^-\to\mu^++\mu^-$ can occur either by a virtual $Z^0$ or by a virtual $\gamma$ (Fig. 10.8); at low energies the photon mechanism overwhelmingly dominates.[^ep-10-6-parity] That's why

[^ep-10-6-parity]: It is interesting to note, however, that there is a weak contamination in *every* electromagnetic process, since the $Z^0$ couples to everything the $\gamma$ does (and then some). For example, the Coulomb potential binding the electrons to the nucleus in an atom is slightly modified by $Z^0$ exchange, and this is observable in atomic spectra. Similarly, there is a weak contribution to electron-proton scattering. Although these effects are minute, they carry a tell-tale signature: parity violation. (See ref. 15.)

![[elementary-particles-figure-10-8-z-photon-annihilation.svg|**Fig. 10.8** Weak and electromagnetic contributions to $e^++e^-\to\mu^++\mu^-$.]]

*neutrino* scattering was originally used to confirm the existence of neutral weak interactions; neutrinos have no electromagnetic coupling, so the weak effects are not obscured. But neutrino experiments are notoriously difficult, hence the long delay. An alternative would be to work at extremely high energies—specifically, in the neighborhood of the $Z^0$ mass, where the denominator of the $Z^0$ propagator is small, and the “weak” interaction is correspondingly large. In the early days it was hard to estimate $\mechanical{\theta_w}$, and hence the $Z^0$ mass was quite uncertain. But by the late seventies a variety of experimental data pointed to $\mechanical{\theta_w}\approx29^\circ$, and hence to $\mechanical{M_Z}=90\,\unit{GeV}/\mechanical{c}^2$ (see Problem 10.17). This prediction was stunningly confirmed in 1983,<sup>13</sup> and inspired a major effort to build electron-positron colliders that would operate at the $Z^0$ peak. [Two such facilities, the Stanford Linear Collider (SLC) at SLAC, and LEP at CERN, are presently under construction, and scheduled to begin operation in 1987 and 1989, respectively.]

## Example 10.5 Electron-Positron Scattering Near the Z⁰ Pole

Consider the process $e^++e^-\to f+\bar f$ (Fig. 10.9), where $f$ is any quark or lepton.[^ep-10-6-electron] This time we shall *not* use the approximate form of the $Z^0$ propagator [eq. (10.97)], for we are interested precisely in the regime $\mechanical{q}^2\approx(\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c})^2$. The amplitude is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal M
&=-\frac{g_z^2}{4(\mechanical{q}^2-(\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c})^2)}
[\signal{\bar u}(4)\gamma^\mu(c_V^f-c_A^f\gamma^5)\signal{v}(3)]\\
&\quad\times\left(g_{\mu\nu}-\frac{\mechanical{q_\mu}\mechanical{q_\nu}}{(\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c})^2}\right)
[\signal{\bar v}(2)\gamma^\nu(c_V^e-c_A^e\gamma^5)\signal{u}(1)]
\end{aligned}
\tag{10.105}
$$

where $\mechanical{q}=\mechanical{p_1}+\mechanical{p_2}=\mechanical{p_3}+\mechanical{p_4}$. Since we are working in the vicinity of 90 $\unit{GeV}$, we can afford to ignore the lepton and quark masses.[^ep-10-6-top] In this case the second term in the propagator contributes nothing, for $\mechanical{q_\mu}$ contracts with $\gamma^\mu$ to give

\signal{\bar u}(4)\not{\mechanical{q}}(c_V-c_A\gamma^5)\signal{v}(3)
$$

$$
\not{\mechanical{p_3}}(c_V-c_A\gamma^5)\signal{v}(3)
=(c_V+c_A\gamma^5)\not{\mechanical{p_3}}\signal{v}(3)=0
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal M
&=-\frac{g_z^2}{4[\mechanical{q}^2-(\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c})^2]}
[\signal{\bar u}(4)\gamma^\mu(c_V^f-c_A^f\gamma^5)\signal{v}(3)]\\
&\quad\times[\signal{\bar v}(2)\gamma_\mu(c_V^e-c_A^e\gamma^5)\signal{u}(1)]
\end{aligned}
\tag{10.106}
$$

and it follows that

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle
&=\left[\frac{g_z^2}{8(\mechanical{q}^2-(\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c})^2)}\right]^2
\operatorname{Tr}\{\gamma^\mu(c_V^f-c_A^f\gamma^5)\not{\mechanical{p_3}}
\gamma^\nu(c_V^f-c_A^f\gamma^5)\not{\mechanical{p_4}}\}\\
&\quad\times\operatorname{Tr}\{\gamma_\mu(c_V^e-c_A^e\gamma^5)\not{\mechanical{p_1}}
\gamma_\nu(c_V^e-c_A^e\gamma^5)\not{\mechanical{p_2}}\}
\end{aligned}
\tag{10.107}
$$

Now, the first trace is

$$
(c_V^2+c_A^2)[\mechanical{p_3^\mu}\mechanical{p_4^\nu}
+\mechanical{p_3^\nu}\mechanical{p_4^\mu}
-g^{\mu\nu}(\mechanical{p_3}\cdot\mechanical{p_4})]
-2ic_Vc_A\epsilon^{\mu\nu\lambda\sigma}\mechanical{p_{3\lambda}}\mechanical{p_{4\sigma}}
\tag{10.108}
$$

and there is the corresponding expression for the second trace, so we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle
&=\frac12\left[\frac{g_z^2}{\mechanical{q}^2-(\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c})^2}\right]^2
\{[(c_V^f)^2+(c_A^f)^2][(c_V^e)^2+(c_A^e)^2]\\
&\qquad\times[(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_3})(\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_4})
+(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_4})(\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_3})]\\
&\qquad+4c_V^fc_A^fc_V^ec_A^e

-(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_4})(\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_3})]\}
\end{aligned}
\tag{10.109}
$$

In the CM frame this reduces to

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle
&=\left[\frac{g_z^2\energy{E}^2}{(2\energy{E})^2-(\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c}^2)^2}\right]^2
\{[(c_V^f)^2+(c_A^f)^2][(c_V^e)^2+(c_A^e)^2](1+\cos^2\mechanical{\theta})\\
&\qquad-8c_V^fc_A^fc_V^ec_A^e\cos\mechanical{\theta}\}
\end{aligned}
\tag{10.110}
$$

where $\energy{E}$ is the energy of each particle and $\mechanical{\theta}$ is the scattering angle. The differential scattering cross section (6.42) is therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\Omega}
&=\left(\frac{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}g_z^2\energy{E}}
{16\pi[(2\energy{E})^2-(\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c}^2)^2]}\right)^2
\{[(c_V^f)^2+(c_A^f)^2][(c_V^e)^2+(c_A^e)^2](1+\cos^2\mechanical{\theta})\\
&\qquad-8c_V^fc_A^fc_V^ec_A^e\cos\mechanical{\theta}\}
\end{aligned}
\tag{10.111}
$$

and the total cross section is

$$
\mechanical{\sigma}
=\frac1{3\pi}
\left(\frac{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}g_z^2\energy{E}}
{4[(2\energy{E})^2-(\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c}^2)^2]}\right)^2
[(c_V^f)^2+(c_A^f)^2][(c_V^e)^2+(c_A^e)^2]
\tag{10.112}
$$

As it stands, $\mechanical{\sigma}$ blows up at the $Z^0$ pole—that is, when the total energy $(2\energy{E})$ hits the value $\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c}^2$ (just right to put the $Z^0$ on its mass shell). The problem is that we have treated the $Z^0$ as a *stable* particle, which it is not. It has a finite lifetime $\mechanical{\tau_Z}$ (not yet accurately measured), which has the effect of “smearing out” its mass. We can account for this by modifying the propagator<sup>16</sup>

$$
\frac1{\mechanical{q}^2-(\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c})^2}
\to\frac1{\mechanical{q}^2-(\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c})^2
+i\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{\Gamma_Z}}
\tag{10.113}
$$

where $\mechanical{\Gamma_Z}$ is the decay rate $(\mechanical{\Gamma_Z}=1/\mechanical{\tau_Z})$. With this adjustment, the cross section becomes

$$
\mechanical{\sigma}
=\frac{(\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}g_z^2\energy{E})^2}{48\pi}
\frac{[(c_V^f)^2+(c_A^f)^2][(c_V^e)^2+(c_A^e)^2]}
{[(2\energy{E})^2-(\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c}^2)^2]^2
+(\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c}^2\mechanical{\Gamma_Z})^2}
\tag{10.114}
$$

Because $\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{\Gamma_Z}\ll\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c}^2$, the correction for finite $Z^0$ lifetime is negligible except in the immediate vicinity of the $Z^0$ pole, where it has the effect of softening the infinite spike.

In Chapter 8 we calculated the cross section for the same process when mediated by a *photon* [eq. (8.6)]:

$$
\mechanical{\sigma}
=\frac{(\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}\electric{g_e}^2)^2}{48\pi}
\frac{(\electric{Q^f})^2}{\energy{E}^2}
\tag{10.115}
$$

(where $\electric{Q^f}$ is the charge of $f$, in units of $\electric{e}$). Thus the ratio of weak to electromagnetic rates in (for example) muon production, is

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\frac{\mechanical{\sigma}(e^+e^-\to Z^0\to\mu^+\mu^-)}
{\mechanical{\sigma}(e^+e^-\to\gamma\to\mu^+\mu^-)}
=\left\{\frac{(\tfrac12-2\sin^2\mechanical{\theta_w}
+4\sin^4\mechanical{\theta_w})^2}
{(\sin\mechanical{\theta_w}\cos\mechanical{\theta_w})^4}\right\}\\
&\qquad\times\frac{\energy{E}^4}
{[(2\energy{E})^2-(\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c}^2)^2]^2
+(\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{\Gamma_Z}\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c}^2)^2}
\end{aligned}
\tag{10.116}
$$

The factor in curly brackets is approximately 2, if we use the current value of the weak mixing angle (10.95). Substantially below the $Z^0$ pole $(2\energy{E}\ll\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c}^2)$, then,

$$
\frac{\mechanical{\sigma_Z}}{\mechanical{\sigma_\gamma}}
\cong2\left(\frac{\energy{E}}{\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c}^2}\right)^4
\tag{10.117}
$$

and the electromagnetic route dominates (at $2\energy{E}=\tfrac12\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c}^2$, for instance, the weak contribution is less than 1%). But right *on* the $Z^0$ pole $(2\energy{E}=\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c}^2)$

$$
\frac{\mechanical{\sigma_Z}}{\mechanical{\sigma_\gamma}}
\cong\frac18\left(\frac{\mechanical{M_Z}\mechanical{c}^2}{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{\Gamma_Z}}\right)
\tag{10.118}
$$
