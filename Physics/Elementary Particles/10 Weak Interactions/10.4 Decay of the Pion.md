> [!TLDR]
> The pion decay form factor cancels from the branching ratio, while the weak vertex suppresses the electron mode.

<!-- PDF page 322; printed page 314 -->

According to the quark model, the decay of a charged pion $(\pi^-\to l^-+\bar\nu_l$, where $l$ is a muon or an electron) is really a *scattering* event in which the incident quarks happen to be bound together:

![[elementary-particles-chapter10-pion-quark-annihilation.svg|A charged pion's constituent quarks annihilate through a W boson to produce a charged lepton and an antineutrino.]]

<!-- PDF page 323; printed page 315 -->

In this sense it is a weak-interaction analog to positronium decay $(e^++e^-\to\gamma+\gamma)$ or $\eta_c$ decay $(c+\bar c\to g+g)$—electromagnetic and strong processes, respectively. We *could* analyze it this way, following the methods of Example 7.8 and Chapter 9, Section 9.3 (see Problem 10.10), but in the end we would be stuck with a factor of $|\signal{\psi}(0)|^2$, and at this stage we have no idea what the wave function $(\signal{\psi})$ of the quarks within a pion looks like. Given that such a calculation will carry this undetermined multiplicative factor *anyway*, it is simpler to proceed as follows.

Redraw the Feynman diagram, with a blob to represent the coupling of $\pi^-$ to $W^-$:

![[elementary-particles-chapter10-pion-composite-vertex.svg|An unknown pion–W form factor replaces the constituent-quark vertex.]]

We may not know how the $W$ couples to the pion, but we *do* know how it couples to the leptons, so the amplitude must have the general form

$$
\mathcal M=\frac{g_w^2}{8(\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c})^2}
[\signal{\bar u}(3)\gamma_\mu(1-\gamma^5)\signal{v}(2)]F^\mu
\tag{10.72}
$$

where $F^\mu$ is a “form factor” describing the $\pi\to W$ blob. It has to be a four-vector, to contract with the $\gamma_\mu$ in the lepton factor. But the pion has spin zero; the only vector associated with it, out of which we might construct $F^\mu$, is its momentum, $\mechanical{p^\mu}$.[^ep-10-4-formfactor] (I won't bother with a subscript on the pion's momentum: $\mechanical{p}\equiv\mechanical{p_1}$.) So $F^\mu$ must be some scalar quantity times $\mechanical{p^\mu}$:

$$
F^\mu=f_\pi\mechanical{p^\mu}
\tag{10.73}
$$

(In principle, $f_\pi$ is a function of $\mechanical{p}^2$—the only available scalar—but since the pion is on its mass shell, $\mechanical{p}^2=\mechanical{m_\pi}^2\mechanical{c}^2$ and hence $f_\pi$ is simply a fixed number. We call it the “pion decay constant.”)[^ep-10-4-wavefunction]

[^ep-10-4-formfactor]: Notice that we introduce the (weak) pion form factor at the level of $\mathcal M$, whereas for the (electromagnetic) proton form factors and structure functions we waited until the $\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle$ stage. The reason is that the proton has a spin, and we would have to include that in the roster of available vectors; it is only after we have *averaged* over the spins that the list reduces to two, and the problem becomes manageable. The pion, however, has *no* spin, so we can afford to introduce the form factor directly in $\mathcal M$, where it is only a *vector* quantity, instead of a tensor.

[^ep-10-4-wavefunction]: The pion decay constant evidently contains the factor of $|\signal{\psi}(0)|^2$ alluded to earlier; we have simply wrapped our ignorance in a more convenient package. (See Problem 10.10.)

<!-- PDF page 324; printed page 316 -->

Summing over the outgoing spins, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle
&=\left[\frac{f_\pi}{8}\left(\frac{g_w}{\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c}}\right)^2\right]^2
\mechanical{p_\mu}\mechanical{p_\nu}
\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^\mu(1-\gamma^5)\not{\mechanical{p_2}}
\gamma^\nu(1-\gamma^5)(\not{\mechanical{p_3}}+\mechanical{m_l}\mechanical{c})]\\
&=\frac18\left[f_\pi\left(\frac{g_w}{\mechanical{M_W}\mechanical{c}}\right)^2\right]^2
[2(\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{p_2})(\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{p_3})
-\mechanical{p}^2(\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_3})]
\end{aligned}
\tag{10.74}
$$

[the trace was already calculated in equation (10.9)]. But $\mechanical{p}=\mechanical{p_2}+\mechanical{p_3}$, so

$$
\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{p_2}=\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_3},\qquad
\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{p_3}
=\mechanical{m_l}^2\mechanical{c}^2+\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_3}
\tag{10.75}
$$

and

$$
\mechanical{p}^2=\mechanical{p_2}^2+\mechanical{p_3}^2+2\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_3},
\quad\text{so that }\quad
2\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_3}=(\mechanical{m_\pi}^2-\mechanical{m_l}^2)\mechanical{c}^2
\tag{10.76}
$$

Thus

$$
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle
=\left(\frac{g_w}{2\mechanical{M_W}}\right)^4
f_\pi^2\mechanical{m_l}^2(\mechanical{m_\pi}^2-\mechanical{m_l}^2)
\tag{10.77}
$$

(a *constant*).

The decay rate is given by the standard formula (6.32):

$$
\mechanical{\Gamma}
=\frac{|\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}|}{8\pi\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{m_\pi}^2\mechanical{c}}
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle
\tag{10.78}
$$

and the outgoing momentum is [see eq. (6.31) or Problem 3.16]

$$
|\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}|=\frac{\mechanical{c}}{2\mechanical{m_\pi}}
(\mechanical{m_\pi}^2-\mechanical{m_l}^2)
\tag{10.79}
$$

Thus

$$
\mechanical{\Gamma}=\frac{f_\pi^2}{\pi\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{m_\pi}^3}
\left(\frac{g_w}{4\mechanical{M_W}}\right)^4
\mechanical{m_l}^2(\mechanical{m_\pi}^2-\mechanical{m_l}^2)^2
\tag{10.80}
$$

Of course, without knowing the decay constant, $f_\pi$, we cannot calculate the pion lifetime.[^ep-10-4-ansatz] Nevertheless, we are able to determine the branching ratio

$$
\frac{\mechanical{\Gamma}(\pi^-\to e^-+\bar\nu_e)}
{\mechanical{\Gamma}(\pi^-\to\mu^-+\bar\nu_\mu)}
=\frac{\mechanical{m_e}^2(\mechanical{m_\pi}^2-\mechanical{m_e}^2)^2}
{\mechanical{m_\mu}^2(\mechanical{m_\pi}^2-\mechanical{m_\mu}^2)^2}
=1.28\times10^{-4}
\tag{10.81}
$$

The experimental number is $1.23\pm0.02\times10^{-4}$. At first glance this is a very surprising result, for it predicts (correctly) that the pion prefers the muon mode, in spite of the fact that the electron is much lighter. Phase space considerations favor decays for which the mass decrease is as large as possible, and unless some conservation law intervenes, we ordinarily find that the lightest final state is the most common one. Pion decay is the notorious exception, and it calls for some special dynamical explanation. A clue is suggested by equation (10.80): Notice that if the electron were *massless*, the $\pi^-\to e^-+\bar\nu_e$ mode would be forbidden *completely*. Can we understand this limiting case? Yes. The pion has spin 0, so the electron and the antineutrino must emerge with opposite spins, and hence equal helicities:

[^ep-10-4-ansatz]: It is a rather striking fact that if you put in $f_\pi=\mechanical{m_\pi}\mechanical{c}$ (or, better yet, $\mechanical{m_\pi}\mechanical{c}\cos\mechanical{\theta_C}$) you come out very close to the $\pi^-\to\mu^-+\bar\nu_\mu$ lifetime, but I know of no persuasive theoretical justification for this ansatz.

<!-- PDF page 325; printed page 317 -->

![[elementary-particles-chapter10-pion-helicity.svg|The electron and antineutrino move in opposite directions with opposite spins and equal helicities.]]

The antineutrino is always right-handed, so the electron must be right-handed as well. But if the electron were truly massless, then (like the neutrino) it would only exist as a left-handed particle. More precisely, the $1-\gamma^5$ in the weak vertex factor would couple only to left-handed electrons, just as it couples only to left-handed neutrinos (see Problem 10.11). That's why if the electron were massless, the decay $\pi^-\to e^-+\bar\nu_e$ could not occur *at all*, and why (the physical electron being very *close* to massless) the decay is so heavily suppressed.
