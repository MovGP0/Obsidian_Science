**3.1.** Solve equation (3.1) for $\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z}$, in terms of $\mechanical{x'},\mechanical{y'},\mechanical{z'},\mechanical{t'}$, and check that you recover equation (3.3).

**3.2.** (a) Derive equation (3.4).

(b) According to clocks on the ground (system $S$), streetlights $A$ and $B$ (situated $4\,\unit{\mathrm{km}}$ apart) were both turned on at precisely 8:00 P.M. Which one went on first according to an observer on a train (system $S'$), which moves from $A$ toward $B$ at $\tfrac{3}{5}$ the speed of light? How much later (in seconds) did the other light go on?

<!-- PDF page 108; printed page 100 -->

> [!note]
> As always in relativity, we are talking here about what $S'$ *observed*, *after* correcting for the time it took the light to reach her, not what she actually *saw* (which would depend on where she was located on the train).

**3.3.** (a) How do *volumes* transform? Specifically, if a container has volume $\mechanical{V'}$ in its own rest frame, $S'$, what is its volume as measured by an observer in $S$, with respect to which it is moving at speed $\mechanical{v}$?

(b) How do *densities* transform? (If a container holds $\mechanical{\rho'}$ molecules per unit volume in its own rest frame, $S'$, how many molecules per unit volume does it carry in $S$?)

**3.4.** Cosmic ray muons are produced high in the atmosphere (at $8000\,\unit{\mathrm{m}}$, say) and travel toward the earth at very nearly the speed of light ($0.998\mechanical{c}$, say).

(a) Given the lifetime of the muon ($2.2\times10^{-6}\,\unit{\mathrm{sec}}$), how far would it go before disintegrating, according to prerelativistic physics? Would the muons make it to ground level?

(b) Now answer the same question using *relativistic* physics. (Because of time dilation, the muons last longer, so they travel farther.)

(c) Now analyze the same process from the perspective of the *muon*. (In its reference frame it only lasts $2.2\times10^{-6}\,\unit{\mathrm{sec}}$; how, then, does it make it to ground?)

(d) Pions are also produced in the upper atmosphere. [In fact, the sequence is proton (from outer space) hits proton (in atmosphere) $\to p+n+$ pions. The pions then decay into muons: $\pi^-\to\mu^-+\bar\nu_\mu$; $\pi^+\to\mu^++\nu_\mu$.] But the lifetime of the pion is much shorter, a hundredth that of the muon. Should the pions reach ground level? (Assume that the pions also have a speed of $0.998\mechanical{c}$.)

(a) According to prerelativistic physics?

(b) According to relativity?

**3.8.** A second-rank tensor is called *symmetric* if it is unchanged when you switch the indices ($s^{\nu\mu}=s^{\mu\nu}$); it is called *antisymmetric* if it changes sign ($a^{\nu\mu}=-a^{\mu\nu}$).

(a) How many independent elements are there in a symmetric tensor? (Since $s^{12}=s^{21}$, these would count as only *one* independent element.)

(b) How many independent elements are there in an antisymmetric tensor?

(c) If $s^{\mu\nu}$ is symmetric, show that $s_{\mu\nu}$ is also symmetric. If $a^{\mu\nu}$ is antisymmetric, show that $a_{\mu\nu}$ is antisymmetric.

(d) If $s^{\mu\nu}$ is symmetric and $a^{\mu\nu}$ is antisymmetric, show that $s^{\mu\nu}a_{\mu\nu}=0$.

(e) Show that any second-rank tensor ($t^{\mu\nu}$) can be written as the sum of an antisymmetric part ($a^{\mu\nu}$) and a symmetric part ($s^{\mu\nu}$): $t^{\mu\nu}=a^{\mu\nu}+s^{\mu\nu}$. Construct $s^{\mu\nu}$ and $a^{\mu\nu}$ explicitly, given $t^{\mu\nu}$.

**3.10.** Consider a collision in which particle $A$ (with mass $\mechanical{m_A}$ and proper velocity $\mechanical{\boldsymbol\eta_A}$) hits particle $B$ (mass $\mechanical{m_B}$, proper velocity $\mechanical{\boldsymbol\eta_B}$), producing particle $C(\mechanical{m_C},\mechanical{\boldsymbol\eta_C})$ and particle $D(\mechanical{m_D},\mechanical{\boldsymbol\eta_D})$. Suppose that (relativistic) energy and momentum are conserved in system $S$ (i.e., $\mechanical{p_A^\mu}+\mechanical{p_B^\mu}=\mechanical{p_C^\mu}+\mechanical{p_D^\mu}$). Using the Lorentz transformations (3.7), show that (relativistic) energy and momentum are *also* conserved in $S'$. (Do *not* assume that mass is conserved—in general, it is *not*: $\mechanical{m_A}+\mechanical{m_B}\ne\mechanical{m_C}+\mechanical{m_D}$.)

**3.11.** Is $\mechanical{p^\mu}$ timelike, spacelike, or lightlike, for a (real) particle of mass $\mechanical{m}$? How about a massless particle? How about a virtual particle?

**3.12.** How much more does a hot potato weigh than a cold one (in $\unit{\mathrm{kg}}$)?

**3.13.** A pion traveling at speed $\mechanical{v}$ decays into a muon and a neutrino, $\pi^-\to\mu^-+\bar\nu_\mu$. If the neutrino emerges at $90^\circ$ to the original pion direction, at what angle does the $\mu$ come off? [*Answer:* $\tan\mechanical{\theta}=(1-\mechanical{m_\mu}^{2}/\mechanical{m_\pi}^{2})/(2\mechanical{\beta}\mechanical{\gamma}^{2})$]

**3.14.** Particle $A$ (energy $\energy{E}$) hits particle $B$ (at rest), producing particles $C_1,C_2,\ldots$: $A+B\to C_1+C_2+\cdots+C_n$. Calculate the threshold (i.e., minimum $\energy{E}$) for this reaction, in terms of the various particle masses.

[*Answer:*

$$
\energy{E}=\frac{\mechanical{M}^{2}-\mechanical{m_A}^{2}-\mechanical{m_B}^{2}}{2\mechanical{m_B}}\mechanical{c}^{2},
\qquad\text{where }\mechanical{M}=\mechanical{m_1}+\mechanical{m_2}+\cdots+\mechanical{m_n}
$$

]

**3.15.** Use the result of Problem 3.14 to find the threshold energies for the following reactions, assuming the target proton is stationary:

(a) $p+p\to p+p+\pi^0$

(b) $p+p\to p+p+\pi^++\pi^-$

(c) $\pi^-+p\to p+\bar p+n$

(d) $\pi^-+p\to K^0+\Sigma^0$

(e) $p+p\to p+\Sigma^++K^0$

**3.16.** Particle $A$, at rest, decays into particles $B$ and $C$ ($A\to B+C$).

(a) Find the energy of the outgoing particles, in terms of the various masses.

[*Answer:*

$$
\energy{E_B}=\frac{\mechanical{m_A}^{2}+\mechanical{m_B}^{2}-\mechanical{m_C}^{2}}{2\mechanical{m_A}}\mechanical{c}^{2}
$$

]

(b) Find the magnitudes of the outgoing momenta.

[*Answer:*

$$
\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_B}\rvert=\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_C}\rvert
=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda(\mechanical{m_A}^{2},\mechanical{m_B}^{2},\mechanical{m_C}^{2})}}{2\mechanical{m_A}}\mechanical{c},
$$

where $\lambda$ is the so-called *triangle function*:

$$
\lambda(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2-2xy-2xz-2yz.
$$

]

(c) Note that $\lambda$ factors: $\lambda(a^2,b^2,c^2)=(a+b+c)(a+b-c)(a-b+c)(a-b-c)$. Thus $\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_B}\rvert$ goes to zero when $\mechanical{m_A}=\mechanical{m_B}+\mechanical{m_C}$, and runs imaginary if $\mechanical{m_A}<(\mechanical{m_B}+\mechanical{m_C})$. Explain.

**3.18.** (a) A pion at rest decays into a muon and a neutrino ($\pi^-\to\mu^-+\bar\nu_\mu$). On the average, how far will the muon travel (in vacuum) before disintegrating? [*Answer:* $\mechanical{d}=[(\mechanical{m_\pi}^{2}-\mechanical{m_\mu}^{2})/(2\mechanical{m_\pi}\mechanical{m_\mu})]\mechanical{c}\mechanical{\tau}=186\,\unit{\mathrm{m}}$.]

**3.19.** Particle $A$, at rest, decays into three or more particles: $A\to B+C+D+\cdots$.

(a) Determine the maximum and minimum energies that $B$ can have in such a decay, in terms of the various masses.

(b) Find the maximum and minimum electron energies in muon decay, $\mu^-\to e^-+\bar\nu_e+\nu_\mu$.

**3.20.** (a) A particle traveling at speed $\mechanical{v}$ approaches an identical particle at rest. What is the speed of each particle in the CM frame? (Classically, of course, it would just be $\mechanical{v}/2$. Why *isn't* this true relativistically?)

(b) Use your result in part (a) to compute the kinetic energy of each particle in the CM frame, and thus rederive equation (3.53).

**3.21.** In reactions of the type $A+B\to A+C_1+C_2+\cdots$ (in which particle $A$ scatters off particle $B$, producing $C_1,C_2,\ldots$), there is another inertial frame [besides the lab ($B$ at rest) and the CM ($\mechanical{\mathbf p_{\mathrm{TOT}}}=0$)] which is sometimes useful. It is called the *Breit*, or “brick wall,” frame, and it is the system in which $A$ recoils with its momentum reversed ($\mechanical{\mathbf p_{\mathrm{after}}}=-\mechanical{\mathbf p_{\mathrm{before}}}$), as though it had bounced off a brick wall. Take the case of elastic scattering ($A+B\to A+B$); if particle $A$ carries energy $\energy{E}$, and scatters at an angle $\mechanical{\theta}$, in the CM, what is its energy in the Breit frame? Find the velocity of the Breit frame (magnitude and direction) relative to the CM.

**3.22.** In a two-body scattering event, $A+B\to C+D$, it is convenient to introduce the *Mandelstam variables*

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{s}&\equiv(\mechanical{p_A}+\mechanical{p_B})^2/\mechanical{c}^{2}\\
\mechanical{t}&\equiv(\mechanical{p_A}-\mechanical{p_C})^2/\mechanical{c}^{2}\\
\mechanical{u}&\equiv(\mechanical{p_A}-\mechanical{p_D})^2/\mechanical{c}^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

(a) Show that $\mechanical{s}+\mechanical{t}+\mechanical{u}=\mechanical{m_A}^{2}+\mechanical{m_B}^{2}+\mechanical{m_C}^{2}+\mechanical{m_D}^{2}$.

The *theoretical* virtue of the Mandelstam variables is that they are Lorentz invariants, with the same value in any inertial system. *Experimentally*, though, the more accessible parameters are energies and scattering angles.

(b) Find the CM energy of $A$, in terms of $\mechanical{s},\mechanical{t},\mechanical{u}$ and the masses. [*Answer:* $\energy{E_A^{\mathrm{CM}}}=(\mechanical{s}+\mechanical{m_A}^{2}-\mechanical{m_B}^{2})\mechanical{c}^{2}/2\sqrt{\mechanical{s}}$]

(c) Find the Lab ($B$ at rest) energy of $A$. [*Answer:* $\energy{E_A^{\mathrm{lab}}}=(\mechanical{s}-\mechanical{m_A}^{2}-\mechanical{m_B}^{2})\mechanical{c}^{2}/2\mechanical{m_B}$]

(d) Find the total CM energy ($\energy{E_{\mathrm{TOT}}}=\energy{E_A}+\energy{E_B}=\energy{E_C}+\energy{E_D}$). [*Answer:* $\energy{E_{\mathrm{TOT}}^{\mathrm{CM}}}=\sqrt{\mechanical{s}}\mechanical{c}^{2}$]

**3.23.** For elastic scattering of identical particles, $A+A\to A+A$, show that the Mandelstam variables (Problem 3.22) become

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{s}&=4(\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{2}+\mechanical{m}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2})/\mechanical{c}^{2}\\
\mechanical{t}&=-2\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{2}(1-\cos\mechanical{\theta})/\mechanical{c}^{2}\\
\mechanical{u}&=-2\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{2}(1+\cos\mechanical{\theta})/\mechanical{c}^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\mechanical{\mathbf p}$ is the CM momentum of the incident particle, and $\mechanical{\theta}$ is the scattering angle.

**3.24.** (Compton scattering.) A photon of wavelength $\signal{\lambda}$ collides elastically with a charged particle of mass $\mechanical{m}$. If the photon scatters at angle $\mechanical{\theta}$, find its outgoing wavelength, $\signal{\lambda'}$. [*Answer:* $\signal{\lambda'}=\signal{\lambda}+(\energy{h}/\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})(1-\cos\mechanical{\theta})$]
