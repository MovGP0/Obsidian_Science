> [!TLDR]
> Lorentz transformations relate event coordinates in inertial frames and give simultaneity, length contraction, time dilation, and velocity addition.

<!-- PDF page 89; printed page 81 -->

*In this chapter I summarize the basic principles, notation, and terminology of relativistic kinematics. This is material you must know cold in order to understand Chapters 6 through 11 (it is not needed for Chapters 4 and 5, however, and if you prefer you can read them first). Although the treatment is reasonably self-contained, I do assume that you have encountered special relativity before—if not, you should pause here and read the appropriate chapter in any introductory physics text before proceeding. If you are already quite familiar with relativity, this chapter will be an easy review—but read through it anyway because some of the notation may be new to you.*

According to the special theory of relativity,<sup>1</sup> the laws of physics apply just as well in a reference system moving at constant velocity as they do in one at rest. An embarrassing implication of this is that there's no way of telling which system (if any) is at rest, and hence there is no way of knowing what “the” velocity of any other system might be. So perhaps I had better start over. Ahem.

According to the special theory of relativity,<sup>1</sup> the laws of physics are equally valid in all *inertial* reference systems. An inertial system is one in which Newton's first law (the law of inertia) is obeyed: objects keep moving in straight lines at constant speeds unless acted upon by some force.[^ep-3-1-inertial] It's easy to see that any two inertial systems must be moving at constant velocity with respect to one another, and conversely, that any system moving at constant velocity with respect to an inertial system is itself inertial.

[^ep-3-1-inertial]: If you are wondering whether a freely falling system in a uniform gravitational field is “inertial,” you know more than is good for you. Let's just keep gravity out of it.

Imagine, then, that we have two inertial frames, $S$ and $S'$, with $S'$ moving at uniform velocity $\mechanical{\mathbf v}$ (magnitude $\mechanical{v}$) with respect to $S$ ($S$, then, is moving at velocity $-\mechanical{\mathbf v}$ with respect to $S'$). We may as well lay out our coordinates in such a way that the motion is along the common $\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{x'}$ axis (Fig. 3.1), and set the master clocks at the origin in each system so that both read zero at the instant the two coincide (that is, $\mechanical{t}=\mechanical{t'}=0$ when $\mechanical{x}=\mechanical{x'}=0$). Suppose, now, that some event occurs at position $(\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z})$ and time $\mechanical{t}$ in $S$. Question: What are the spacetime coordinates $(\mechanical{x'},\mechanical{y'},\mechanical{z'})$ and $\mechanical{t'}$ of this *same event* in $S'$? The answer is provided by the Lorentz transformations:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{i.}\quad &\mechanical{x'}=\mechanical{\gamma}(\mechanical{x}-\mechanical{v}\mechanical{t})\\
\text{ii.}\quad &\mechanical{y'}=\mechanical{y}\\
\text{iii.}\quad &\mechanical{z'}=\mechanical{z}\\
\text{iv.}\quad &\mechanical{t'}=\mechanical{\gamma}\left(\mechanical{t}-\frac{\mechanical{v}}{\mechanical{c}^{2}}\mechanical{x}\right)
\end{aligned}
\tag{3.1}
$$

where

$$
\mechanical{\gamma}\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^{2}/\mechanical{c}^{2}}}
\tag{3.2}
$$

The inverse transformations, which take us back from $S'$ to $S$, are obtained by simply changing the sign of $\mechanical{v}$ (see Problem 3.1):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{i.}\quad &\mechanical{x}=\mechanical{\gamma}(\mechanical{x'}+\mechanical{v}\mechanical{t'})\\
\text{ii.}\quad &\mechanical{y}=\mechanical{y'}\\
\text{iii.}\quad &\mechanical{z}=\mechanical{z'}\\
\text{iv.}\quad &\mechanical{t}=\mechanical{\gamma}\left(\mechanical{t'}+\frac{\mechanical{v}}{\mechanical{c}^{2}}\mechanical{x'}\right)
\end{aligned}
\tag{3.3}
$$

The Lorentz transformations have a number of immediate consequences, of which I mention briefly the most important:

1. *The relativity of simultaneity:* If two events occur at the same time in $S$, but at different locations, then they do *not* occur at the same time in $S'$. Specifically, if $\mechanical{t_A}=\mechanical{t_B}$, then

$$
\mechanical{t'_A}=\mechanical{t'_B}+\frac{\mechanical{\gamma}\mechanical{v}}{\mechanical{c}^{2}}(\mechanical{x_B}-\mechanical{x_A})
\tag{3.4}
$$

![[elementary-particles-fig-3-1-inertial-frames.svg|**Fig. 3.1** The inertial systems S and S′.]]

<!-- PDF page 91; printed page 83 -->

(see Problem 3.2). Events that are simultaneous in one inertial system, then, are not simultaneous in others.

2. *Lorentz contraction:* Suppose a stick lies on the $\mechanical{x'}$ axis, at rest in $S'$. Say one end is at the origin ($\mechanical{x'}=0$) and the other is at $\mechanical{L'}$ (so its length in $S'$ is $\mechanical{L'}$). What is its length as measured in $S$? Since the stick is *moving* with respect to $S$, we must be careful to record the positions of its two ends at the same instant, say $\mechanical{t}=0$. At that moment the left end is at $\mechanical{x}=0$ and the right end, according to equation (i), is at $\mechanical{x}=\mechanical{L'}/\mechanical{\gamma}$. Thus the length of the stick is $\mechanical{L}=\mechanical{L'}/\mechanical{\gamma}$, in $S$. Notice that $\mechanical{\gamma}$ is always greater than or equal to 1. It follows that a *moving object is shortened* by a factor of $\mechanical{\gamma}$, as compared with its length in the system in which it is at rest. Notice that Lorentz contraction only applies to lengths *along the direction of motion*; perpendicular dimensions are not affected.

3. *Time dilation:* Suppose the clock at the origin in $S'$ ticks off an interval $\mechanical{T'}$; for simplicity, say it runs from $\mechanical{t'}=0$ to $\mechanical{t'}=\mechanical{T'}$. How long is this period as measured in $S$? Well, it begins at $\mechanical{t}=0$, and it ends when $\mechanical{t'}=\mechanical{T'}$ at $\mechanical{x'}=0$, so [according to eq. (iv′)] $\mechanical{t}=\mechanical{\gamma}\mechanical{T'}$. Evidently the clocks in $S$ tick off a *longer* interval, $\mechanical{T}=\mechanical{\gamma}\mechanical{T'}$, by that same factor of $\mechanical{\gamma}$; or, put it the other way around: *moving clocks run slow*. Unlike Lorentz contraction, which is only indirectly relevant to elementary particle physics, time dilation is a commonplace in the laboratory. For in a sense every unstable particle has a built-in clock: whatever it is that tells the particle when its time is up. And these internal clocks do indeed run slow when the particle is moving. That is to say, a moving particle lasts longer (by a factor of $\mechanical{\gamma}$) than it would at rest.[^ep-3-1-lifetime] (The tabulated lifetimes are, of course, for particles at rest.) In fact, the cosmic ray muons produced in the upper atmosphere would never make it to ground level were it not for time dilation (see Problem 3.4).

4. *Velocity addition.* Suppose a particle is moving in the $\mechanical{x}$ direction at speed $\mechanical{u'}$, with respect to $S'$. What is its speed, $\mechanical{u}$, with respect to $S$? Well, it travels a distance $\mechanical{\Delta x}=\mechanical{\gamma}(\mechanical{\Delta x'}+\mechanical{v}\mechanical{\Delta t'})$ in a time $\mechanical{\Delta t}=\mechanical{\gamma}[\mechanical{\Delta t'}+(\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}^{2})\mechanical{\Delta x'}]$, so

$$
\frac{\mechanical{\Delta x}}{\mechanical{\Delta t}}
=\frac{\mechanical{\Delta x'}+\mechanical{v}\mechanical{\Delta t'}}{\mechanical{\Delta t'}+(\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}^{2})\mechanical{\Delta x'}}
=\frac{(\mechanical{\Delta x'}/\mechanical{\Delta t'})+\mechanical{v}}{1+(\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}^{2})(\mechanical{\Delta x'}/\mechanical{\Delta t'})}.
$$

But $\mechanical{\Delta x}/\mechanical{\Delta t}=\mechanical{u}$, and $\mechanical{\Delta x'}/\mechanical{\Delta t'}=\mechanical{u'}$, so

$$
\mechanical{u}=\frac{\mechanical{u'}+\mechanical{v}}{1+(\mechanical{u'}\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}^{2})}
\tag{3.5}
$$

The numerator represents the classical answer to the same question, $\mechanical{u}=\mechanical{u'}+\mechanical{v}$; the denominator introduces a relativistic correction that is small unless $\mechanical{u'}$ and $\mechanical{v}$ are close to $\mechanical{c}$. Notice that if $\mechanical{u'}=\mechanical{c}$, then $\mechanical{u}=\mechanical{c}$ also: the speed of light is the same in all inertial systems.

[^ep-3-1-lifetime]: Actually, the disintegration of an individual particle is a random process; when we speak of a “lifetime” we really mean the *average* lifetime of that particle type. When I say that a moving particle lasts longer, I really mean that the *average* lifetime of a group of moving particles is longer.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z},\mechanical{x'},\mechanical{y'},\mechanical{z'}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{m}}$ | Spatial coordinates | The position of an event in the two inertial frames. |
| $\mechanical{x_A},\mechanical{x_B}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{m}}$ | Event positions | The positions of two simultaneous events in frame S. |
| $\mechanical{t},\mechanical{t'},\mechanical{t_A},\mechanical{t_B},\mechanical{t'_A},\mechanical{t'_B}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{s}}$ | Event times | The times assigned to events in each frame. |

| $\mechanical{c}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}}$ | Speed of light | The invariant speed of light. |
| $\mechanical{\gamma}$ | $1$ | Lorentz factor | The factor that relates lengths and time intervals between frames. |
| $\mechanical{L},\mechanical{L'}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{m}}$ | Stick length | The length of the stick measured in each frame. |
| $\mechanical{T},\mechanical{T'}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{s}}$ | Clock interval | The elapsed time measured in each frame. |
| $\mechanical{u},\mechanical{u'}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}}$ | Particle speed | The particle speed measured in each frame. |
| $\mechanical{\Delta x},\mechanical{\Delta x'}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{m}}$ | Displacement | The distance traveled along the direction of relative motion. |
| $\mechanical{\Delta t},\mechanical{\Delta t'}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{s}}$ | Travel time | The time used to measure the particle displacement. |
