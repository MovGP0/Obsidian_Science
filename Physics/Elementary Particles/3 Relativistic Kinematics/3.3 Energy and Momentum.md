> [!TLDR]
> Proper time and proper velocity define the energy-momentum four-vector, rest energy, kinetic energy, and the photon energy relation.

<!-- PDF page 95; printed page 87 -->

Suppose you're driving down the highway, and pretend for the sake of argument that you're going at close to the speed of light. You might want to keep an eye on two different “times”: if you're worried about making an appointment in San Francisco, you should check the stationary clocks posted now and then along the side of the road. But if you're wondering when would be an appropriate time to stop for a bite to eat, it would be more sensible to look at the watch on your wrist. For according to relativity, the moving clock (in this case, your watch) is running slow (relative to the “stationary” clocks on the ground), and so too is your heart rate, your metabolism, your speech and thought, *everything*. Specifically, while the “ground” time advances by an infinitesimal amount $d\mechanical{t}$, your own (or *proper*) time advances by the smaller amount $d\mechanical{\tau}$:

$$
d\mechanical{\tau}=\frac{d\mechanical{t}}{\mechanical{\gamma}}
\tag{3.28}
$$

At normal driving speeds, of course, $\mechanical{\gamma}$ is so close to 1 that $d\mechanical{t}$ and $d\mechanical{\tau}$ are essentially identical, but in elementary particle physics the distinction between laboratory time (read off the clock on the wall) and particle time (as it would appear on the particle's watch) is crucial. Although we can always get from one to the other, using equation (3.28), in practice it is usually most convenient to work with proper time, because it is invariant. All observers can read the particle's watch, and at any given moment they must all agree on what it says, even though their own clocks may differ from it and from one another.

When we speak of the “velocity” of a particle (with respect to the laboratory), we mean, of course, the distance it travels (measured in the lab frame) divided by the time it takes (measured on the lab clock):

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf v}=\frac{d\mechanical{\mathbf x}}{d\mechanical{t}}
\tag{3.29}
$$

But in view of what has just been said, it is also useful to introduce the “proper” velocity, $\mechanical{\boldsymbol\eta}$, which is the distance traveled (again, measured in the lab frame) divided by the *proper* time:[^ep-3-3-proper-velocity]

<!-- PDF page 96; printed page 88 -->

$$
\mechanical{\boldsymbol\eta}\equiv\frac{d\mechanical{\mathbf x}}{d\mechanical{\tau}}
\tag{3.30}
$$

According to equation (3.28), the two velocities are related by a factor of $\mechanical{\gamma}$:

$$
\mechanical{\boldsymbol\eta}=\mechanical{\gamma}\mechanical{\mathbf v}
\tag{3.31}
$$

However, $\mechanical{\boldsymbol\eta}$ is much easier to work with, for if we want to go from the lab system, $S$, to a moving system, $S'$, *both the numerator and the denominator in (3.29) must be transformed* [leading to the cumbersome velocity addition rule (3.5)], whereas in equation (3.30) *only the numerator transforms*; $d\mechanical{\tau}$, as we have seen, is invariant. In fact, proper velocity is part of a four-vector:

$$
\mechanical{\eta^\mu}=\frac{d\mechanical{x^\mu}}{d\mechanical{\tau}}
\tag{3.32}
$$

whose zeroth component is

$$
\mechanical{\eta^0}=\frac{d\mechanical{x^0}}{d\mechanical{\tau}}
=\frac{d(\mechanical{c}\mechanical{t})}{(1/\mechanical{\gamma})d\mechanical{t}}
=\mechanical{\gamma}\mechanical{c}
\tag{3.33}
$$

Thus

$$
\mechanical{\eta^\mu}=\mechanical{\gamma}(\mechanical{c},\mechanical{v_x},\mechanical{v_y},\mechanical{v_z})
\tag{3.34}
$$

Incidentally, $\mechanical{\eta_\mu}\mechanical{\eta^\mu}$ should be invariant, and it is:

$$
\mechanical{\eta_\mu}\mechanical{\eta^\mu}
=\mechanical{\gamma}^{2}(\mechanical{c}^{2}-\mechanical{v_x}^{2}-\mechanical{v_y}^{2}-\mechanical{v_z}^{2})
=\mechanical{\gamma}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}(1-\mechanical{v}^{2}/\mechanical{c}^{2})
=\mechanical{c}^{2}
\tag{3.35}
$$

They don't make 'em more invariant than *that!*

Classically, momentum is mass times velocity. We would like to carry this over in relativity, but the question arises: Which velocity should we use—*ordinary* velocity or *proper* velocity? Classical considerations offer no clue, for the two are equal in the nonrelativistic limit. In a sense, it's just a matter of definition, but there is a subtle and compelling reason why ordinary velocity would be a *bad* choice, whereas proper velocity is a *good* choice. The point is this: If we defined momentum as $\mechanical{m}\mechanical{\mathbf v}$, then the law of conservation of momentum would be inconsistent with the principle of relativity (if it held in one inertial system, it would *not* hold in other inertial systems). But if we define momentum as $\mechanical{m}\mechanical{\boldsymbol\eta}$, then conservation of momentum *is* consistent with the principle of relativity (if it holds in one inertial system, it automatically holds in all inertial systems). I'll let you prove this for yourself in Problem 3.10. Mind you, this doesn't guarantee that momentum *is* conserved; that's a matter for *experiments* to decide. But it *does* say that if we're hoping to extend momentum conservation to the relativistic domain, we had better *not* define momentum as $\mechanical{m}\mechanical{\mathbf v}$, whereas $\mechanical{m}\mechanical{\boldsymbol\eta}$ is perfectly acceptable.

[^ep-3-3-proper-velocity]: Proper velocity is a hybrid quantity, in the sense that distance is measured in the *lab* frame, whereas time is measured in the *particle* frame. Some people object to the adjective “proper” in this context, holding that this should be reserved for quantities measured entirely in the particle frame. Of course, in its *own* frame the particle never moves at all—its velocity is zero. If my terminology disturbs you, call $\mechanical{\boldsymbol\eta}$ the “four-velocity.” I should add that although proper velocity is the more convenient quantity to *calculate* with, ordinary velocity is still the more *natural* quantity from the point of view of an observer watching a particle fly past.

<!-- PDF page 97; printed page 89 -->

That's a tricky argument, and if you didn't follow it, try reading that last paragraph again. The upshot is that in relativity, momentum is defined as mass times *proper* velocity:

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf p}\equiv\mechanical{m}\mechanical{\boldsymbol\eta}
\tag{3.36}
$$

Since proper velocity is part of a four-vector, the same goes for momentum:

$$
\mechanical{p^\mu}=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{\eta^\mu}
\tag{3.37}
$$

The spatial components of $\mechanical{p^\mu}$ constitute the (relativistic) momentum three-vector:

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf p}=\mechanical{\gamma}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{\mathbf v}
=\frac{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{\mathbf v}}{\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^{2}/\mechanical{c}^{2}}}
\tag{3.38}
$$

Meanwhile, the “time” component is

$$
\mechanical{p^0}=\mechanical{\gamma}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}
\tag{3.39}
$$

For reasons that will appear in a moment, we define the “relativistic *energy*,” $\energy{E}$, as

$$
\energy{E}=\mechanical{\gamma}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}
=\frac{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}{\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^{2}/\mechanical{c}^{2}}}
\tag{3.40}
$$

The zeroth component of $\mechanical{p^\mu}$, then, is $\energy{E}/\mechanical{c}$. Thus energy and momentum together make up a four-vector—the *energy-momentum four-vector*:

$$
\mechanical{p^\mu}=\left(\frac{\energy{E}}{\mechanical{c}},\mechanical{p_x},\mechanical{p_y},\mechanical{p_z}\right)
\tag{3.41}
$$

Incidentally, from equations (3.35) and (3.37) we have

$$
\mechanical{p_\mu}\mechanical{p^\mu}
=\frac{\energy{E}^{2}}{\mechanical{c}^{2}}-\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{2}
=\mechanical{m}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}
\tag{3.42}
$$

which, again, is manifestly invariant.

The relativistic momentum (3.38) reduces to the classical expression in the nonrelativistic regime ($\mechanical{v}\ll\mechanical{c}$), but the same cannot be said for relativistic energy (3.40). To see how this quantity comes to be called “energy,” we expand the radical in a Taylor series:

$$
\energy{E}=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mechanical{v}^{2}}{\mechanical{c}^{2}}+\frac{3}{8}\frac{\mechanical{v}^{4}}{\mechanical{c}^{4}}+\cdots\right)
=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{v}^{2}+\frac{3}{8}\mechanical{m}\frac{\mechanical{v}^{4}}{\mechanical{c}^{2}}+\cdots
\tag{3.43}
$$

Notice that the *second* term here corresponds to the classical kinetic energy, while the leading term ($\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}$) is a constant. Now you may recall that in classical mechanics only *changes* in energy are physically significant—you can add a constant with impunity. In this sense the relativistic formula *is* consistent with the classical one, in the limit $\mechanical{v}\ll\mechanical{c}$ where the higher terms in the expansion are negligible. The constant term, which survives even when $\mechanical{v}=0$, is called the *rest energy*;

<!-- PDF page 98; printed page 90 -->

$$
\energy{R}\equiv\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}
\tag{3.44}
$$

the remainder, which is energy attributable to the motion of the particle, is the *relativistic kinetic energy*:

$$
\energy{T}=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}(\mechanical{\gamma}-1)
=\frac{1}{2}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{v}^{2}+\frac{3}{8}\mechanical{m}\frac{\mechanical{v}^{4}}{\mechanical{c}^{2}}+\cdots
\tag{3.45}
$$

(Notice that I have never mentioned *relativistic mass* in all this. It is a superfluous quantity that serves no useful function. In case you encounter it, the definition is $\mechanical{m_{\mathrm{rel}}}\equiv\mechanical{\gamma}\mechanical{m}$; it has died out because it differs from $\energy{E}$ only by a factor of $\mechanical{c}^{2}$. Whatever can be said about $\mechanical{m_{\mathrm{rel}}}$ could just as well be said about $\energy{E}$, for instance, the “conservation of relativistic mass” is nothing but conservation of energy, with a factor of $\mechanical{c}^{2}$ divided out.)

In classical mechanics there is no such thing as a massless particle; its momentum ($\mechanical{m}\mechanical{v}$) would be zero, its kinetic energy ($\tfrac{1}{2}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{v}^{2}$) would be zero, it could sustain no force, since $\mechanical{F}=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{a}$—it would be a dynamical cipher. At first glance you might suppose that the same would be true in relativity, but a careful inspection of the formulas

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf p}=\frac{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{\mathbf v}}{\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^{2}/\mechanical{c}^{2}}},
\qquad
\energy{E}=\frac{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}{\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^{2}/\mechanical{c}^{2}}}
\tag{3.46}
$$

reveals a loophole: When $\mechanical{m}=0$ the numerators are zero, but if $\mechanical{v}=\mechanical{c}$, the denominators *also* vanish, and these equations are indeterminate (0/0). So it is just possible that *we could allow $\mechanical{m}=0$, provided the particle always travels at the speed of light*. In this case equations (3.46) will not serve to define $\energy{E}$ and $\mechanical{\mathbf p}$; nevertheless, equation (3.42) presumably still applies, so that

$$
\energy{E}=\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p}\rvert\mechanical{c}
\tag{3.47}
$$

for massless particles. Personally, I would regard this “argument” as a joke, were it not for the fact that at least two types of massless particles (the photon and the neutrinos) are known to exist in nature. They do indeed travel at the speed of light, and their energy and momentum are related by equation (3.47). So evidently we must take the loophole seriously. You may well ask: If equations (3.46) do *not* define $\mechanical{\mathbf p}$ and $\energy{E}$, what *does* determine the momentum and energy of a massless particle? Not the mass (that's zero by assumption), not the speed (that's always $\mechanical{c}$). How, then, *does* a photon with an energy of $2\,\unit{\mathrm{eV}}$ differ from a photon with an energy of $3\,\unit{\mathrm{eV}}$? Relativity offers no answer to this question, but curiously enough *quantum* mechanics *does*, in the form of Planck's formula:

$$
\energy{E}=\energy{h}\signal{\nu}
\tag{3.48}
$$

<!-- PDF page 99; printed page 91 -->

It is the *frequency* of the photon that determines its energy and momentum: The $2\,\unit{\mathrm{eV}}$ photon is *red*, and the $3\,\unit{\mathrm{eV}}$ photon is *purple!*

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\mechanical{t}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{s}}$ | Laboratory time | The time measured by a clock in the laboratory frame. |
| $\mechanical{\tau}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{s}}$ | Proper time | The time measured by a clock that moves with the particle. |
| $\mechanical{\mathbf x},\mechanical{x^\mu}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{m}}$ | Position and spacetime coordinates | The laboratory position and the position-time four-vector. |
| $\mechanical{\mathbf v},\mechanical{v},\mechanical{v_x},\mechanical{v_y},\mechanical{v_z}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}}$ | Ordinary velocity | The particle velocity, speed, and Cartesian components. |
| $\mechanical{\boldsymbol\eta},\mechanical{\eta^\mu},\mechanical{\eta_\mu}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}}$ | Proper velocity | The displacement per unit proper time and its four-vector form. |
| $\mechanical{\gamma}$ | $1$ | Lorentz factor | The factor that relates ordinary and proper velocity. |
| $\mechanical{c}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}}$ | Speed of light | The invariant speed used in the relativistic relations. |
| $\mechanical{m}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{kg}}$ | Mass | The invariant mass of the particle. |
| $\mechanical{\mathbf p},\mechanical{p_x},\mechanical{p_y},\mechanical{p_z}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}}$ | Momentum | The particle momentum and its spatial components. |
| $\mechanical{p^\mu},\mechanical{p_\mu}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}}$ | Energy-momentum four-vector | The vector whose time component is energy divided by the speed of light. |
| $\energy{E}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{J}}$ | Relativistic energy | The total particle energy, also expressed in electronvolts for the photons. |
| $\energy{R}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{J}}$ | Rest energy | The energy associated with the invariant mass. |
| $\energy{T}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{J}}$ | Kinetic energy | The total energy minus the rest energy. |
| $\mechanical{m_{\mathrm{rel}}}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{kg}}$ | Relativistic mass | The alternative mass definition given in the source. |
| $\mechanical{F}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{N}}$ | Force | The classical force in Newton's second law. |
| $\mechanical{a}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}}$ | Acceleration | The acceleration in Newton's second law. |
| $\energy{h}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{J\,s}}$ | Planck constant | The constant that relates photon frequency to energy. |
| $\signal{\nu}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{Hz}}$ | Photon frequency | The frequency that determines photon energy and momentum. |
