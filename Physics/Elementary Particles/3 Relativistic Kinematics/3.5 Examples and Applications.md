> [!TLDR]
> Five worked examples use energy-momentum invariants and the center-of-momentum frame to solve collisions, decays, and threshold problems.

<!-- PDF page 101; printed page 93 -->

![[elementary-particles-fig-3-3-sticky-collision.svg|**Fig. 3.3** Sticky collision of two equal masses (Example 3.1).]]

Solving problems in relativistic kinematics is as much an art as a science. Although the *physics* involved is minimal—nothing but conservation of energy and conservation of momentum—the *algebra* can be formidable. Whether a given problem takes two lines or seven pages depends a lot on how skillful and experienced you are at manipulating the tools and the tricks of the trade. I now propose to work a few examples, pointing out as I go along some of the labor-saving devices that are available to you.<sup>2</sup>

### Example 3.1

Two lumps of clay, each of mass $\mechanical{m}$, collide head-on at $\tfrac{3}{5}\mechanical{c}$ (Fig. 3.3). They stick together. Question: What is the mass $\mechanical{M}$ of the final composite lump?

Question: What is the mass, $\mechanical{M}$ of the final composite lump?

*Solution.* Conservation of energy says $\energy{E_1}+\energy{E_2}=\energy{E_M}$. Conservation of momentum says $\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}+\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}=\mechanical{\mathbf p_M}$. In this case conservation of momentum is trivial: $\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}=-\mechanical{\mathbf p_2}$, so the final lump is at rest (which was obvious from the start). The initial energies are equal, so conservation of energy yields

$$
\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c}^{2}=2\energy{E_m}
=\frac{2\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}{\sqrt{1-(3/5)^2}}
=\frac{5}{4}(2\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2})
$$

Conclusion: $\mechanical{M}=\tfrac{5}{2}\mechanical{m}$. Notice that this is *greater* than the sum of the initial masses; in sticky collisions kinetic energy is converted into rest energy, so the mass increases.

### Example 3.2

A particle of mass $\mechanical{M}$, initially at rest, decays into two pieces, each of mass $\mechanical{m}$ (Fig. 3.4). Question: What is the speed of each piece as it flies off?

*Solution.* This is, of course, the reverse of the process in Example 3.1. Conservation of momentum just says that the two lumps fly off in opposite directions at equal speeds. Conservation of energy requires that

$$
\mechanical{M}=\frac{2\mechanical{m}}{\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^{2}/\mechanical{c}^{2}}},
\quad\text{so}\quad
\mechanical{v}=\mechanical{c}\sqrt{1-(2\mechanical{m}/\mechanical{M})^2}
$$

![[elementary-particles-fig-3-4-equal-particle-decay.svg|**Fig. 3.4** A particle decays into two equal pieces. (Example 3.2).]]

<!-- PDF page 102; printed page 94 -->

This answer makes no sense unless $\mechanical{M}$ exceeds $2\mechanical{m}$; there has to be at least enough rest energy available to cover the rest energies in the final state (any *extra* is fine; it can be soaked up in the form of kinetic energy). We say that $\mechanical{M}=2\mechanical{m}$ is the *threshold* for the process $\mechanical{M}\to2\mechanical{m}$ to occur. The deuteron, for example, is below the threshold for decay into proton plus neutron ($\mechanical{m_d}=1875.6\,\unit{\mathrm{MeV}}/\mechanical{c}^{2}$; $\mechanical{m_p}+\mechanical{m_n}=1877.9\,\unit{\mathrm{MeV}}/\mechanical{c}^{2}$), and therefore is stable. A deuteron can be *pulled* apart, but only by pumping enough energy into the system to make up the difference. (If it puzzles you that a bound state of $p$ and $n$ should weigh *less* than the sum of its parts, the point is that the binding energy of the deuteron, which, like all internal energy, is reflected in its rest mass, is *negative*. Indeed, for *any* stable bound state the binding energy must be negative; if the composite particle weighs *more* than the sum of its constituents, it will spontaneously disintegrate.)

### Example 3.3

A pion at rest decays into a muon plus a neutrino (Fig. 3.5). Question: What is the speed of the muon?

*Solution.* Conservation of energy requires $\energy{E_\pi}=\energy{E_\mu}+\energy{E_\nu}$. Conservation of momentum gives $\mechanical{\mathbf p_\pi}=\mechanical{\mathbf p_\mu}+\mechanical{\mathbf p_\nu}$; but $\mechanical{\mathbf p_\pi}=0$, so $\mechanical{\mathbf p_\mu}=-\mechanical{\mathbf p_\nu}$. Thus the muon and the neutrino fly off back-to-back, with equal and opposite momenta.

To proceed, we need a formula relating the energy of a particle to its momentum; equation (3.42) does the job. [You might have been inclined to solve equation (3.38) for the velocity, and plug the result into equation (3.40). But that would be very poor strategy. In general, velocity is a bad parameter to work with, in relativity. Better to use equation (3.42), which takes you *directly* back and forth between $\energy{E}$ and $\mechanical{\mathbf p}$.]

*Suggestion 1.* To get the energy of a particle, when you know its momentum (or vice versa), use the invariant

$$
\energy{E}^{2}-\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}
=\mechanical{m}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{4}
\tag{3.49}
$$

In the present case, then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\energy{E_\pi}&=\mechanical{m_\pi}\mechanical{c}^{2}\\
\energy{E_\mu}&=\mechanical{c}\sqrt{\mechanical{m_\mu}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\mathbf p_\mu}^{2}}\\
\energy{E_\nu}&=\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_\nu}\rvert\mechanical{c}
=\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_\mu}\rvert\mechanical{c}
\end{aligned}
$$

Putting these into the equation for conservation of energy, we have

$$
\mechanical{m_\pi}\mechanical{c}^{2}
=\mechanical{c}\sqrt{\mechanical{m_\mu}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\mathbf p_\mu}^{2}}
+\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_\mu}\rvert\mechanical{c}
$$

or

$$
(\mechanical{m_\pi}\mechanical{c}-\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_\mu}\rvert)^2
=\mechanical{m_\mu}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{\mathbf p_\mu}^{2}
$$

![[elementary-particles-fig-3-5-pion-decay.svg|**Fig. 3.5** Decay of the charged pion (Example 3.3).]]

<!-- PDF page 103; printed page 95 -->

Solving for $\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_\mu}\rvert$, we find

$$
\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_\mu}\rvert
=\frac{\mechanical{m_\pi}^{2}-\mechanical{m_\mu}^{2}}{2\mechanical{m_\pi}}\mechanical{c}
$$

Meanwhile, the energy of the muon [from eq. (3.49)] is

$$
\energy{E_\mu}=\frac{\mechanical{m_\pi}^{2}+\mechanical{m_\mu}^{2}}{2\mechanical{m_\pi}}\mechanical{c}^{2}
$$

Once we know the energy and momentum of a particle, it is easy to find its velocity. If $\energy{E}=\mechanical{\gamma}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}$ and $\mechanical{\mathbf p}=\mechanical{\gamma}\mechanical{m}\mechanical{\mathbf v}$, dividing gives

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf p}/\energy{E}=\mechanical{\mathbf v}/\mechanical{c}^{2}
$$

*Suggestion 2.* If you know the energy and momentum of a particle, and you want to determine its velocity, use

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf v}=\mechanical{\mathbf p}\mechanical{c}^{2}/\energy{E}
\tag{3.50}
$$

So the answer to our problem is

$$
\mechanical{v_\mu}=\frac{\mechanical{m_\pi}^{2}-\mechanical{m_\mu}^{2}}{\mechanical{m_\pi}^{2}+\mechanical{m_\mu}^{2}}\mechanical{c}
$$

Putting in the actual masses, I get $\mechanical{v_\mu}=0.271\mechanical{c}$.

There is nothing wrong with that calculation; it was a straightforward and systematic exploitation of the conservation laws. But I want to show you now a faster way to get the energy and momentum of the muon, by using four-vector notation. [I should put a superscript $\mu$ on all the four-vectors, but I don't want you to confuse the spacetime index $\mu$ with the particle identifier $\mu$, so here, and often in the future, I will suppress the spacetime indices, and use a dot to indicate the scalar product.] Conservation of energy and momentum requires

$$
\mechanical{p_\pi}=\mechanical{p_\mu}+\mechanical{p_\nu},
\quad\text{or}\quad
\mechanical{p_\nu}=\mechanical{p_\pi}-\mechanical{p_\mu}
$$

Taking the scalar product of each side with itself, we obtain

$$
\mechanical{p_\nu}^{2}=\mechanical{p_\pi}^{2}+\mechanical{p_\mu}^{2}
-2\mechanical{p_\pi}\cdot\mechanical{p_\mu}
$$

But

$$
\mechanical{p_\nu}^{2}=0;\quad
\mechanical{p_\pi}^{2}=\mechanical{m_\pi}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2},\quad
\mechanical{p_\mu}^{2}=\mechanical{m_\mu}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2};\quad\text{and}\quad
\mechanical{p_\pi}\cdot\mechanical{p_\mu}
=\frac{\energy{E_\pi}}{\mechanical{c}}\frac{\energy{E_\mu}}{\mechanical{c}}
=\mechanical{m_\pi}\energy{E_\mu}
$$

Therefore

$$
0=\mechanical{m_\pi}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}+\mechanical{m_\mu}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}
-2\mechanical{m_\pi}\energy{E_\mu}
$$

from which $\energy{E_\mu}$ follows immediately. By the same token

$$
\mechanical{p_\mu}=\mechanical{p_\pi}-\mechanical{p_\nu}
$$

Squaring yields

$$
\mechanical{m_\mu}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}
=\mechanical{m_\pi}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}-2\mechanical{m_\pi}\energy{E_\nu}
$$

<!-- PDF page 104; printed page 96 -->

But $\energy{E_\nu}=\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_\nu}\rvert\mechanical{c}=\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_\mu}\rvert\mechanical{c}$, so

$$
2\mechanical{m_\pi}\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_\mu}\rvert
=(\mechanical{m_\pi}^{2}-\mechanical{m_\mu}^{2})\mechanical{c}
$$

which gives us $\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_\mu}\rvert$. In this case the problem was simple enough that the savings afforded by four-vector notation are meager, but in more complicated problems the benefits can be enormous.

*Suggestion 3.* Use four-vector notation, and exploit the invariant dot product.

One reason the use of invariants is so powerful in this business is that we are free to evaluate them in any inertial system we like. Frequently the laboratory frame is not the simplest one to work with. In a typical scattering experiment, for instance, a beam of particles is fired at a stationary target. The reaction under study might be, say, $p+p\to\text{whatever}$, but in the laboratory the situation is asymmetrical, since one proton is moving and the other is at rest. Kinematically, the process is much simpler when viewed from a system in which the two protons approach one another with equal speeds. We call this the *center-of-momentum* (CM) frame, because in this system the total (three-vector) momentum is zero.

### Example 3.4

The Bevatron at Berkeley was built with the idea of producing antiprotons, by the reaction $p+p\to p+p+p+\bar p$. That is, a high-energy proton strikes a proton at rest, creating (in addition to the original particles) a proton-antiproton pair. Question: What is the threshold energy for this reaction (i.e., the minimum energy of the incident proton)?

*Solution.* In the laboratory the process looks like Figure 3.6a; in the CM frame, it looks like Figure 3.6b. Now, what is the condition for threshold? Answer: just barely enough incident energy to create the extra two particles. In the lab frame it is hard to see how we would formulate this condition, but in the CM it is easy: *All four final particles must be at rest*, with no energy “wasted” in the form of kinetic energy. (We can't have that in the lab frame, of course, since conservation of momentum requires that there be some residual motion.)

![[elementary-particles-fig-3-6-antiproton-production.svg|**Fig. 3.6** p + p → p + p + p + p̄. (a) In the lab frame; (b) in the CM frame.]]

<!-- PDF page 105; printed page 97 -->

Let $\mechanical{p_{\mathrm{TOT}}^\mu}$ be the *total* energy-momentum four-vector in the lab; it is conserved, so it doesn't matter whether we evaluate it before or after the collision. We'll do it *before*:

$$
\mechanical{p_{\mathrm{TOT}}^\mu}
=\left(\frac{\energy{E}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}{\mechanical{c}},
\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p}\rvert,0,0\right)
$$

where $\energy{E}$ and $\mechanical{\mathbf p}$ are the energy and momentum of the incident proton, and $\mechanical{m}$ is the proton mass. Let $\mechanical{p_{\mathrm{TOT}}^{\mu'}}$ be the total energy-momentum four-vector in the CM. Again, we can evaluate it before or after the collision, this time we'll do it *after*:

$$
\mechanical{p_{\mathrm{TOT}}^{\mu'}}=(4\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c},0,0,0)
$$

since (at threshold) all four particles are at rest. Now $\mechanical{p_{\mathrm{TOT}}^\mu}\ne\mechanical{p_{\mathrm{TOT}}^{\mu'}}$, obviously, but the invariant products $\mechanical{p_{\mu\mathrm{TOT}}}\mechanical{p_{\mathrm{TOT}}^\mu}$ and $\mechanical{p_{\mu'\mathrm{TOT}}}\mechanical{p_{\mathrm{TOT}}^{\mu'}}$ are equal:

$$
\left(\frac{\energy{E}}{\mechanical{c}}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}\right)^2
-\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{2}=(4\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})^2
$$

Using the standard relation (3.49) to eliminate $\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{2}$, and solving for $\energy{E}$, we find

$$
\energy{E}=7\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}
$$

Evidently the incident proton must carry a kinetic energy at least six times its rest energy, for this process to occur. (And in fact the first antiprotons were discovered when the machine reached about $6000\,\unit{\mathrm{MeV}}$.)

This is perhaps a good place to emphasize the distinction between a *conserved* quantity and an *invariant* quantity. Energy is *conserved*—the same value *after* the collision as *before*—but it is not invariant. Mass is *invariant*—the same in all inertial systems—but it is not conserved. Some quantities are both invariant and conserved; many are neither. As Example 3.4 indicates, the clever exploitation of conserved and invariant quantities can save you a lot of messy algebra. It also demonstrates that some problems are easier to analyze in the CM system, whereas others may be simpler in the lab frame.

*Suggestion 4.* If a problem seems cumbersome in the lab frame, try analyzing it in the CM system.

Even if you're dealing with something more complicated than a collision of two identical particles, the center-of-momentum (in which $\mechanical{\mathbf p_{\mathrm{TOT}}}=0$) is still a useful reference frame, for in this system conservation of momentum is trivial: zero before, zero after. But you might wonder whether there is always a CM frame. In other words, given a swarm of particles with masses $\mechanical{m_1},\mechanical{m_2},\mechanical{m_3},\ldots$, and velocities $\mechanical{\mathbf v_1},\mechanical{\mathbf v_2},\mechanical{\mathbf v_3},\ldots$, does there necessarily exist an inertial system in which the total (three-vector) momentum is zero? The answer is *yes*; I will prove it by finding the velocity of that frame and demonstrating that this velocity is less than $\mechanical{c}$. The total energy and momentum in the lab frame ($S$) are

<!-- PDF page 106; printed page 98 -->

$$
\energy{E_{\mathrm{TOT}}}=\sum_i\mechanical{\gamma_i}\mechanical{m_i}\mechanical{c}^{2};
\qquad
\mechanical{\mathbf p_{\mathrm{TOT}}}=\sum_i\mechanical{\gamma_i}\mechanical{m_i}\mechanical{\mathbf v_i}
\tag{3.51}
$$

Since $\mechanical{p_{\mathrm{TOT}}^\mu}$ is a four-vector, we can use the Lorentz transformations to get the momentum in system $S'$, moving in the direction of $\mechanical{\mathbf p_{\mathrm{TOT}}}$ with speed $\mechanical{v}$

$$
\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p'_{\mathrm{TOT}}}\rvert
=\mechanical{\gamma}\left(\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_{\mathrm{TOT}}}\rvert
-\mechanical{\beta}\frac{\energy{E_{\mathrm{TOT}}}}{\mechanical{c}}\right)
$$

In particular, this momentum is *zero* if $\mechanical{v}$ is chosen such that

$$
\frac{\mechanical{v}}{\mechanical{c}}
=\frac{\lvert\mechanical{\mathbf p_{\mathrm{TOT}}}\rvert\mechanical{c}}{\energy{E_{\mathrm{TOT}}}}
=\frac{\left\lvert\sum_i\mechanical{\gamma_i}\mechanical{m_i}\mechanical{\mathbf v_i}\right\rvert}
{\sum_i\mechanical{\gamma_i}\mechanical{m_i}\mechanical{c}}
$$

Now, the length of the sum of three-vectors cannot exceed the sum of their lengths (this geometrically evident fact is known as the *triangle inequality*), so

$$
\frac{\mechanical{v}}{\mechanical{c}}\le
\frac{\sum_i\mechanical{\gamma_i}\mechanical{m_i}(\mechanical{v_i}/\mechanical{c})}
{\sum_i\mechanical{\gamma_i}\mechanical{m_i}}
$$

and since $\mechanical{v_i}<\mechanical{c}$, we can be sure that $\mechanical{v}<\mechanical{c}$.[^ep-3-5-massive] Thus, the CM system always exists, and its velocity relative to the lab frame is given by

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf v_{\mathrm{CM}}}
=\frac{\mechanical{\mathbf p_{\mathrm{TOT}}}\mechanical{c}^{2}}{\energy{E_{\mathrm{TOT}}}}
\tag{3.52}
$$

It seems odd, looking back at the answer to Example 3.4, that it takes an incident kinetic energy *six times* the proton rest energy to produce a $p$–$\bar p$ pair. After all, we're only creating $2\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}$ of new rest energy. This example illustrates the inefficiency of scattering off a stationary target; conservation of momentum forces you to waste a lot of energy as kinetic energy in the final state. Suppose we could have fired the two protons at one another, making the laboratory itself the CM system. Then it would suffice to give each proton a kinetic energy of only $\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}$, one-sixth of what the stationary-target experiment requires. This realization led, in the early 1970s, to the development of so-called *colliding-beam* machines (see Fig. 3.7). Today, virtually every new machine in high-energy physics is a collider.

### Example 3.5

Suppose two identical particles, each with mass $\mechanical{m}$ and kinetic energy $\energy{T}$, collide head-on. Question: What is their *relative* kinetic energy, $\energy{T'}$ (i.e., the kinetic energy of one in the rest system of the other)?

[^ep-3-5-massive]: I am tacitly assuming that at least one of the particles is massive. If *all* of them are massless, we *may* obtain $\mechanical{v}=\mechanical{c}$, in which case there is no CM system. For example, there is no CM frame for a single photon.

<!-- PDF page 107; printed page 99 -->

![[elementary-particles-fig-3-7-colliding-beams-fixed-target.svg|**Fig. 3.7** Two experimental arrangements: (a) Colliding beams; (b) fixed target.]]

*Solution.* There are many ways to do this one. A quick method is to write down the total four-momentum in the CM and in the lab

$$
\mechanical{p_{\mathrm{TOT}}^\mu}
=\left(\frac{2\energy{E}}{\mechanical{c}},\mathbf 0\right),
\qquad
\mechanical{p_{\mathrm{TOT}}^{\mu'}}
=\left(\frac{\energy{E'}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}{\mechanical{c}},\mechanical{\mathbf p'}\right)
$$

set $(\mechanical{p_{\mathrm{TOT}}})^2=(\mechanical{p'_{\mathrm{TOT}}})^2$:

$$
\left(\frac{2\energy{E}}{\mechanical{c}}\right)^2
=\left(\frac{\energy{E'}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}{\mechanical{c}}\right)^2
-\mechanical{\mathbf p'}^{2}
$$

use equation (3.49) to eliminate $\mechanical{\mathbf p'}$

$$
2\energy{E}^{2}=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}(\energy{E'}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2})
$$

and express the answer in terms of $\energy{T}=\energy{E}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}$ and $\energy{T'}=\energy{E'}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}$

$$
\energy{T'}=4\energy{T}\left(1+\frac{\energy{T}}{2\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}\right)
\tag{3.53}
$$

The *classical* answer would have been $\energy{T'}=4\energy{T}$, to which this reduces when $\energy{T}\ll\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}$. (In the rest system of $B$, $A$ has, classically, twice the velocity, and hence four times as much kinetic energy as in the CM.) Now, a factor of 4 is some benefit, to be sure, but the *relativistic* gain can be greater by far. Colliding electrons with a laboratory kinetic energy of $1\,\unit{\mathrm{GeV}}$, for example, would have a *relative* kinetic energy of $4000\,\unit{\mathrm{GeV}}$!
