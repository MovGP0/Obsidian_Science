> [!TLDR]
> Maxwell's equations give a massless photon wave equation with two transverse polarization states.

<!-- PDF page 233; printed page 225 -->

In classical electrodynamics the electric and magnetic fields ($\electric{\mathbf E}$ and $\magnetic{\mathbf B}$) set up by a charge density $\electric{\rho}$ and a current density $\electric{\mathbf J}$ are determined by Maxwell's equations:[^ep-7-4-classical]

$$
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
\text{(i)}\quad\nabla\cdot\electric{\mathbf E}&=4\pi\electric{\rho}
&\text{(iii)}\quad\nabla\cdot\magnetic{\mathbf B}&=0\\
\text{(ii)}\quad\nabla\times\electric{\mathbf E}
+\frac{1}{\mechanical{c}}\frac{\partial\magnetic{\mathbf B}}{\partial\mechanical{t}}&=0
&\text{(iv)}\quad\nabla\times\magnetic{\mathbf B}
-\frac{1}{\mechanical{c}}\frac{\partial\electric{\mathbf E}}{\partial\mechanical{t}}&=\frac{4\pi}{\mechanical{c}}\electric{\mathbf J}
\end{aligned}
\right\}
\tag{7.70}
$$

In relativistic notation, $\electric{\mathbf E}$ and $\magnetic{\mathbf B}$ together form an antisymmetric second-rank tensor, the “field strength tensor,” $F^{\mu\nu}$:

$$
F^{\mu\nu}=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&-\electric{E_x}&-\electric{E_y}&-\electric{E_z}\\
\electric{E_x}&0&-\magnetic{B_z}&\magnetic{B_y}\\
\electric{E_y}&\magnetic{B_z}&0&-\magnetic{B_x}\\
\electric{E_z}&-\magnetic{B_y}&\magnetic{B_x}&0
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{7.71}
$$

(that is, $F^{01}=\electric{E_x}$, $F^{12}=-\magnetic{B_z}$, etc.), while $\electric{\rho}$ and $\electric{\mathbf J}$ constitute a four-vector:

$$
\electric{J^\mu}=(\mechanical{c}\electric{\rho},\electric{\mathbf J}).
\tag{7.72}
$$

The inhomogeneous Maxwell equations [(i) and (iv)] can now be written more neatly (Problem 7.18)

$$
\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=\frac{4\pi}{\mechanical{c}}\electric{J^\nu}.
\tag{7.73}
$$

<!-- PDF page 234; printed page 226 -->

From the antisymmetry of $F^{\mu\nu}$ (that is: $F^{\mu\nu}=-F^{\nu\mu}$) it follows (Problem 7.18) that $\electric{J^\mu}$ is divergenceless:

$$
\partial_\mu\electric{J^\mu}=0.
\tag{7.74}
$$

Or, in three-vector notation, $\nabla\cdot\electric{\mathbf J}=-\partial\electric{\rho}/\partial\mechanical{t}$; this is the “continuity equation,” expressing local conservation of charge (Problem 7.19).

As for the homogeneous Maxwell equations, (iii) is equivalent to the statement that $\magnetic{\mathbf B}$ can be written as the curl of a vector potential, $\magnetic{\mathbf A}$:

$$
\magnetic{\mathbf B}=\nabla\times\magnetic{\mathbf A}.
\tag{7.75}
$$

With this, (ii) becomes

$$
\nabla\times\left(\electric{\mathbf E}+\frac{1}{\mechanical{c}}
\frac{\partial\magnetic{\mathbf A}}{\partial\mechanical{t}}\right)=0.
\tag{7.76}
$$

which is equivalent to the statement that $\electric{\mathbf E}+(1/\mechanical{c})(\partial\magnetic{\mathbf A}/\partial\mechanical{t})$ can be written as the gradient of a scalar potential, $\electric{V}$:

$$
\electric{\mathbf E}=-\nabla\electric{V}-\frac{1}{\mechanical{c}}
\frac{\partial\magnetic{\mathbf A}}{\partial\mechanical{t}}.
\tag{7.77}
$$

In relativistic notation, equations (7.75) and (7.77) become

$$
F^{\mu\nu}=\partial^\mu\magnetic{A^\nu}-\partial^\nu\magnetic{A^\mu}
\tag{7.78}
$$

where

$$
\magnetic{A^\mu}=(\electric{V},\magnetic{\mathbf A}).
\tag{7.79}
$$

In terms of this four-vector potential, the inhomogeneous Maxwell equations (7.73) read:

$$
\partial_\mu\partial^\mu\magnetic{A^\nu}
-\partial^\nu(\partial_\mu\magnetic{A^\mu})
=\frac{4\pi}{\mechanical{c}}\electric{J^\nu}.
\tag{7.80}
$$

In classical electrodynamics the *fields* are the physical entities; the potentials are simply useful mathematical constructs. The virtue of the potential formulation is that it *automatically* takes care of the homogeneous Maxwell equations: given equations (7.75) and (7.77), (ii) and (iii) follow immediately, no matter what $\electric{V}$ and $\magnetic{\mathbf A}$ may be. This leaves us only the inhomogeneous equation (7.80) to worry about. The *defect* of the potential formulation is that $\electric{V}$ and $\magnetic{\mathbf A}$ are not uniquely determined. Indeed, it is clear from equation (7.78) that new potentials

$$
\magnetic{A'_\mu}=\magnetic{A_\mu}+\partial_\mu\lambda.
\tag{7.81}
$$

(where $\lambda$ is any function of position and time) would do just as well, since $\partial^\mu\magnetic{A'^\nu}-\partial^\nu\magnetic{A'^\mu}=\partial^\mu\magnetic{A^\nu}-\partial^\nu\magnetic{A^\mu}$. Such a change of potentials, which has no effect on the fields, is called a *gauge transformation*. We can exploit this gauge freedom to impose an extra constraint on the potential:<sup>4</sup>

$$
\partial_\mu\magnetic{A^\mu}=0.
\tag{7.82}
$$

This is called the *Lorentz condition*; with it Maxwell's equations (7.80) simplify still further:

<!-- PDF page 235; printed page 227 -->

$$
\square\magnetic{A^\mu}=\frac{4\pi}{\mechanical{c}}\electric{J^\mu}.
\tag{7.83}
$$

Here $\square\equiv\partial^\mu\partial_\mu=\frac{1}{\mechanical{c}^2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\mechanical{t}^2}-\nabla^2$; it is called the *d'Alembertian operator*.

Even the Lorentz condition, however, does not uniquely specify $\magnetic{A^\mu}$. Further gauge transformations are possible, without disturbing equation (7.82), provided that the gauge function $\lambda$ satisfies the wave equation:

$$
\square\lambda=0.
\tag{7.84}
$$

Unfortunately, there is no clean way to eliminate the residual ambiguity in $\magnetic{A^\mu}$, and one can either (1) live with the indeterminacy, which means carrying along spurious degrees of freedom, or (2) impose an additional constraint, which spoils the manifest Lorentz covariance of the theory. Both approaches have been used in formulating quantum electrodynamics; we shall follow the latter course. In empty space, where $\electric{J^\mu}=0$, we pick (see Problem 7.20)

$$
\magnetic{A^0}=0.
\tag{7.85}
$$

The Lorentz condition then reads

$$
\nabla\cdot\magnetic{\mathbf A}=0.
\tag{7.86}
$$

This choice (the *Coulomb gauge*) is attractively simple, but by selecting one component ($\magnetic{A^0}$) for special treatment, it ties us down to a particular inertial system (either that, or it obliges us to perform a gauge transformation in conjunction with every Lorentz transformation in order to restore the Coulomb gauge condition). In practice, this is very seldom a problem, but it is aesthetically displeasing.

In quantum electrodynamics $\magnetic{A^\mu}$ becomes the wave function of the photon. The free photon satisfies equation (7.83) with $\electric{J^\mu}=0$

$$
\square\magnetic{A^\mu}=0.
\tag{7.87}
$$

which we recognize in this context as the Klein–Gordon equation (7.9) for a massless particle. As in the case of the Dirac equation, we look for plane-wave solutions with momentum $\mechanical{p}=(\energy{E}/\mechanical{c},\mechanical{\mathbf p})$:

$$
\magnetic{A^\mu}(\mechanical{x})=a e^{-(i/\energy{\hbar})\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{x}}
\signal{\epsilon^\mu}(\mechanical{p}).
\tag{7.88}
$$

Here $\signal{\epsilon^\mu}$ is the *polarization vector*—it characterizes the spin of the photon—and $a$ is a normalization factor. Substituting equation (7.88) into equation (7.87), we obtain a constraint on $\mechanical{p^\mu}$:

$$
\mechanical{p^\mu}\mechanical{p_\mu}=0,\qquad
\text{so that}\quad\energy{E}=|\mechanical{\mathbf p}|\mechanical{c}.
\tag{7.89}
$$

which is as it should be for a massless particle.

Meanwhile, $\signal{\epsilon^\mu}$ has four components, but they are not all independent. The Lorentz condition (7.82) requires that

$$
\mechanical{p^\mu}\signal{\epsilon_\mu}=0.
\tag{7.90}
$$

<!-- PDF page 236; printed page 228 -->

Moreover, in the Coulomb gauge we have

$$
\signal{\epsilon^0}=0,\qquad
\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf p}=0.
\tag{7.91}
$$

which is to say that the polarization three-vector ($\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon}$) is perpendicular to the direction of propagation; we say that a free photon is *transversely polarized*. For this reason the Coulomb gauge is also known as the *transverse* gauge. Now, there are two linearly independent three-vectors that are perpendicular to $\mechanical{\mathbf p}$; for example, if $\mechanical{\mathbf p}$ points in the $\mechanical{z}$ direction, we might choose

$$
\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_{(1)}}=(1,0,0),\qquad
\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_{(2)}}=(0,1,0).
\tag{7.92}
$$

Thus instead of *four* independent solutions for a given momentum (too many, for a particle of spin 1), we are left with only *two*. That looks like too *few*: shouldn't the photon have *three* spin states? The answer is *no*: a *massive* particle of spin $\mechanical{s}$ admits $2\mechanical{s}+1$ different spin orientations, but a *massless* particle has only two, regardless of its spin (except for $\mechanical{s}=0$, which has only one). Along its direction of motion it can only have $\mechanical{m_s}=+\mechanical{s}$ or $\mechanical{m_s}=-\mechanical{s}$; its helicity, in other words, can only be $+1$ or $-1$.[^ep-7-4-polarization]

[^ep-7-4-classical]: This section presupposes some familiarity with classical electrodynamics; it is designed to make the description of photons in quantum electrodynamics more plausible, but if you don't understand it, skip directly to Section 7.5. As always, I use Gaussian cgs units.

[^ep-7-4-polarization]: Photon states with $\mechanical{m_s}=\pm1$ correspond to right- and left-circular polarization; the respective polarization vectors are $\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_\pm}=\mp(\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_1}\pm i\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_2})/\sqrt2$. Notice that it is by specifying a particular gauge that we eliminate the nonphysical ($\mechanical{m_s}=0$) solution. If we were to follow a “covariant” approach, in which we avoid imposing the Coulomb gauge condition, longitudinal free photons would be present in the theory. But these “ghosts” decouple from everything else, and they do not affect the final results.
