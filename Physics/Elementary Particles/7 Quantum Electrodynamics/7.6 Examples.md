## Example 7.1 Electron–Muon Scattering

In applying the Feynman rules, we proceed “backward” along each fermion line (Fig. 7.2):

$$
\begin{aligned}
(2\pi)^4\int&
[\signal{\bar u^{(s_3)}}(\mechanical{p_3})(i\electric{g_e}\gamma^\mu)
\signal{u^{(s_1)}}(\mechanical{p_1})]
\frac{-ig_{\mu\nu}}{\mechanical{q}^2}
[\signal{\bar u^{(s_4)}}(\mechanical{p_4})(i\electric{g_e}\gamma^\nu)
\signal{u^{(s_2)}}(\mechanical{p_2})]\\
&\times\delta^4(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3}-\mechanical{q})
\delta^4(\mechanical{p_2}+\mechanical{q}-\mechanical{p_4})\,d^4\mechanical{q}
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that the space-time indices on the photon propagator contract with those of the vertex factors at either end of the photon line. Carrying out the (trivial) $\mechanical{q}$ integration, and dropping the overall delta function, we find

$$
\mathcal M=-\frac{\electric{g_e}^2}{(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3})^2}
[\signal{\bar u^{(s_3)}}(\mechanical{p_3})\gamma^\mu
\signal{u^{(s_1)}}(\mechanical{p_1})]
[\signal{\bar u^{(s_4)}}(\mechanical{p_4})\gamma_\mu
\signal{u^{(s_2)}}(\mechanical{p_2})].
\tag{7.104}
$$

In spite of its complicated appearance, with four spinors and eight $\gamma$ matrices, this is just a *number*, which you can work out once the spins are specified (see Problem 7.24).

## Example 7.2 Electron–Electron Scattering

In this case there is a second diagram, in which the electron that emerges with momentum $\mechanical{p_3}$ and spin $\mechanical{s_3}$ comes from the $\mechanical{p_2}$, $\mechanical{s_2}$ electron, instead of the $\mechanical{p_1}$, $\mechanical{s_1}$ electron (Fig. 7.3). We can obtain this amplitude from equation (7.104) simply by the replacement $\mechanical{p_3},\mechanical{s_3}\leftrightarrow\mechanical{p_4},\mechanical{s_4}$. According to rule 8, the two diagrams are to be *subtracted*, so the total amplitude is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal M={}&-\frac{\electric{g_e}^2}{(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3})^2}
[\signal{\bar u}(3)\gamma^\mu\signal{u}(1)]
[\signal{\bar u}(4)\gamma_\mu\signal{u}(2)]\\
&+\frac{\electric{g_e}^2}{(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_4})^2}
[\signal{\bar u}(4)\gamma^\mu\signal{u}(1)]
[\signal{\bar u}(3)\gamma_\mu\signal{u}(2)].
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.105}
$$

## Example 7.3 Electron–Positron Scattering

Again, there are two diagrams.[^ep-7-6-classical-limit] The first is similar to the electron-muon diagram (Fig. 7.4):

$$
\begin{aligned}
(2\pi)^4\int&
[\signal{\bar u}(3)(i\electric{g_e}\gamma^\mu)\signal{u}(1)]
\frac{-ig_{\mu\nu}}{\mechanical{q}^2}\\
&\times[\signal{\bar v}(2)(i\electric{g_e}\gamma^\nu)\signal{v}(4)]
\delta^4(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3}-\mechanical{q})
\delta^4(\mechanical{p_2}+\mechanical{q}-\mechanical{p_4})\,d^4\mechanical{q}
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that “proceeding backwards” along an *antiparticle* line means working *forward* in time: the order is always *adjoint spinor/gamma matrix/spinor*. The amplitude for this diagram is thus

$$
\mathcal M_1=-\frac{\electric{g_e}^2}{(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3})^2}
[\signal{\bar u}(3)\gamma^\mu\signal{u}(1)]
[\signal{\bar v}(2)\gamma_\mu\signal{v}(4)].
\tag{7.106}
$$

The other diagram represents virtual annihilation of the electron and positron, followed by pair production (Fig. 7.5):

$$
\begin{aligned}
(2\pi)^4\int&
[\signal{\bar u}(3)(i\electric{g_e}\gamma^\mu)\signal{v}(4)]
\frac{-ig_{\mu\nu}}{\mechanical{q}^2}\\
&\times[\signal{\bar v}(2)(i\electric{g_e}\gamma^\nu)\signal{u}(1)]
\delta^4(\mechanical{q}-\mechanical{p_3}-\mechanical{p_4})
\delta^4(\mechanical{p_1}+\mechanical{p_2}-\mechanical{q})\,d^4\mechanical{q}
\end{aligned}
$$

The amplitude for this diagram is therefore

$$
\mathcal M_2=-\frac{\electric{g_e}^2}{(\mechanical{p_1}+\mechanical{p_2})^2}
[\signal{\bar u}(3)\gamma^\mu\signal{v}(4)]
[\signal{\bar v}(2)\gamma_\mu\signal{u}(1)].
\tag{7.107}
$$

![[elementary-particles-fig-7-4-scattering.svg|**Fig. 7.4** Electron–positron scattering.]]

![[elementary-particles-fig-7-5-bhabha-annihilation.svg|**Fig. 7.5** Second diagram contributing to electron–positron scattering.]]

Now, do we *add* these diagrams, or *subtract* them? Interchanging the incoming positron and the outgoing electron in the second diagram (Fig. 7.5), and then redrawing it in a more customary configuration

![[elementary-particles-qed-bhabha-antisymmetrization.svg|Interchanged external lines redrawn in the usual exchange configuration.]]

we recover the first diagram (Fig. 7.4). According to rule 8, then, we need a minus sign:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal M={}&-\frac{\electric{g_e}^2}{(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3})^2}
[\signal{\bar u}(3)\gamma^\mu\signal{u}(1)]
[\signal{\bar v}(2)\gamma_\mu\signal{v}(4)]\\
&+\frac{\electric{g_e}^2}{(\mechanical{p_1}+\mechanical{p_2})^2}
[\signal{\bar u}(3)\gamma^\mu\signal{v}(4)]
[\signal{\bar v}(2)\gamma_\mu\signal{u}(1)].
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.108}
$$

## Example 7.4 Compton Scattering

For an example involving the electron propagator and photon polarization, consider the case of Compton scattering, $\gamma+e\to\gamma+e$. Again there are two diagrams, but they do *not* differ by the interchange of fermions, and the amplitudes *add*. The first diagram (Fig. 7.6) yields

$$
\begin{aligned}
(2\pi)^4\int&\signal{\epsilon_\mu}(2)
\left[\signal{\bar u}(4)(i\electric{g_e}\gamma^\mu)
\frac{i(\not{\mechanical{q}}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})}
{(\mechanical{q}^2-\mechanical{m}^2\mechanical{c}^2)}
(i\electric{g_e}\gamma^\nu)\signal{u}(1)\right]\\
&\times\signal{\epsilon_\nu}(3)^*
\delta^4(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3}-\mechanical{q})
\delta^4(\mechanical{p_2}+\mechanical{q}-\mechanical{p_4})\,d^4\mechanical{q}
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that the space-time index on each photon polarization vector is contracted with the index of the $\gamma$ matrix at the vertex where the photon was created or absorbed. Notice also how the electron propagator fits in as we work our way backward along the fermion line. I have introduced here the convenient shorthand “$a$-slash”:

<!-- PDF page 244; printed page 236 -->

![[elementary-particles-fig-7-6-compton-scattering.svg|**Fig. 7.6** Compton scattering.]]

$$
\not a\equiv a^\mu\gamma_\mu.
\tag{7.109}
$$

Evidently, the amplitude associated with this diagram is[^ep-7-6-slash]

$$
\mathcal M_1=
\frac{\electric{g_e}^2}{(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3})^2-\mechanical{m}^2\mechanical{c}^2}
[\signal{\bar u}(4)\not{\signal{\epsilon}}(2)
(\not{\mechanical{p_1}}-\not{\mechanical{p_3}}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})
\not{\signal{\epsilon}}(3)^*\signal{u}(1)].
\tag{7.110}
$$

Meanwhile, the second diagram (Fig. 7.7) yields

$$
\mathcal M_2=
\frac{\electric{g_e}^2}{(\mechanical{p_1}+\mechanical{p_2})^2-\mechanical{m}^2\mechanical{c}^2}
[\signal{\bar u}(4)\not{\signal{\epsilon}}(3)^*
(\not{\mechanical{p_1}}+\not{\mechanical{p_2}}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})
\not{\signal{\epsilon}}(2)\signal{u}(1)].
\tag{7.111}
$$

and the total amplitude is $\mathcal M=\mathcal M_1+\mathcal M_2$.

[^ep-7-6-point-charges]: It doesn't have to be an $e$ and a $\mu$, of course. Any spin-$\frac12$ point charges would do ($e$ and $\tau$, for instance, or $\mu$ and $\tau$, or electron and quark, etc.), as long as you put in the correct masses and charges. As a matter of fact, most books use electron-*proton* scattering as the canonical example, but that is actually a rather *inappropriate* choice, since the proton is a composite structure, not a point particle. Still, to the extent that the internal structure of the proton can be ignored, it is not a bad approximation (it is rather like treating the sun as a point mass in the theory of the solar system). In the regime where the “muon” is much heavier than the “electron,” we have *Mott scattering*; if, moreover, the “electron” is nonrelativistic, it reduces to *Rutherford scattering*, and actually reproduces exactly the same formula for the cross section as Rutherford obtained using classical mechanics.

[^ep-7-6-classical-limit]: The fact that there are *two* diagrams for electron-electron and electron-positron scattering, but only *one* for electron-muon scattering, would appear offhand to be inconsistent with the classical limit. After all, Coulomb's Law says that the force of attraction or repulsion between two particles depends only on their charges, not on whether they happen to be identical (or antiparticles of one another). In the nonrelativistic limit, then, we should get the same answer whether we use the electron-muon formula or the electron-electron formula. The amplitudes, to be sure, are not the same, but the cross-section formula (6.34) carries a factor of $S$, which is $\frac12$ for electron-electron scattering and 1 for electron-muon scattering. [For electron-positron scattering, $S=1$, but the second amplitude (7.107) is smaller than the first (7.106) by a factor $(\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c})^2$, so that in the nonrelativistic limit only $\mathcal M_1$ contributes.]

[^ep-7-6-slash]: Here and below, $\not{\signal{\epsilon}}^*$ means $\gamma^\mu(\signal{\epsilon_\mu}^*)$; the $\gamma$ matrix is *not* conjugated.

| $\mechanical{m},\mechanical{M}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{g}}$ | Particle masses | The electron and heavy scattering-particle masses. |
| $\mechanical{v}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}}$ | Electron speed | The speed used in the nonrelativistic limit. |
