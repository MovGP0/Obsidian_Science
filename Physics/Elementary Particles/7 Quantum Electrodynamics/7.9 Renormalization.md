> [!TLDR]
> Vacuum polarization changes the photon propagator and gives a renormalized coupling that depends on momentum transfer.

<!-- PDF page 254; printed page 246 -->

In Section 7.6 we considered “electron-muon” scattering, described in lowest order by the diagram

![[elementary-particles-qed-renormalization-tree.svg|Lowest-order electron–muon scattering diagram.]]

and by the corresponding amplitude

$$
\mathcal M=-\electric{g_e}^2
[\signal{\bar u}(\mechanical{p_3})\gamma^\mu\signal{u}(\mechanical{p_1})]
\frac{g_{\mu\nu}}{\mechanical{q}^2}
[\signal{\bar u}(\mechanical{p_4})\gamma^\nu\signal{u}(\mechanical{p_2})]
\tag{7.170}
$$

with

$$
\mechanical{q}=\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3}
\tag{7.171}
$$

There are a number of fourth-order corrections, of which perhaps the most interesting is the “vacuum polarization” diagram

![[elementary-particles-qed-renormalization-vacuum-polarization.svg|Electron–positron loop in the exchanged photon line.]]

Here the virtual photon momentarily splits into an electron-positron pair, leading (as we saw qualitatively in Chapter 2) to a modification in the effective charge of the electron. My purpose now is to show how this works out quantitatively.

The amplitude for this diagram is (Problem 7.38)

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal M={}&\frac{-i\electric{g_e}^4}{\mechanical{q}^4}
[\signal{\bar u}(\mechanical{p_3})\gamma^\mu\signal{u}(\mechanical{p_1})]\\
&\times\left\{\int\frac{d^4\mechanical{k}}{(2\pi)^4}
\frac{\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma_\mu(\not{\mechanical{k}}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})
\gamma_\nu(\not{\mechanical{q}}-\not{\mechanical{k}}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})]}
{(\mechanical{k}^2-\mechanical{m}^2\mechanical{c}^2)
((\mechanical{q}-\mechanical{k})^2-\mechanical{m}^2\mechanical{c}^2)}
\right\}
[\signal{\bar u}(\mechanical{p_4})\gamma^\nu\signal{u}(\mechanical{p_2})].
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.172}
$$

<!-- PDF page 255; printed page 247 -->

Its inclusion amounts to a modification of the photon propagator:

$$
\frac{g_{\mu\nu}}{\mechanical{q}^2}
\longrightarrow\frac{g_{\mu\nu}}{\mechanical{q}^2}
-\frac{i}{\mechanical{q}^4}I_{\mu\nu}
\tag{7.173}
$$

where [comparing (7.170) and (7.172)]:

$$
I_{\mu\nu}=-\electric{g_e}^2\int\frac{d^4\mechanical{k}}{(2\pi)^4}
\frac{\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma_\mu(\not{\mechanical{k}}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})
\gamma_\nu(\not{\mechanical{q}}-\not{\mechanical{k}}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})]}
{(\mechanical{k}^2-\mechanical{m}^2\mechanical{c}^2)
((\mechanical{q}-\mechanical{k})^2-\mechanical{m}^2\mechanical{c}^2)}
\tag{7.174}
$$

Unfortunately, this integral is divergent. Naively, it should go like

$$
\int|\mechanical{k}|^3\,d|\mechanical{k}|
\frac{|\mechanical{k}|^2}{|\mechanical{k}|^4}
=\int|\mechanical{k}|\,d\mechanical{k}=|\mechanical{k}|^2,
\qquad\text{as }|\mechanical{k}|\to\infty
\tag{7.175}
$$

(That is, it should be “quadratically divergent.”) In actual fact, because of cancellations in the algebra, it only goes like $\ln|\mechanical{k}|$ (it is “logarithmically divergent”). But never mind—either way, it blows up. We encountered a similar problem in Chapter 6: it seems to be characteristic of closed-loop diagrams in the Feynman calculus. Once again, the strategy will be to absorb the infinities into “renormalized” masses and coupling constants.

The integral (7.174) carries two space-time indices; once we have integrated over $\mechanical{k}$, the only four-vector left is $\mechanical{q^\mu}$, so $I_{\mu\nu}$ must have the general form $g_{\mu\nu}(\quad)+\mechanical{q_\mu}\mechanical{q_\nu}(\quad)$, where the parentheses contain some functions of $\mechanical{q}^2$. We write it thus:<sup>7</sup>

$$
I_{\mu\nu}=-ig_{\mu\nu}\mechanical{q}^2I(\mechanical{q}^2)
+\mechanical{q_\mu}\mechanical{q_\nu}J(\mechanical{q}^2)
\tag{7.176}
$$

The second term contributes nothing to $\mathcal M$, since the $\mechanical{q_\mu}$ contracts with $\gamma^\mu$ in equation (7.172), giving

$$
[\signal{\bar u}(\mechanical{p_3})\not{\mechanical{q}}\signal{u}(\mechanical{p_1})]
=\signal{\bar u}(\mechanical{p_3})
(\not{\mechanical{p_1}}-\not{\mechanical{p_3}})\signal{u}(\mechanical{p_1})
$$

while, from equation (7.94)

$$
\not{\mechanical{p_1}}\signal{u}(\mechanical{p_1})=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c},
\qquad
\signal{\bar u}(\mechanical{p_3})\not{\mechanical{p_3}}
=\signal{u}(\mechanical{p_3})\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}
$$

and hence

$$
[\signal{\bar u}(\mechanical{p_3})\not{\mechanical{q}}\signal{u}(\mechanical{p_1})]=0
\tag{7.177}
$$

So we'll forget about the second term in equation (7.176). As for the first term, appropriate massaging of the integral (7.174) reduces it to the form (Problem 7.39)

$$
I(\mechanical{q}^2)=\frac{\electric{g_e}^2}{12\pi^2}
\left\{\int_{\mechanical{m}^2}^\infty\frac{dx}{x}
-6\int_0^1 z(1-z)
\ln\left(1-\frac{\mechanical{q}^2}{\mechanical{m}^2\mechanical{c}^2}z(1-z)\right)\,dz\right\}
\tag{7.178}
$$

The first integral clearly isolates the logarithmic divergence. To handle it, we temporarily impose a cutoff $\mechanical{M}$ (not to be confused with the mass of the muon), which we shall send to infinity at the end of the calculation:

$$
\int_{\mechanical{m}^2}^\infty\frac{dx}{x}
\longrightarrow\int_{\mechanical{m}^2}^{\mechanical{M}^2}\frac{dx}{x}
=\ln\frac{\mechanical{M}^2}{\mechanical{m}^2}
\tag{7.179}
$$

<!-- PDF page 256; printed page 248 -->

The second integral

$$
f(x)\equiv6\int_0^1 z(1-z)\ln(1+xz(1-z))\,dz
\tag{7.180}
$$

is perfectly finite. It's irritating that it has to be left in this form; the integral just can't be reduced to elementary functions. It is easy enough, however, to evaluate numerically (Fig. 7.11), and the limiting expressions for large and small $x$ are simple:

$$
f(x)\cong
\begin{cases}
x/5&(x\ll1)\\
\ln x&(x\gg1)
\end{cases}
\tag{7.181}
$$

At any rate

$$
I(\mechanical{q}^2)=\frac{\electric{g_e}^2}{12\pi^2}
\left\{\ln\left(\frac{\mechanical{M}^2}{\mechanical{m}^2}\right)
-f\left(\frac{-\mechanical{q}^2}{\mechanical{m}^2\mechanical{c}^2}\right)\right\}
\tag{7.182}
$$

Notice that $\mechanical{q}^2$ is *negative*, here. If the incident electron's three-momentum in the *CM* is $\mechanical{\mathbf p}$, and the scattering angle is $\mechanical{\theta}$, then (Problem 7.40)

$$
\mechanical{q}^2=-4\mechanical{\mathbf p}^2\sin^2\frac{\mechanical{\theta}}2
\tag{7.183}
$$

Thus $-\mechanical{q}^2/\mechanical{m}^2\mechanical{c}^2\sim\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2$, and the limiting cases in equation (7.181) correspond to nonrelativistic and ultrarelativistic scattering, respectively.

The amplitude for electron-muon scattering, including vacuum polarization, is therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal M={}&-\electric{g_e}^2
[\signal{\bar u}(\mechanical{p_3})\gamma^\mu\signal{u}(\mechanical{p_1})]
\frac{g_{\mu\nu}}{\mechanical{q}^2}
\left\{1-\frac{\electric{g_e}^2}{12\pi^2}
\left[\ln\left(\frac{\mechanical{M}^2}{\mechanical{m}^2}\right)
-f\left(\frac{-\mechanical{q}^2}{\mechanical{m}^2\mechanical{c}^2}\right)\right]\right\}\\
&\times[\signal{\bar u}(\mechanical{p_4})\gamma^\nu\signal{u}(\mechanical{p_2})].
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.184}
$$

Now comes the critical step, in which we “sop up” the infinity (contained for the moment in the cutoff $\mechanical{M}$) by introducing the “renormalized” coupling constant

$$
\electric{g_R}\equiv\electric{g_e}
\sqrt{1-\frac{\electric{g_e}^2}{12\pi^2}
\ln\left(\frac{\mechanical{M}^2}{\mechanical{m}^2}\right)}
\tag{7.185}
$$

Rewriting (7.184) in terms of $\electric{g_R}$, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal M={}&-\electric{g_R}^2
[\signal{\bar u}(\mechanical{p_3})\gamma^\mu\signal{u}(\mechanical{p_1})]
\frac{g_{\mu\nu}}{\mechanical{q}^2}
\left\{1+\frac{\electric{g_R}^2}{12\pi^2}
f\left(\frac{-\mechanical{q}^2}{\mechanical{m}^2\mechanical{c}^2}\right)\right\}
[\signal{\bar u}(\mechanical{p_4})\gamma^\nu\signal{u}(\mechanical{p_2})].
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.186}
$$

![[elementary-particles-fig-7-11-source-plot.png|**Fig. 7.11** Graph of f(x) &#91;eq. (7.180)&#93;. Solid line is the numerical result; dashed line below is ln x &#91;which approximates f(x) at large x&#93;; straight line above is x/5 &#91;which approximates f(x) at small x&#93;.]]

[Equation (7.184) is only valid to order $\electric{g_e}^4$ anyway, so it doesn't matter whether we use $\electric{g_e}$ or $\electric{g_R}$ within the curly brackets.] There are two important things to notice about this result:

1. The infinities are gone: there is no $\mechanical{M}$ in equation (7.186). All reference to the cutoff has been absorbed into the coupling constant. To be sure, everything is now written in terms of $\electric{g_R}$, instead of $\electric{g_e}$. But that's all to the good: $\electric{g_R}$, not $\electric{g_e}$, is what we actually measure in the laboratory (in Heaviside–Lorentz units it is the charge of the electron—or muon—and we determine it experimentally as the coefficient of attraction or repulsion between two such particles). If, in our theoretical analysis, we look only at “tree level” (lowest-order) diagrams, we are led to suppose that the physical charge is the same as the “bare” coupling constant, $\electric{g_e}$. But as soon as we include higher-order effects we find that it is really $\electric{g_R}$, not $\electric{g_e}$, that corresponds to the measured electric charge. Does this mean that our earlier results are all wrong? No. What it means is that by naively interpreting $\electric{g_e}$ as the physical electric charge we were unwittingly taking into account the divergent part of the higher-order diagrams.

2. There remains the *finite* correction term, and here the important thing to notice is that it *depends on* $\mechanical{q}^2$. We can absorb this, too, into the coupling constant, but the “constant” is now a function of $\mechanical{q}^2$; we call it a “running” coupling constant:

$$
\electric{g_R}(\mechanical{q}^2)=\electric{g_R}(0)
\sqrt{1+\frac{\electric{g_R}(0)^2}{12\pi^2}
f\left(\frac{-\mechanical{q}^2}{\mechanical{m}^2\mechanical{c}^2}\right)}
\tag{7.187}
$$

or, in terms of the fine structure “constant” ($\electric{g_e}=\sqrt{4\pi\electric{\alpha}}$):

$$
\electric{\alpha}(\mechanical{q}^2)=\electric{\alpha}(0)
\left\{1+\frac{\electric{\alpha}(0)}{3\pi}
f\left(\frac{-\mechanical{q}^2}{\mechanical{m}^2\mechanical{c}^2}\right)\right\}
\tag{7.188}
$$

The effective charge of the electron (and the muon), then, depends on the momentum transferred in the collision. Higher momentum transfer means closer approach, so another way of saying it is that the effective charge of each particle depends on how far apart they are. This is a consequence of vacuum polarization, which “screens” each charge. We now have an explicit formula for what was, in Chapter 2, a purely qualitative description. How come Millikan and Rutherford, or even Coulomb, never noticed this effect? If the electron's charge is not a constant, why doesn't this foul up everything from electronics to chemistry? The answer is that the variation is extremely slight, in nonrelativistic situations. Even in a head-on collision at $\frac1{10}\mechanical{c}$, the correction term in equation (7.188) is only about $6\times10^{-6}$ (Problem 7.41). For most purposes, therefore, $\electric{\alpha}(0)=\frac1{137}$ will do just fine. Nevertheless, the second term in (7.188) makes a detectable contribution to the Lamb shift, for example.<sup>8</sup> Moreover, we shall encounter the same problem in quantum chromodynamics, where (because of quark confinement) the short-distance, relativistic regime is the case of interest.

We have concentrated on one particular fourth-order process (vacuum polarization), but there are, of course, several others. There are the “ladder-diagrams”:

<!-- PDF page 258; printed page 250 -->

![[elementary-particles-qed-renormalization-ladders.svg|Finite ladder and crossed-ladder diagrams.]]

These are finite and present no particular problems. But there are also three divergent graphs:

![[elementary-particles-qed-renormalization-divergent-corrections.svg|Two electron mass corrections and an electron vertex correction.]]

(and of course three more in which the extra virtual photon couples to the muon). The first two renormalize the electron's mass; the third modifies its magnetic moment. In addition, all three, considered separately, contribute to the renormalization of the electron's charge. Luckily, the latter contributions cancel one another, so that equation (7.185) remains valid. (I say “luckily,” for these corrections depend on the mass of the particle to which the virtual photon line attaches, and if they did *not* cancel we would have a different renormalization for the muon than for the electron. The Ward identity (the official name for this cancellation) guarantees that renormalization preserves the equality of electric charges, irrespective of the mass of the carrier).[^ep-7-9-muon-bubble] And then, there are even higher-order diagrams, such as

![[elementary-particles-qed-renormalization-higher-orders.svg|Two and three vacuum-polarization insertions, followed by further terms.]]

These introduce further terms in equation (7.188), of order $\electric{\alpha}^2$, $\electric{\alpha}^3$, and so on, but we shall not pursue the matter here, for the essential *ideas* are now on the table.

[^ep-7-9-muon-bubble]: Of course, we can put a *muon* bubble onto a photon line, just as we did with the *electron* in equation (7.172). But this will modify the electron and muon charge (and for that matter the tau and the quarks) by the same amount. The electron insertion is the dominant correction, simply because it is the lightest charged particle.
