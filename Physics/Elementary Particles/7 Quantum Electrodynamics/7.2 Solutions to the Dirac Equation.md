> [!TLDR]
> Rest and plane-wave Dirac solutions form electron and positron spinors with energy-dependent components and a stated normalization.

<!-- PDF page 224; printed page 216 -->

Let's now look for simple solutions to the Dirac equation. Suppose first that $\signal{\psi}$ is *independent of position*:

<!-- PDF page 225; printed page 217 -->

$$
\frac{\partial\signal{\psi}}{\partial\mechanical{x}}
=\frac{\partial\signal{\psi}}{\partial\mechanical{y}}
=\frac{\partial\signal{\psi}}{\partial\mechanical{z}}=0.
\tag{7.22}
$$

In view of equation (7.5), this describes a state with zero momentum ($\mechanical{\mathbf p}=\mathbf0$). The Dirac equation (7.20) reduces to

$$
\frac{i\energy{\hbar}}{\mechanical{c}}\gamma^0\frac{\partial\signal{\psi}}{\partial\mechanical{t}}
-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}\signal{\psi}=0
\tag{7.23}
$$

or

$$
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}\partial\signal{\psi_A}/\partial\mechanical{t}\\\partial\signal{\psi_B}/\partial\mechanical{t}\end{pmatrix}
=-i\frac{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}{\energy{\hbar}}
\begin{pmatrix}\signal{\psi_A}\\\signal{\psi_B}\end{pmatrix}
\tag{7.24}
$$

where

$$
\signal{\psi_A}=\begin{pmatrix}\signal{\psi_1}\\\signal{\psi_2}\end{pmatrix}
\tag{7.25}
$$

carries the upper two components, and

$$
\signal{\psi_B}=\begin{pmatrix}\signal{\psi_3}\\\signal{\psi_4}\end{pmatrix}
\tag{7.26}
$$

carries the lower two. Thus

$$
\frac{\partial\signal{\psi_A}}{\partial\mechanical{t}}
=-i\left(\frac{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}{\energy{\hbar}}\right)\signal{\psi_A},\qquad
-\frac{\partial\signal{\psi_B}}{\partial\mechanical{t}}
=-i\left(\frac{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}{\energy{\hbar}}\right)\signal{\psi_B}
\tag{7.27}
$$

and the solutions are

$$
\signal{\psi_A}(\mechanical{t})=e^{-i(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}/\energy{\hbar})\mechanical{t}}\signal{\psi_A}(0),\qquad
\signal{\psi_B}(\mechanical{t})=e^{+i(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}/\energy{\hbar})\mechanical{t}}\signal{\psi_B}(0).
\tag{7.28}
$$

Referring to equation (5.10) we recognize the factor

$$
e^{-i\energy{E}\mechanical{t}/\energy{\hbar}}
\tag{7.29}
$$

as the characteristic time dependence of a quantum state with energy $\energy{E}$. For a particle at rest, $\energy{E}=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}$, so $\signal{\psi_A}$ is exactly what we should have expected, in the case $\mechanical{\mathbf p}=\mathbf0$. But what about $\signal{\psi_B}$? It ostensibly represents a state with *negative energy* ($\energy{E}=-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}$). This is the famous disaster I mentioned back in Chapter 1, which Dirac at first tried to avoid by postulating an unseen infinite “sea” of negative energy particles, which fill up all those unwanted states.[^ep-7-2-complete] Instead, we now interpret the “negative energy” solutions as representing *antiparticles* with *positive* energy. Thus $\signal{\psi_A}$ describes electrons (for example), whereas $\signal{\psi_B}$ describes *positrons*. Each is a two-component spinor, just right for a system of spin $\tfrac{1}{2}$. In conclusion then, the Dirac equation with $\mechanical{\mathbf p}=\mathbf0$ admits four independent solutions (ignoring normalization factors, for the moment):

<!-- PDF page 226; printed page 218 -->

$$
\begin{aligned}
\signal{\psi^{(1)}}&=e^{-i(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}/\energy{\hbar})\mechanical{t}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\\0\end{pmatrix},
&\signal{\psi^{(2)}}&=e^{-i(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}/\energy{\hbar})\mechanical{t}}\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix},\\
\signal{\psi^{(3)}}&=e^{+i(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}/\energy{\hbar})\mechanical{t}}\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix},
&\signal{\psi^{(4)}}&=e^{+i(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}/\energy{\hbar})\mechanical{t}}\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix},
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.30}
$$

describing, respectively, an electron with spin up, an electron with spin down, a positron with spin up, and a positron with spin down.

We look next for *plane-wave* solutions, of the form

$$
\signal{\psi}(\mechanical{\mathbf r},\mechanical{t})
=a e^{-i/\energy{\hbar}(\energy{E}\mechanical{t}-\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf r})}
\signal{u}(\energy{E},\mechanical{\mathbf p})
\tag{7.31}
$$

or, in tidier notation,

$$
\signal{\psi}(\mechanical{x})=a e^{-(i/\energy{\hbar})\mechanical{x}\cdot\mechanical{p}}\signal{u}(\mechanical{p}).
\tag{7.32}
$$

(Here $a$ is a normalization constant, irrelevant to our present purpose, but necessary later to keep the units consistent.) We're hoping to find a bispinor $\signal{u}(\mechanical{p})$ such that $\signal{\psi}(\mechanical{x})$ satisfies the Dirac equation. (At this stage $\mechanical{p}\equiv(\energy{E}/\mechanical{c},\mechanical{\mathbf p})$ is simply a set of four arbitrary parameters, but since they turn out to represent energy and momentum it seems simplest to assign them the appropriate letters right from the start.) Because the $\mechanical{x}$ dependence is confined to the exponent

$$
\partial_\mu\signal{\psi}
=-\frac{i}{\energy{\hbar}}\mechanical{p_\mu}a
e^{-(i/\energy{\hbar})\mechanical{x^\nu}\mechanical{p_\nu}}\signal{u}.
\tag{7.33}
$$

Putting this into the Dirac equation (7.20), we get

$$
\gamma^\mu\mechanical{p_\mu}a e^{-(i/\energy{\hbar})\mechanical{x}\cdot\mechanical{p}}\signal{u}
-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}a e^{-(i/\energy{\hbar})\mechanical{x}\cdot\mechanical{p}}\signal{u}=0
$$

or

$$
(\gamma^\mu\mechanical{p_\mu}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})\signal{u}=0.
\tag{7.34}
$$

This is known as the “momentum space Dirac equation”. Notice that it is purely algebraic, with no derivatives. If $\signal{u}$ satisfies equation (7.34), then $\signal{\psi}$ (7.32) satisfies the Dirac equation (7.20).

Now

$$
\begin{aligned}
\gamma^\mu\mechanical{p_\mu}
&=\gamma^0\mechanical{p^0}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf p}\\
&=\frac{\energy{E}}{\mechanical{c}}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}
-\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}0&\boldsymbol{\sigma}\\-\boldsymbol{\sigma}&0\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}
\energy{E}/\mechanical{c}&-\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\\
\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}&-\energy{E}/\mechanical{c}
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.35}
$$

Thus

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\gamma^\mu\mechanical{p_\mu}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})\signal{u}
&=\begin{pmatrix}
(\energy{E}/\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})&-\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\\
\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}&(-\energy{E}/\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\signal{u_A}\\\signal{u_B}\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}
(\energy{E}/\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})\signal{u_A}
-\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\signal{u_B}\\
\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}\signal{u_A}
-(\energy{E}/\mechanical{c}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})\signal{u_B}
\end{pmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

<!-- PDF page 227; printed page 219 -->

where, as before, the subscript $A$ denotes the upper two components, and $B$ stands for the lower two. In order to satisfy equation (7.34), then, we must have

$$
\signal{u_A}=\frac{\mechanical{c}}{\energy{E}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}
(\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})\signal{u_B},\qquad
\signal{u_B}=\frac{\mechanical{c}}{\energy{E}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}
(\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})\signal{u_A}.
\tag{7.36}
$$

Substituting the second of these into the first gives

$$
\signal{u_A}=\frac{\mechanical{c}^{2}}{\energy{E}^{2}-\mechanical{m}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{4}}
(\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})^{2}\signal{u_A}.
\tag{7.37}
$$

But

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}
&=\mechanical{p_x}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}
+\mechanical{p_y}\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}
+\mechanical{p_z}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}
\mechanical{p_z}&(\mechanical{p_x}-i\mechanical{p_y})\\
(\mechanical{p_x}+i\mechanical{p_y})&-\mechanical{p_z}
\end{pmatrix}
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.38}
$$

so

$$
(\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})^{2}
=\begin{pmatrix}
\mechanical{p_z}^{2}+(\mechanical{p_x}-i\mechanical{p_y})(\mechanical{p_x}+i\mechanical{p_y})&
\mechanical{p_z}(\mechanical{p_x}-i\mechanical{p_y})-\mechanical{p_z}(\mechanical{p_x}-i\mechanical{p_y})\\
\mechanical{p_z}(\mechanical{p_x}+i\mechanical{p_y})-\mechanical{p_z}(\mechanical{p_x}+i\mechanical{p_y})&
(\mechanical{p_x}+i\mechanical{p_y})(\mechanical{p_x}-i\mechanical{p_y})+\mechanical{p_z}^{2}
\end{pmatrix}
=\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{2}\mathbf1
\tag{7.39}
$$

where $\mathbf1$ is the $2\times2$ unit matrix (written in explicitly, just this once). Thus

$$
\signal{u_A}=\frac{\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}}{\energy{E}^{2}-\mechanical{m}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{4}}\signal{u_A}
\tag{7.40}
$$

and hence[^ep-7-2-nonzero]

$$
\energy{E}^{2}-\mechanical{m}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{4}
=\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}.
\tag{7.41}
$$

That is, in order to satisfy the Dirac equation, $\energy{E}$ and $\mechanical{\mathbf p}$ [in eq. (7.31)] must obey the usual relativistic energy-momentum relation. That's hardly *surprising*, but it is interesting to see how the Dirac equation enforces this requirement. As an equation for $\energy{E}$, (7.41) admits two solutions:

$$
\energy{E}=\pm\sqrt{\mechanical{m}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{4}+\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}}.
\tag{7.42}
$$

The positive root is associated with particle states, and the negative root with antiparticle states.

Returning to equation (7.36), and using (7.38), it is a simple matter to construct four independent solutions to the Dirac equation (ignoring normalization factors for a moment):

<!-- PDF page 228; printed page 220 -->

$$
\begin{aligned}
(1)\quad&\text{Pick }\signal{u_A}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},
&&\text{then }\signal{u_B}
=\frac{\mechanical{c}}{\energy{E}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}
(\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}
=\frac{\mechanical{c}}{\energy{E}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}
\begin{pmatrix}\mechanical{p_z}\\\mechanical{p_x}+i\mechanical{p_y}\end{pmatrix};\\
(2)\quad&\text{Pick }\signal{u_A}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix},
&&\text{then }\signal{u_B}
=\frac{\mechanical{c}}{\energy{E}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}
(\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}
=\frac{\mechanical{c}}{\energy{E}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}
\begin{pmatrix}\mechanical{p_x}-i\mechanical{p_y}\\-\mechanical{p_z}\end{pmatrix};\\
(3)\quad&\text{Pick }\signal{u_B}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix},
&&\text{then }\signal{u_A}
=\frac{\mechanical{c}}{\energy{E}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}
(\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}
=\frac{\mechanical{c}}{\energy{E}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}
\begin{pmatrix}\mechanical{p_z}\\\mechanical{p_x}+i\mechanical{p_y}\end{pmatrix};\\
(4)\quad&\text{Pick }\signal{u_B}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix},
&&\text{then }\signal{u_A}
=\frac{\mechanical{c}}{\energy{E}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}
(\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}
=\frac{\mechanical{c}}{\energy{E}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}
\begin{pmatrix}\mechanical{p_x}-i\mechanical{p_y}\\-\mechanical{p_z}\end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.43}
$$

For (1) and (2) we must use the plus sign in equation (7.42), otherwise $\signal{u_B}$ blows up as $\mechanical{\mathbf p}\to\mathbf0$; these are evidently *particle* solutions. For (3) and (4) we are obliged to use the minus sign; these are *antiparticle* states. It is convenient to *normalize* these spinors in such a way that[^ep-7-2-normalizations]

$$
\signal{u}^{\dagger}\signal{u}=2\lvert\energy{E}\rvert/\mechanical{c}
\tag{7.44}
$$

where the dagger signifies the transpose conjugate (or “Hermitian conjugate”):

$$
\signal{u}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\\\gamma\\\delta\end{pmatrix}
\implies\signal{u}^{\dagger}=(\alpha^{*}\ \beta^{*}\ \gamma^{*}\ \delta^{*})
$$

so that

$$
\signal{u}^{\dagger}\signal{u}
=\lvert\alpha\rvert^{2}+\lvert\beta\rvert^{2}+\lvert\gamma\rvert^{2}+\lvert\delta\rvert^{2}.
\tag{7.45}
$$

Thus the four solutions are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\signal{u^{(1)}}&=N\begin{pmatrix}
1\\0\\
\dfrac{\mechanical{c}(\mechanical{p_z})}{\energy{E}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}\\
\dfrac{\mechanical{c}(\mechanical{p_x}+i\mechanical{p_y})}{\energy{E}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}
\end{pmatrix},
&\signal{u^{(2)}}&=N\begin{pmatrix}
0\\1\\
\dfrac{\mechanical{c}(\mechanical{p_x}-i\mechanical{p_y})}{\energy{E}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}\\
\dfrac{\mechanical{c}(-\mechanical{p_z})}{\energy{E}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}
\end{pmatrix},
\end{aligned}
$$

(with $\energy{E}=\sqrt{\mechanical{m}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{4}+\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}}$)

<!-- PDF page 229; printed page 221 -->

$$
\begin{aligned}
\signal{u^{(3)}}&=N\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{\mechanical{c}(\mechanical{p_z})}{\energy{E}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}\\
\dfrac{\mechanical{c}(\mechanical{p_x}+i\mechanical{p_y})}{\energy{E}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}\\
1\\0
\end{pmatrix},
&\signal{u^{(4)}}&=N\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{\mechanical{c}(\mechanical{p_x}-i\mechanical{p_y})}{\energy{E}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}\\
\dfrac{\mechanical{c}(-\mechanical{p_z})}{\energy{E}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}\\
0\\1
\end{pmatrix},
\end{aligned}
\quad
(\text{with }\energy{E}=-\sqrt{\mechanical{m}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{4}+\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}})
\tag{7.46}
$$

and the normalization constant is (see Problem 7.3)

$$
N=\sqrt{(\lvert\energy{E}\rvert+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2})/\mechanical{c}}.
\tag{7.47}
$$

You might guess that $\signal{u^{(1)}}$ describes an electron with spin up, $\signal{u^{(2)}}$ an electron with spin down, and so on, but this is not quite the case. For Dirac particles the spin matrices [generalizing (4.21)] are

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf S}=\frac{\energy{\hbar}}{2}\boldsymbol{\Sigma},
\qquad\text{with }\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=
\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\sigma}&0\\0&\boldsymbol{\sigma}\end{pmatrix}
\tag{7.48}
$$

and it's easy to check that $\signal{u^{(1)}}$, for instance, is not an eigenstate of $\Sigma_z$. However, if we orient the $z$ axis so that it points along the direction of motion (in which case $\mechanical{p_x}=\mechanical{p_y}=0$) then $\signal{u^{(1)}}$, $\signal{u^{(2)}}$, $\signal{u^{(3)}}$, and $\signal{u^{(4)}}$ are eigenspinors of $\mechanical{S_z}$; $\signal{u^{(1)}}$ and $\signal{u^{(3)}}$ are spin up, and $\signal{u^{(2)}}$ and $\signal{u^{(4)}}$ are spin down[^ep-7-2-spin] (Problem 7.6).

I said earlier that $\energy{E}$ and $\mechanical{\mathbf p}$ [in eq. (7.31)] are mathematical parameters which correspond physically to energy and momentum, and this is quite true for the electron states, $\signal{u^{(1)}}$ and $\signal{u^{(2)}}$. However, the $\energy{E}$ in $\signal{u^{(3)}}$ and $\signal{u^{(4)}}$ *cannot* represent positron energy; all free particles, positrons and electrons alike, carry *positive* energy. The “negative-energy” solutions must be reinterpreted as *positive* energy antiparticle states. To express these solutions in terms of the *physical* energy and momentum of the positron, we flip the signs of $\energy{E}$ and $\mechanical{\mathbf p}$:

$$
\signal{\psi}(\mechanical{\mathbf r},\mechanical{t})
=a e^{i/\energy{\hbar}(\energy{E}\mechanical{t}-\mechanical{\mathbf p}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf r})}
\signal{u}(-\energy{E},-\mechanical{\mathbf p})
\qquad[\text{for solutions (3) and (4)}].
\tag{7.49}
$$

Mind you, these are the same old solutions to the Dirac equation; I have simply adopted a different sign convention for the parameters—one that better conforms to their physical interpretation.[^ep-7-2-parameters] It is customary to use the letter $\signal{v}$ for positron states, expressed in terms of the physical energy and momentum:[^ep-7-2-conjugation]

<!-- PDF page 230; printed page 222 -->

$$
\begin{aligned}
\signal{v^{(1)}}(\energy{E},\mechanical{\mathbf p})
&=\signal{u^{(4)}}(-\energy{E},-\mechanical{\mathbf p})
=N\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{\mechanical{c}(\mechanical{p_x}-i\mechanical{p_y})}{\energy{E}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}\\
\dfrac{\mechanical{c}(-\mechanical{p_z})}{\energy{E}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}\\0\\1
\end{pmatrix},\\
\signal{v^{(2)}}(\energy{E},\mechanical{\mathbf p})
&=-\signal{u^{(3)}}(-\energy{E},-\mechanical{\mathbf p})
=-N\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{\mechanical{c}(\mechanical{p_z})}{\energy{E}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}\\
\dfrac{\mechanical{c}(\mechanical{p_x}+i\mechanical{p_y})}{\energy{E}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}}\\1\\0
\end{pmatrix},
\end{aligned}
\quad
(\text{with }\energy{E}=\sqrt{\mechanical{m}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{4}+\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}})
\tag{7.50}
$$

From now on I will never mention $\signal{u^{(3)}}$ and $\signal{u^{(4)}}$; the solutions we shall use are $\signal{u^{(1)}}$, $\signal{u^{(2)}}$ (representing the two spin states of an electron with energy $\energy{E}$ and momentum $\mechanical{\mathbf p}$) and $\signal{v^{(1)}}$, $\signal{v^{(2)}}$ (representing the two spin states of a positron with energy $\energy{E}$ and momentum $\mechanical{\mathbf p}$). Notice that whereas the $\signal{u}$'s satisfy the momentum space Dirac equation (7.34) in the form

$$
(\gamma^\mu\mechanical{p_\mu}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})\signal{u}=0
\tag{7.34}
$$

the $\signal{v}$'s obey the equation with the sign of $\mechanical{p_\mu}$ reversed:

$$
(\gamma^\mu\mechanical{p_\mu}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})\signal{v}=0.
\tag{7.51}
$$

Incidentally, plane waves are, of course, rather special solutions to the Dirac equation. They are the ones of interest to us, however, because they describe particles with specified energies and momenta, and in a typical experiment these are the parameters we control and measure.

[^ep-7-2-complete]: You might ask why we don't simply assume that $\signal{\psi_B}$ is always zero; call the negative energy solutions “physically unacceptable” and forget about them. Unfortunately, this can't be done. In a quantum system we need a *complete* set of states, and the positive energy states by themselves are *not* complete. In the Schrödinger equation the sign of $i$ is purely conventional. Had we made the other choice, then $e^{i\energy{E}\mechanical{t}/\energy{\hbar}}$ would replace (7.29) as the characteristic time dependence of a stationary state of energy $\energy{E}$. In relativistic quantum theory *both* signs are forced on us and this, when properly interpreted, implies the existence of antiparticles.
[^ep-7-2-nonzero]: Equation (7.40) would also allow $\signal{u_A}=0$ as a solution; however, the same argument, starting with equation (7.36) but inserting the first into the second, yields equation (7.40) with $\signal{u_B}$ in place of $\signal{u_A}$. Thus unless $\signal{u_A}$ and $\signal{u_B}$ are *both* zero (in which case we have no solution at all) equation (7.41) must hold.
[^ep-7-2-normalizations]: Actually, there are at least three different conventions in the literature: $\signal{u}^{\dagger}\signal{u}=2\lvert\energy{E}\rvert/\mechanical{c}$ (Halzen and Martin), $\signal{u}^{\dagger}\signal{u}=\lvert\energy{E}\rvert/\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^{2}$ (Bjorken and Drell), $\signal{u}^{\dagger}\signal{u}=1$ (Bogoliubov and Shirkov). In this one instance I depart from Bjorken and Drell, whose choice introduces spurious difficulties when $\mechanical{m}\to0$.
[^ep-7-2-spin]: As a matter of fact, it is *impossible* to construct spinors that satisfy equation (7.34) and are, at the same time, eigenstates of $\mechanical{S_z}$ (except in the special case $\mechanical{\mathbf p}=\mechanical{p_z}\hat{\mathbf z}$). The reason is that $\mechanical{\mathbf S}$ by itself is not a conserved quantity; only the *total* angular momentum, $\mechanical{\mathbf L}+\mechanical{\mathbf S}$, is conserved here (see Problem 7.8). It is possible to construct eigenstates of *helicity*, $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\cdot\mechanical{\hat{\mathbf p}}$ (there's no orbital angular momentum about the direction of motion), but these are rather cumbersome (see Problem 7.7), and in practice it is easier to work with the spinors (7.46), even though their physical interpretation is not so clean. All that really matters is that we have a *complete* set of solutions.
[^ep-7-2-parameters]: If it bothers you to change notation in “midstream” like this, go back to equation (7.32) and don't call it $\mechanical{p^\mu}$ at all—call it $\mechanical{k^\mu}$ (or something). Then, at the end, identify $\mechanical{k^0}=\energy{E}/\mechanical{c}$, $\mechanical{\mathbf k}=\mechanical{\mathbf p}$ for solutions (1) and (2), $\mechanical{k^0}=-\energy{E}/\mechanical{c}$, $\mechanical{\mathbf k}=-\mechanical{\mathbf p}$ for solutions (3) and (4).
[^ep-7-2-conjugation]: It is conventional to associate $\signal{v^{(1)}}$ with $\signal{u^{(4)}}$, and $\signal{v^{(2)}}$ with $-\signal{u^{(3)}}$. In the special case $\mechanical{p_x}=\mechanical{p_y}=0$, then, $\signal{v^{(1)}}$ is *spin down* and $\signal{v^{(2)}}$ is *spin up*. This seems silly at first, but there is a reason for it: The charge conjugation operator takes an electron with spin up into a positron with spin down, so this way $\signal{u^{(1)}}$, $\signal{v^{(1)}}$ are “particle-antiparticle pairs,” as are $\signal{u^{(2)}}$, $\signal{v^{(2)}}$ (see Problem 7.9).
