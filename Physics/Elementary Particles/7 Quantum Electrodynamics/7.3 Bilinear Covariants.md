> [!TLDR]
> Adjoint spinors form bilinears with scalar, pseudoscalar, vector, pseudovector, and antisymmetric tensor transformation rules.

<!-- PDF page 230; printed page 222 -->

I mentioned in Section 7.1 that the components of a Dirac spinor do not transform as a four-vector, when you go from one inertial system to another. How, then, *do* they transform? I shall not work it out here, but merely quote the result:<sup>2</sup> If we go to a system moving with speed $\mechanical{v}$ in the $x$ direction, the transformation rule is

$$
\signal{\psi}\to\signal{\psi'}=S\signal{\psi}
\tag{7.52}
$$

<!-- PDF page 231; printed page 223 -->

where $S$ is the following $4\times4$ matrix:

$$
S=a_++a_-\gamma^0\gamma^1
=\begin{pmatrix}a_+&a_-\sigma_1\\a_-\sigma_1&a_+\end{pmatrix}
\tag{7.53}
$$

with

$$
a_\pm=\pm\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}(\mechanical{\gamma}\pm1)}
\tag{7.54}
$$

and $\mechanical{\gamma}=1/\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^{2}/\mechanical{c}^{2}}$, as usual.

Suppose we want to construct a scalar quantity out of a spinor $\signal{\psi}$. It would be reasonable to try the expression

$$
\signal{\psi}^{\dagger}\signal{\psi}
=(\signal{\psi_1}^{*}\ \signal{\psi_2}^{*}\ \signal{\psi_3}^{*}\ \signal{\psi_4}^{*})
\begin{pmatrix}\signal{\psi_1}\\\signal{\psi_2}\\\signal{\psi_3}\\\signal{\psi_4}\end{pmatrix}
=\lvert\signal{\psi_3}\rvert^{2}+\lvert\signal{\psi_2}\rvert^{2}
+\lvert\signal{\psi_3}\rvert^{2}+\lvert\signal{\psi_4}\rvert^{2}.
\tag{7.55}
$$

Unfortunately, this is *not* a scalar, as you can check by applying the preceding transformation rule:[^ep-7-3-transpose]

$$
(\signal{\psi}^{\dagger}\signal{\psi})'
=(\signal{\psi'})^{\dagger}\signal{\psi'}
=\signal{\psi}^{\dagger}S^{\dagger}S\signal{\psi}
\ne(\signal{\psi}^{\dagger}\signal{\psi}).
\tag{7.56}
$$

In fact (see Problem 7.11):

$$
S^{\dagger}S=S^{2}
=\mechanical{\gamma}\begin{pmatrix}
1&-\dfrac{\mechanical{v}}{\mechanical{c}}\sigma_1\\
-\dfrac{\mechanical{v}}{\mechanical{c}}\sigma_1&1
\end{pmatrix}\ne1.
\tag{7.57}
$$

Of course, the sum of the squares of the elements of a four-vector is not invariant either; we need minus signs for the spatial components [eq. (3.12)]. With a little trial-and-error you will discover that in the case of spinors we need minus signs for the third and fourth components. Just as we introduced *covariant* four-vectors to keep track of the signs in Chapter 3, we now introduce the *adjoint* spinor:

$$
\signal{\bar\psi}\equiv\signal{\psi}^{\dagger}\gamma^0
=(\signal{\psi_1}^{*}\ \signal{\psi_2}^{*}\ {-\signal{\psi_3}^{*}}\ {-\signal{\psi_4}^{*}}).
\tag{7.58}
$$

I claim that the quantity

$$
\signal{\bar\psi}\signal{\psi}
=\signal{\psi}^{\dagger}\gamma^0\signal{\psi}
=\lvert\signal{\psi_1}\rvert^{2}+\lvert\signal{\psi_2}\rvert^{2}
-\lvert\signal{\psi_3}\rvert^{2}-\lvert\signal{\psi_4}\rvert^{2}
\tag{7.59}
$$

*is* a relativistic invariant. For $S^{\dagger}\gamma^0S=\gamma^0$ (Problem 7.11), and hence

$$
(\signal{\bar\psi}\signal{\psi})'
=(\signal{\psi'})^{\dagger}\gamma^0\signal{\psi'}
=\signal{\psi}^{\dagger}S^{\dagger}\gamma^0S\signal{\psi}
=\signal{\psi}^{\dagger}\gamma^0\signal{\psi}
=\signal{\bar\psi}\signal{\psi}.
\tag{7.60}
$$

In Chapter 4 we learned to distinguish scalars and pseudoscalars, according to their behavior under the parity transformation, $P:(\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z})\to(-\mechanical{x},-\mechanical{y},-\mechanical{z})$. Pseudoscalars change sign, scalars do not. It is natural to ask whether $\signal{\bar\psi}\signal{\psi}$ is the

<!-- PDF page 232; printed page 224 -->

former type, or the latter. First, we need to know how Dirac spinors transform under $P$. Again, I won't derive it, but simply quote the result:<sup>3</sup>

$$
\signal{\psi}\to\signal{\psi'}=\gamma^0\signal{\psi}.
\tag{7.61}
$$

It follows that

$$
(\signal{\bar\psi}\signal{\psi})'
=(\signal{\psi'})^{\dagger}\gamma^0\signal{\psi'}
=\signal{\psi}^{\dagger}(\gamma^0)^{\dagger}\gamma^0\gamma^0\signal{\psi}
=\signal{\psi}^{\dagger}\gamma^0\signal{\psi}
=\signal{\bar\psi}\signal{\psi}
\tag{7.62}
$$

so $(\signal{\bar\psi}\signal{\psi})$ is *invariant* under $P$; it's a “true” scalar. But we can also make a *pseudoscalar* out of $\signal{\psi}$:

$$
\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^5\signal{\psi}
\tag{7.63}
$$

where

$$
\gamma^5\equiv i\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3
=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{7.64}
$$

I'll let you check that it is Lorentz-invariant (Problem 7.12). As for its behavior under parity

$$
(\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^5\signal{\psi})'
=(\signal{\psi'})^{\dagger}\gamma^0\gamma^5\signal{\psi'}
=\signal{\psi}^{\dagger}\gamma^0\gamma^0\gamma^5\gamma^0\signal{\psi}
=\signal{\psi}^{\dagger}\gamma^5\gamma^0\signal{\psi}.
\tag{7.65}
$$

(I used the fact that $(\gamma^0)^{2}=1$ in the last step.) Now, the $\gamma^0$ is on the “wrong side” of the $\gamma^5$, but we can “pull it through” by noting that it *anticommutes* with $\gamma^1$, $\gamma^2$, and $\gamma^3$ [eq. (7.15)] and *commutes* (of course) with itself ($\gamma^1\gamma^0=-\gamma^0\gamma^1$, $\gamma^2\gamma^0=-\gamma^0\gamma^2$, $\gamma^3\gamma^0=-\gamma^0\gamma^3$, $\gamma^0\gamma^0=\gamma^0\gamma^0$), so

$$
\gamma^5\gamma^0=i\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3\gamma^0
=(-1)^{3}\gamma^0(i\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3)
=-\gamma^0\gamma^5.
$$

By the same token, $\gamma^5$ anticommutes with all the other $\gamma$ matrices:

$$
\{\gamma^\mu,\gamma^5\}=0.
\tag{7.66}
$$

At any rate

$$
(\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^5\signal{\psi})'
=-\signal{\psi}^{\dagger}\gamma^0\gamma^5\signal{\psi}
=-(\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^5\signal{\psi})
\tag{7.67}
$$

so it's a *pseudoscalar*.

All told, there are 16 products of the form $\signal{\psi_i}^{*}\signal{\psi_j}$ (taking one component from $\signal{\psi}^{*}$ and one from $\signal{\psi}$), since $i$ and $j$ run from 1 to 4. These 16 products can be added together in various linear combinations to construct quantities with distinct transformation behavior, as follows:

$$
\left.
\begin{aligned}
(1)\quad\signal{\bar\psi}\signal{\psi}&=\text{scalar}&&\text{(one component)}\\
(2)\quad\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^5\signal{\psi}&=\text{pseudoscalar}&&\text{(one component)}\\
(3)\quad\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\signal{\psi}&=\text{vector}&&\text{(four components)}\\
(4)\quad\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\gamma^5\signal{\psi}&=\text{pseudovector}&&\text{(four components)}\\
(5)\quad\signal{\bar\psi}\sigma^{\mu\nu}\signal{\psi}&=\text{antisymmetric tensor}&&\text{(six components)}
\end{aligned}
\right\}
\tag{7.68}
$$

where

$$
\sigma^{\mu\nu}\equiv\frac{i}{2}(\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu-\gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu).
\tag{7.69}
$$

This gives 16 terms, so it's all we can hope to make, in this way. You cannot, for example, construct a *symmetric* tensor bilinear in $\signal{\psi}^{*}$ and $\signal{\psi}$, and if you're

<!-- PDF page 233; printed page 225 -->

looking for a vector, $\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\signal{\psi}$ is the only candidate.[^ep-7-3-normalization] (Another way to think of it is this: 1, $\gamma^5$, $\gamma^\mu$, $\gamma^\mu\gamma^5$, and $\sigma^{\mu\nu}$ constitute a “basis” for the space of all $4\times4$ matrices; *any* $4\times4$ matrix can be written as a linear combination of these 16. In particular, if you ever encounter a product of five $\gamma$ matrices, say, you may be sure that it can be reduced down to a product of no more than two.) Pause a moment to admire the notation in (7.68). The tensorial character of the *bilinear covariants*, and even their behavior under parity, is indicated at a glance: $\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^\mu\signal{\psi}$ *looks* like a four-vector, and it *is* a four-vector. But $\gamma^\mu$ by itself is certainly *not* a four-vector; it's a collection of four fixed matrices (7.17); *they* don't change when you go to a different inertial system—it's $\signal{\psi}$ that changes.

[^ep-7-3-transpose]: Note that the transpose of a product is the product of the transposes in *reverse order*:

    $$
    (\widetilde{AB})_{ij}=(AB)_{ji}=A_{jk}B_{ki}
    =\widetilde B_{ik}\widetilde A_{kj}=(\widetilde B\widetilde A)_{ij}.
    $$

    The same goes for the Hermitian conjugate:

    $$
    (AB)^{\dagger}=B^{\dagger}A^{\dagger}.
    $$

[^ep-7-3-normalization]: Notice that $\signal{\bar\psi}\gamma^0\signal{\psi}=\signal{\psi}^{\dagger}\gamma^0\gamma^0\signal{\psi}=\signal{\psi}^{\dagger}\signal{\psi}$, so $\signal{\psi}^{\dagger}\signal{\psi}$ is actually the zeroth component of a four-vector. That's why the normalization convention (7.44), which no doubt looked peculiar at the time, is actually very sensible. By normalizing $\signal{u}^{\dagger}\signal{u}$ to the zeroth component of the four-vector $\mechanical{p^\mu}$, we obtain a relativistically “natural” convention (see Problem 7.14). By the way, as in nonrelativistic quantum mechanics, $\signal{\psi}^{*}\signal{\psi}$ has the dimensions of (volume)$^{-1}$, so the constant $a$ in equation (7.31) carries the units $\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}(\energy{\hbar}^{-3/2})$.
