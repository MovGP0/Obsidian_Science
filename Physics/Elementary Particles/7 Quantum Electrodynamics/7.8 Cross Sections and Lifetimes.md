We are now back on familiar “turf.” Having calculated $|\mathcal M|^2$ (or, where appropriate, $\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle$), we simply plug it into the relevant cross-section formula from Chapter 6: equation (6.34), in the general case; equation (6.42), for two-body scattering in the *CM*; or one of the equations from Problems (6.8), (6.9), or (6.10), in the lab frame.

## Example 7.7 Mott and Rutherford Scattering

An electron (mass $\mechanical{m}$) scatters off a much heavier “muon” (mass $\mechanical{M}\gg\mechanical{m}$). Assuming that the recoil of $\mechanical{M}$ can be neglected, find the differential scattering cross section in the lab frame ($\mechanical{M}$ at rest).

*Solution.* According to Problem (6.8), the cross section is given by

$$
\frac{d\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\mechanical{\Omega}}
=\left(\frac{\energy{\hbar}}{8\pi\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c}}\right)^2
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle
$$

Because the target is stationary, we have (see Fig. 7.8):

$$
\mechanical{p_1}=\left(\frac{\energy{E}}{\mechanical{c}},\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}\right),
\quad\mechanical{p_2}=(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c},\mathbf0),
\quad\mechanical{p_3}=\left(\frac{\energy{E}}{\mechanical{c}},\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}\right),
\quad\mechanical{p_4}=(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c},\mathbf0)
$$

where $\energy{E}$ is the incident (and scattered) electron energy, $\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}$ is the incident momentum, and $\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}$ is the scattered momentum (their magnitudes are equal, $|\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}|=|\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}|\equiv|\mechanical{\mathbf p}|$, and the angle between them is $\mechanical{\theta}$: $\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}=\mechanical{\mathbf p}^2\cos\mechanical{\theta}$). So

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3})^2
&=-(\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}-\mechanical{\mathbf p_3})^2
=-\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}^2-\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}^2
+2\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}\\
&=-2\mechanical{\mathbf p}^2(1-\cos\mechanical{\theta})
=-4\mechanical{\mathbf p}^2\sin^2\frac{\mechanical{\theta}}2
\end{aligned}
$$

![[elementary-particles-fig-7-8-heavy-target.svg|**Fig. 7.8** Electron scattering from a heavy target.]]

<!-- PDF page 249; printed page 241 -->

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_3})
&=\frac{\energy{E}^2}{\mechanical{c}^2}
-\mechanical{\mathbf p_1}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf p_3}
=\mechanical{\mathbf p}^2+\mechanical{m}^2\mechanical{c}^2
-\mechanical{\mathbf p}^2\cos\mechanical{\theta}
=\mechanical{m}^2\mechanical{c}^2
+2\mechanical{\mathbf p}^2\sin^2\frac{\mechanical{\theta}}2\\
(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2})(\mechanical{p_3}\cdot\mechanical{p_4})
&=(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_4})(\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_3})
=(\mechanical{M}\energy{E})^2\\
(\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_4})&=(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})^2
\end{aligned}
$$

Putting this into equation (7.126), we have

$$
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle
=\left(\frac{\electric{g_e}^2\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c}}
{\mechanical{\mathbf p}^2\sin^2(\mechanical{\theta}/2)}\right)^2
\left((\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})^2
+\mechanical{\mathbf p}^2\cos^2\frac{\mechanical{\theta}}2\right)
\tag{7.127}
$$

and therefore (recalling that $\electric{g_e}=\sqrt{4\pi\electric{\alpha}}$)

$$
\frac{d\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\mechanical{\Omega}}
=\left(\frac{\electric{\alpha}\energy{\hbar}}
{2\mechanical{\mathbf p}^2\sin^2(\mechanical{\theta}/2)}\right)^2
\left[(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})^2
+\mechanical{\mathbf p}^2\cos^2\frac{\mechanical{\theta}}2\right].
\tag{7.128}
$$

This is the Mott formula. It gives, to good approximation, the differential cross section for electron-proton scattering. If the incident electron is nonrelativistic, so that $\mechanical{\mathbf p}^2\ll(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})^2$, equation (7.128) reduces to the Rutherford formula (compare Example 6.4):

$$
\frac{d\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\mechanical{\Omega}}
=\left(\frac{\electric{e}^2}
{2\mechanical{m}\mechanical{v}^2\sin^2(\mechanical{\theta}/2)}\right)^2.
\tag{7.129}
$$

What about decays? Actually, there is no such thing, in pure QED, for if a single fermion goes *in*, that same fermion must eventually come *out*; a fermion line cannot simply terminate within a diagram, nor is there any mechanism in QED for converting one fermion (say, a muon) into another (such as an electron). To be sure, there exist electromagnetic decays of *composite* particles, for example, $\pi^0\to\gamma+\gamma$; but the *electromagnetic* component in this process is nothing but quark-antiquark pair annihilation, $q+\bar q\to\gamma+\gamma$. It is really a *scattering* event, in which the two colliding particles happen to be in a bound state. The cleanest example of such a process is the decay of positronium: $e^++e^-\to\gamma+\gamma$, which we consider in the following example. We'll do the analysis in the positronium rest frame (which is to say, in the *CM* frame of the electron-positron pair). They are typically moving rather slowly; indeed, for purposes of calculating the amplitude we shall assume they are at rest. On the other hand, this is one of those cases in which we cannot average over initial spins, because the composite system is either in the singlet configuration—spins antiparallel—or in the triplet configuration—spins parallel—and the formula for the cross section (and hence the lifetime) is quite different in the two cases.[^ep-7-8-singlet]

## Example 7.8 Pair Annihilation

Compute the amplitude, $\mathcal M$, for $e^++e^-\to\gamma+\gamma$, assuming the electron and positron are at rest, and in the singlet spin configuration.

<!-- PDF page 250; printed page 242 -->

*Solution.* Two diagrams contribute, as shown in Figure 7.9. The amplitudes are (for simplicity I'll suppress the complex conjugate signs on the $\signal{\epsilon}$'s):

$$
\mathcal M_1=
\frac{\electric{g_e}^2}
{(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3})^2-\mechanical{m}^2\mechanical{c}^2}
\signal{\bar v}(2)\not{\signal{\epsilon_4}}
(\not{\mechanical{p_1}}-\not{\mechanical{p_3}}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})
\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}\signal{u}(1)
\tag{7.130}
$$

$$
\mathcal M_2=
\frac{\electric{g_e}^2}
{(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_4})^2-\mechanical{m}^2\mechanical{c}^2}
\signal{\bar v}(2)\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}
(\not{\mechanical{p_1}}-\not{\mechanical{p_4}}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})
\not{\signal{\epsilon_4}}\signal{u}(1)
\tag{7.131}
$$

and they *add*

$$
\mathcal M=\mathcal M_1+\mathcal M_2
\tag{7.132}
$$

With the initial particles at rest, the photons come out “back to back,” and we may as well choose the $\mechanical{z}$ axis to coincide with the photon line; then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{p_1}&=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}(1,0,0,0),
&\mechanical{p_2}&=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}(1,0,0,0),\\
\mechanical{p_3}&=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}(1,0,0,1),
&\mechanical{p_4}&=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}(1,0,0,-1)
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.133}
$$

and hence

$$
(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3})^2-\mechanical{m}^2\mechanical{c}^2
=(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_4})^2-\mechanical{m}^2\mechanical{c}^2
=-2(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})^2.
\tag{7.134}
$$

The amplitudes simplify somewhat if we exploit rule $5'$ from Section 7.7:

$$
\not{\mechanical{p_1}}\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}
=-\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}\not{\mechanical{p_1}}
+2(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\signal{\epsilon_3})
$$

But $\signal{\epsilon_3}$ has only spatial components (in the Coulomb gauge), whereas $\mechanical{p_1}$ is purely temporal, so $\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\signal{\epsilon_3}=0$, and hence

$$
\not{\mechanical{p_1}}\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}
=-\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}\not{\mechanical{p_1}}
\tag{7.135}
$$

Similarly

$$
\not{\mechanical{p_3}}\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}
=-\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}\not{\mechanical{p_3}}
+2(\mechanical{p_3}\cdot\signal{\epsilon_3})
$$

but $(\mechanical{p_3}\cdot\signal{\epsilon_3})=0$ by virtue of the Lorentz condition (7.90), so

$$
\not{\mechanical{p_3}}\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}
=-\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}\not{\mechanical{p_3}}
\tag{7.136}
$$

Therefore

$$
(\not{\mechanical{p_1}}-\not{\mechanical{p_3}}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})
\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}\signal{u}(1)
=\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}
(-\not{\mechanical{p_1}}+\not{\mechanical{p_3}}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})\signal{u}(1)
$$

But $(\not{\mechanical{p_1}}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})\signal{u}(1)=0$, because $\signal{u}(1)$ satisfies the Dirac equation (7.34), so

$$
(\not{\mechanical{p_1}}-\not{\mechanical{p_3}}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})
\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}\signal{u}(1)
=\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}\not{\mechanical{p_3}}\signal{u}(1)
\tag{7.137}
$$

![[elementary-particles-fig-7-9-pair-annihilation.svg|**Fig. 7.9** Two contributions to pair annihilation.]]

<!-- PDF page 251; printed page 243 -->

By the same token

$$
(\not{\mechanical{p_1}}-\not{\mechanical{p_4}}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})
\not{\signal{\epsilon_4}}\signal{u}(1)
=\not{\signal{\epsilon_4}}\not{\mechanical{p_4}}\signal{u}(1)
\tag{7.138}
$$

Putting all this together, we find

$$
\mathcal M=-\frac{\electric{g_e}^2}{2(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})^2}
\signal{\bar v}(2)
[\not{\signal{\epsilon_4}}\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}\not{\mechanical{p_3}}
+\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}\not{\signal{\epsilon_4}}\not{\mechanical{p_4}}]
\signal{u}(1).
\tag{7.139}
$$

Now

$$
\not{\mechanical{p_3}}=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}(\gamma^0-\gamma^3),
\qquad\not{\mechanical{p_4}}=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}(\gamma^0+\gamma^3)
$$

so the expression in square brackets can be written as

$$
\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}
[(\not{\signal{\epsilon_4}}\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}
+\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}\not{\signal{\epsilon_4}})\gamma^0
-(\not{\signal{\epsilon_4}}\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}
-\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}\not{\signal{\epsilon_4}})\gamma^3]
\tag{7.140}
$$

But

$$
\not{\signal{\epsilon}}=-\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon}\cdot\boldsymbol\gamma
=-\begin{pmatrix}
0&\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon}\\
-\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon}&0
\end{pmatrix}
\tag{7.141}
$$

and therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}\not{\signal{\epsilon_4}}
&=\begin{pmatrix}
0&\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_3}\\
-\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_3}&0
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
0&\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_4}\\
-\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_4}&0
\end{pmatrix}\\
&=-\begin{pmatrix}
(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_3})
(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_4})&0\\
0&(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_3})
(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_4})
\end{pmatrix}
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.142}
$$

In Chapter 4 (Problem 4.20) we encountered the useful theorem

$$
(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf a)(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf b)
=\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b+i\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)
\tag{7.143}
$$

It follows that

$$
(\not{\signal{\epsilon_4}}\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}
+\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}\not{\signal{\epsilon_4}})
=-2\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_3}\cdot\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_4}
\tag{7.144}
$$

(which we could have obtained directly from rule $5'$), and

$$
(\not{\signal{\epsilon_4}}\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}
-\not{\signal{\epsilon_3}}\not{\signal{\epsilon_4}})
=2i(\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_3}\times\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_4})
\cdot\boldsymbol\Sigma
\tag{7.145}
$$

where $\boldsymbol\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol\sigma&0\\0&\boldsymbol\sigma\end{pmatrix}$, as before. Accordingly

$$
\mathcal M=\frac{\electric{g_e}^2}{(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})}
\signal{\bar v}(2)
[\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_3}\cdot\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_4}\gamma^0
+i(\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_3}\times\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_4})
\cdot\boldsymbol\Sigma\gamma^3]\signal{u}(1)
\tag{7.146}
$$

So far, I have said nothing about the spins of the electron and positron. Remember that we are interested in the *singlet* state:

$$
(\uparrow\downarrow-\downarrow\uparrow)/\sqrt2
$$

Symbolically

$$
\mathcal M_{\mathrm{singlet}}=
(\mathcal M_{\uparrow\downarrow}-\mathcal M_{\downarrow\uparrow})/\sqrt2
\tag{7.147}
$$

$\mathcal M_{\uparrow\downarrow}$ is obtained from equation (7.146) with “spin up” for the electron

$$
\signal{u}(1)=\sqrt{2\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}}
\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}
\tag{7.148}
$$

<!-- PDF page 252; printed page 244 -->

and “spin down” for the positron

$$
\signal{\bar v}(2)=\sqrt{2\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}}(0\;0\;1\;0)
\tag{7.149}
$$

Using these spinors, we find

$$
\signal{\bar v}(2)\gamma^0\signal{u}(1)=0
\tag{7.150}
$$

$$
\signal{\bar v}(2)\boldsymbol\Sigma\gamma^3\signal{u}(1)
=-2\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}\mechanical{\hat z}
\tag{7.151}
$$

So

$$
\mathcal M_{\uparrow\downarrow}
=-2i\electric{g_e}^2(\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_3}
\times\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_4})_z
\tag{7.152}
$$

Meanwhile, for $\mathcal M_{\downarrow\uparrow}$ we have

$$
\signal{u}(1)=\sqrt{2\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}}
\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad\signal{\bar v}(2)=-\sqrt{2\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}}(0\;0\;0\;1)
\tag{7.153}
$$

from which it follows that

$$
\mathcal M_{\downarrow\uparrow}
=2i\electric{g_e}^2(\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_3}
\times\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_4})_z
=-\mathcal M_{\uparrow\downarrow}.
\tag{7.154}
$$

Thus the amplitude for annihilation of a stationary $e^+e^-$ pair into two photons, which emerge in the directions $\pm\mechanical{\hat z}$, is

$$
\mathcal M_{\mathrm{singlet}}
=-2\sqrt2\,i\electric{g_e}^2(\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_3}
\times\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_4})_z
\tag{7.155}
$$

(I note in passing that since $\mathcal M_{\downarrow\uparrow}=-\mathcal M_{\uparrow\downarrow}$, the *triplet* configuration $(\uparrow\downarrow+\downarrow\uparrow)/\sqrt2$ gives *zero*, confirming our earlier observation that the two-photon decay is forbidden in that case.)

Finally, we must put in the appropriate photon polarization vectors. Recall that for “spin up” ($\mechanical{m_s}=+1$) we have

$$
\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_\uparrow}=-(1/\sqrt2)(1,i,0)
\tag{7.156}
$$

whereas for “spin down” ($\mechanical{m_s}=-1$)

$$
\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_\downarrow}=(1/\sqrt2)(1,-i,0)
\tag{7.157}
$$

If the photon is traveling in the $+\mechanical{z}$ direction, these correspond to right- and left-circular polarization, respectively. Since the $\mechanical{z}$ component of the total angular momentum must be zero, the photon spins must be oppositely aligned: $\uparrow\downarrow$ or $\downarrow\uparrow$. In the first case we have

$$
(\uparrow\downarrow):\quad
\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_3}=-(1/\sqrt2)(1,i,0),\quad
\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_4}=(1/\sqrt2)(1,-i,0),
$$

so that

$$
\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_3}\times\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_4}
=i\mechanical{\hat k}
\tag{7.158}
$$

In the second case 3 and 4 are interchanged, so that

$$
(\downarrow\uparrow):\quad
\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_3}\times\signal{\boldsymbol\epsilon_4}
=-i\mechanical{\hat k}
\tag{7.159}
$$

Evidently, we need the antisymmetric combination, $(\uparrow\downarrow-\downarrow\uparrow)/\sqrt2$, which should come as no surprise: this corresponds to a total spin of zero, just as it did when we combined two particles of spin $\frac12$. Again, the amplitude is $(\mathcal M_{\uparrow\downarrow}-\mathcal M_{\downarrow\uparrow})/\sqrt2$, only this time the arrows refer to photon polarization. Finally, then:

<!-- PDF page 253; printed page 245 -->

$$
\mathcal M_{\mathrm{singlet}}=-4\electric{g_e}^2
\tag{7.160}
$$

(I have restored the complex conjugation of the polarization vectors, suppressed until now; this simply reverses the signs in (7.158) and (7.159).)

That was a lot of work, for a rather modest looking answer. What can we *do* with it? In the first place, we can calculate the total cross section for electron-positron annihilation. In the *CM* frame, the differential cross section is [see eq. (6.42)]

$$
\frac{d\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\mechanical{\Omega}}
=\left(\frac{\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c}}
{8\pi(\energy{E_1}+\energy{E_2})}\right)^2
\frac{|\mechanical{\mathbf p_f}|}{|\mechanical{\mathbf p_i}|}
|\mathcal M|^2
\tag{7.161}
$$

Here

$$
\energy{E_1}=\energy{E_2}=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2,\qquad
|\mechanical{\mathbf p_f}|=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}
\tag{7.162}
$$

and, since the collision is nonrelativistic

$$
|\mechanical{\mathbf p_i}|=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{v}
\tag{7.163}
$$

where $\mechanical{v}$ is the incident electron (or positron) speed. (We used $\mechanical{v}=0$ in calculating $\mathcal M$, but obviously we cannot do so here. Is there an inconsistency in this? Not really. Think of it this way: $\mathcal M$ (and also $\energy{E_1}$, $\energy{E_2}$, $|\mechanical{\mathbf p_f}|$, and $|\mechanical{\mathbf p_i}|$) could be expanded in powers of $\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$. What we have done is to calculate the leading term in each expansion.) Putting all this together, we find

$$
\frac{d\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\mechanical{\Omega}}
=\frac{1}{\mechanical{c}\mechanical{v}}
\left(\frac{\energy{\hbar}\electric{\alpha}}{\mechanical{m}}\right)^2
\tag{7.164}
$$

Since there is no angular dependence, the total cross section is $4\pi$ times this:<sup>6</sup>

$$
\mechanical{\sigma}=\frac{4\pi}{\mechanical{c}\mechanical{v}}
\left(\frac{\energy{\hbar}\electric{\alpha}}{\mechanical{m}}\right)^2
\tag{7.165}
$$

Finally, we would like to determine the lifetime of positronium, in the singlet state. This is clearly *related* to the cross section for pair annihilation (7.165), but what is the precise connection? Well, going back to equation (6.12)

$$
\frac{d\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\mechanical{\Omega}}
=\frac{1}{\mechanical{\mathcal L}}
\frac{d\mechanical{N}}{d\mechanical{\Omega}}
\tag{6.12}
$$

we see that the total number of scattering events per unit time is equal to the luminosity times the total cross section:

$$
\mechanical{N}=\mechanical{\mathcal L}\mechanical{\sigma}
\tag{7.166}
$$

If $\mechanical{\rho}$ is the number of incident particles per unit volume, and if they are traveling at speed $\mechanical{v}$, then the luminosity (Fig. 7.10) is

$$
\mechanical{\mathcal L}=\mechanical{\rho}\mechanical{v}
\tag{7.167}
$$

For a single “atom,” the electron density is $|\signal{\psi}(0)|^2$, and $\mechanical{N}$ represents the probability of a disintegration, per unit time—which is to say, the decay rate. Thus

$$
\mechanical{\Gamma}=\mechanical{v}\mechanical{\sigma}|\signal{\psi}(0)|^2
\tag{7.168}
$$

<!-- PDF page 254; printed page 246 -->

![[elementary-particles-fig-7-10-luminosity-cylinder.svg|**Fig. 7.10** The number of particles in the cylinder is ρAv dt, so the luminosity (number per unit area per unit time) is ρv.]]

Equations (7.165) and (7.168) are the formulas we used in Chapter 5 to determine the lifetime of positronium, $\mechanical{\tau}=1/\mechanical{\Gamma}$ [see eq. (5.84)]:

$$
\mechanical{\Gamma}=\frac{4\pi}{\mechanical{c}}
\left(\frac{\energy{\hbar}\electric{\alpha}}{\mechanical{m}}\right)^2
|\signal{\psi}(0)|^2
\tag{7.169}
$$

[^ep-7-8-singlet]: As a matter of fact, you *can* do this particular problem by Casimir's trick, because of a rather special circumstance: the singlet state can only decay to an *even* number of photons (predominantly *two*) and the triplet to an *odd* number (usually *three*). So in calculating the matrix element for $e^++e^-\to\gamma+\gamma$, we are *automatically* selecting out the singlet configuration even if the triplet was included in the sum over spins.
