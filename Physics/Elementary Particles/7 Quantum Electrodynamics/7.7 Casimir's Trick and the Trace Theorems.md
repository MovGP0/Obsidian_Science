> [!TLDR]
> Casimir's trick converts spin sums into matrix traces, which the gamma-matrix trace theorems reduce.

<!-- PDF page 244; printed page 236 -->

In some experiments the incoming and outgoing electron (or positron) spins are specified, and the photon polarizations are given. If so, the next thing to do is insert the appropriate spinors and polarization vectors into the expression for $\mathcal M$, and compute $|\mathcal M|^2$, the quantity we actually need to determine cross sections and lifetimes. More often, however, we are not interested in the spins. A typical experiment starts out with a beam of particles whose spin orientations are random, and simply counts the number of particles scattered in a given direction. In this case the relevant cross section is the *average* over all initial spin configurations, $i$, and the *sum* over all final spin configurations, $f$. In principle, we could compute $|\mathcal M(i\to f)|^2$ for every possible combination, and then do the summing and averaging:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle\equiv{}&
\textit{average}\text{ over initial spins, }\textit{sum}\text{ over final spins,}\\
&\text{of }|\mathcal M(i\to f)|^2
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.112}
$$

<!-- PDF page 245; printed page 237 -->

In practice, it is much easier to compute $\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle$ directly, without ever evaluating the individual amplitudes.

Consider, for instance, the electron-muon scattering amplitude (7.104). Squaring, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\mathcal M|^2={}&\frac{\electric{g_e}^4}{(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3})^4}
[\signal{\bar u}(3)\gamma^\mu\signal{u}(1)]
[\signal{\bar u}(4)\gamma_\mu\signal{u}(2)]\\
&\times[\signal{\bar u}(3)\gamma^\nu\signal{u}(1)]^*
[\signal{\bar u}(4)\gamma_\nu\signal{u}(2)]^*.
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.113}
$$

(I use $\nu$ for the second space-time index, to avoid confusion.) A glance at the first and third terms (or the second and fourth) reveals that we must handle quantities of the general form

$$
G\equiv[\signal{\bar u}(a)\Gamma_1\signal{u}(b)]
[\signal{\bar u}(a)\Gamma_2\signal{u}(b)]^*
\tag{7.114}
$$

where $(a)$ and $(b)$ stand for the appropriate spins and momenta, and $\Gamma_1$, and $\Gamma_2$ are two $4\times4$ matrices. All the other processes described in Section 7.6—Møller, Bhabha, and Compton scattering, as well as pair production and annihilation—lead to expressions with similar structure. To begin with, we evaluate the complex conjugate (which is the same as the Hermitian conjugate, since the quantity in brackets is a $1\times1$ “matrix”):

$$
[\signal{\bar u}(a)\Gamma_2\signal{u}(b)]^*
=[\signal{u}(a)^\dagger\gamma^0\Gamma_2\signal{u}(b)]^\dagger
=\signal{u}(b)^\dagger\Gamma_2^\dagger\gamma^{0\dagger}\signal{u}(a).
\tag{7.115}
$$

Now, $\gamma^{0\dagger}=\gamma^0$, and $(\gamma^0)^2=1$, so

$$
[\signal{\bar u}(a)\Gamma_2\signal{u}(b)]^*
=\signal{u}(b)^\dagger\gamma^0\gamma^0\Gamma_2^\dagger\gamma^0\signal{u}(a)
=\signal{\bar u}(b)\bar\Gamma_2\signal{u}(a)
\tag{7.116}
$$

where[^ep-7-7-overbar]

$$
\bar\Gamma_2\equiv\gamma^0\Gamma_2^\dagger\gamma^0
\tag{7.117}
$$

Thus

$$
G=[\signal{\bar u}(a)\Gamma_1\signal{u}(b)]
[\signal{\bar u}(b)\bar\Gamma_2\signal{u}(a)].
\tag{7.118}
$$

We are ready now to sum over the spin orientations of particle $(b)$. Using the completeness relation (7.97), we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{b\text{ spins}}G
&=\signal{\bar u}(a)\Gamma_1
\left\{\sum_{s_b=1,2}\signal{u^{(s_b)}}(\mechanical{p_b})
\signal{\bar u^{(s_b)}}(\mechanical{p_b})\right\}
\bar\Gamma_2\signal{u}(a)\\
&=\signal{\bar u}(a)\Gamma_1(\not{\mechanical{p_b}}+\mechanical{m_b}\mechanical{c})
\bar\Gamma_2\signal{u}(a)
=\signal{\bar u}(a)Q\signal{u}(a)
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.119}
$$

where $Q$ is a temporary shorthand for the $4\times4$ matrix

$$
Q\equiv\Gamma_1(\not{\mechanical{p_b}}+\mechanical{m_b}\mechanical{c})\bar\Gamma_2.
\tag{7.120}
$$

Next, we do the same for particle $(a)$:

$$
\sum_{a\text{ spins}}\sum_{b\text{ spins}}G
=\sum_{s_a=1,2}\signal{\bar u^{(s_a)}}(\mechanical{p_a})Q
\signal{u^{(s_a)}}(\mechanical{p_a})
$$

Or, writing out the matrix multiplication explicitly ($i$ and $j$ are summed from 1 to 4):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{s_a=1,2}\signal{\bar u^{(s_a)}}(\mechanical{p_a})_i
Q_{ij}\signal{u^{(s_a)}}(\mechanical{p_a})_j
&=Q_{ij}\left\{\sum_{s_a=1,2}\signal{u^{(s_a)}}(\mechanical{p_a})
\signal{\bar u^{(s_a)}}(\mechanical{p_a})\right\}_{ji}\\
&=Q_{ij}(\not{\mechanical{p_a}}+\mechanical{m_a}\mechanical{c})_{ji}
=\operatorname{Tr}(Q(\not{\mechanical{p_a}}+\mechanical{m_a}\mechanical{c}))
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.121}
$$

<!-- PDF page 246; printed page 238 -->

where $\operatorname{Tr}$ denotes the trace of the matrix (the sum of its diagonal elements):

$$
\operatorname{Tr}(A)\equiv\sum_i A_{ii}
\tag{7.122}
$$

Conclusion:

$$
\sum_{\text{all spins}}
[\signal{\bar u}(a)\Gamma_1\signal{u}(b)]
[\signal{\bar u}(a)\Gamma_2\signal{u}(b)]^*
=\operatorname{Tr}[\Gamma_1(\not{\mechanical{p_b}}+\mechanical{m_b}\mechanical{c})
\bar\Gamma_2(\not{\mechanical{p_a}}+\mechanical{m_a}\mechanical{c})].
\tag{7.123}
$$

This may not look like much of a simplification, but notice that there are *no spinors left*; once we do the summation over spins, it all reduces to matrix multiplication and taking the trace. For want of a better name, I call equation (7.123) “Casimir's trick,” since Casimir was apparently the first one to use it.<sup>5</sup> If either $\signal{u}$ (in 7.123) is replaced by a $\signal{v}$, the corresponding mass on the right-hand side switches sign (see Problem 7.26).

## Example 7.5

In the case of electron-muon scattering (7.113), $\Gamma_2=\gamma^\nu$, and hence $\bar\Gamma_2=\gamma^0\gamma^{\nu\dagger}\gamma^0=\gamma^\nu$ (Problem 7.27). Applying Casimir's trick twice, we find

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle={}&
\frac{\electric{g_e}^4}{4(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3})^4}
\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^\mu(\not{\mechanical{p_1}}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})
\gamma^\nu(\not{\mechanical{p_3}}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})]\\
&\times\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma_\mu(\not{\mechanical{p_2}}+\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})
\gamma_\nu(\not{\mechanical{p_4}}+\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})]
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.124}
$$

where $\mechanical{m}$ is the mass of the electron and $\mechanical{M}$ is the mass of the muon. The factor of $\frac14$ is included because we want the *average* over the initial spins; since there are two particles, each with two allowed spin orientations, the average is a quarter of the sum.

Casimir's trick reduces everything down to a problem of calculating the trace of some complicated product of $\gamma$ matrices. This algebra is facilitated by a number of theorems, which I collect together below (I'll leave the proofs to you—see Problems 7.29 through 7.32). First of all, I should mention three facts about traces in general: if $A$ and $B$ are any two matrices, and $\alpha$ is any number

$$
\begin{aligned}
1.\quad&\operatorname{Tr}(A+B)=\operatorname{Tr}(A)+\operatorname{Tr}(B)\\
2.\quad&\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha A)=\alpha\operatorname{Tr}(A)\\
3.\quad&\operatorname{Tr}(AB)=\operatorname{Tr}(BA)
\end{aligned}
$$

It follows from number 3 that $\operatorname{Tr}(ABC)=\operatorname{Tr}(CAB)=\operatorname{Tr}(BCA)$, but this is *not* equal, in general, to the trace of the matrices taken in the other order: $\operatorname{Tr}(ACB)=\operatorname{Tr}(BAC)=\operatorname{Tr}(CBA)$. In this way you can “peel” matrices off the back end of a product and move them around to the front, but you must preserve the ordering. It is useful to note that

$$
4.\quad g_{\mu\nu}g^{\mu\nu}=4
$$

and to recall the fundamental anticommutation relation for the $\gamma$ matrices (together with an associated rule for “slash” products):

$$
\begin{aligned}
5.\quad&\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu+\gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu=2g^{\mu\nu}
&5'.\quad&\not a\not b+\not b\not a=2a\cdot b
\end{aligned}
$$

<!-- PDF page 247; printed page 239 -->

From these there follows a sequence of “contraction theorems”:

$$
\begin{aligned}
6.\quad&\gamma_\mu\gamma^\mu=4\\
7.\quad&\gamma_\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu=-2\gamma^\nu
&7'.\quad&\gamma_\mu\not a\gamma^\mu=-2\not a\\
8.\quad&\gamma_\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma^\lambda\gamma^\mu=4g^{\nu\lambda}
&8'.\quad&\gamma_\mu\not a\not b\gamma^\mu=4a\cdot b\\
9.\quad&\gamma_\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma^\lambda\gamma^\sigma\gamma^\mu
=-2\gamma^\sigma\gamma^\lambda\gamma^\nu
&9'.\quad&\gamma_\mu\not a\not b\not c\gamma^\mu=-2\not c\not b\not a
\end{aligned}
$$

And finally, there is a set of “trace theorems”:

10. The trace of the product of an *odd* number of gamma matrices is *zero*

$$
\begin{aligned}
11.\quad&\operatorname{Tr}(1)=4\\
12.\quad&\operatorname{Tr}(\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu)=4g^{\mu\nu}
&12'.\quad&\operatorname{Tr}(\not a\not b)=4a\cdot b\\
13.\quad&\operatorname{Tr}(\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma^\lambda\gamma^\sigma)
&13'.\quad&\operatorname{Tr}(\not a\not b\not c\not d)\\
&=4(g^{\mu\nu}g^{\lambda\sigma}-g^{\mu\lambda}g^{\nu\sigma}
+g^{\mu\sigma}g^{\nu\lambda})
&&=4(a\cdot b\,c\cdot d-a\cdot c\,b\cdot d+a\cdot d\,b\cdot c)
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $\gamma^5=i\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3$ is the product of an *even* number of $\gamma$ matrices, it follows from rule 10 that $\operatorname{Tr}(\gamma^5\gamma^\mu)=\operatorname{Tr}(\gamma^5\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma^\lambda)=0$. When $\gamma^5$ is multiplied by an *even* number of $\gamma$'s, we find

$$
\begin{aligned}
14.\quad&\operatorname{Tr}(\gamma^5)=0\\
15.\quad&\operatorname{Tr}(\gamma^5\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu)=0
&15'.\quad&\operatorname{Tr}(\gamma^5\not a\not b)=0\\
16.\quad&\operatorname{Tr}(\gamma^5\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma^\lambda\gamma^\sigma)
=4i\epsilon^{\mu\nu\lambda\sigma}
&16'.\quad&\operatorname{Tr}(\gamma^5\not a\not b\not c\not d)
=4i\epsilon^{\mu\nu\lambda\sigma}a_\mu b_\nu c_\lambda d_\sigma
\end{aligned}
$$

where

$$
\epsilon^{\mu\nu\lambda\sigma}\equiv
\begin{cases}
-1,&\text{if }\mu\nu\lambda\sigma\text{ is an }\textit{even}\text{ permutation of 0123},\\
+1,&\text{if }\mu\nu\lambda\sigma\text{ is an }\textit{odd}\text{ permutation},\\
0,&\text{if any two indices are the same.}
\end{cases}
$$

## Example 7.6

Evaluate the traces in electron-muon scattering [eq. (7.124)].

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^\mu(\not{\mechanical{p_1}}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})
\gamma^\nu(\not{\mechanical{p_3}}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})]\\
&\quad=\operatorname{Tr}(\gamma^\mu\not{\mechanical{p_1}}\gamma^\nu\not{\mechanical{p_3}})
+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}
[\operatorname{Tr}(\gamma^\mu\not{\mechanical{p_1}}\gamma^\nu)
+\operatorname{Tr}(\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\not{\mechanical{p_3}})]
+(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})^2\operatorname{Tr}(\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu)
\end{aligned}
$$

*Solution.* According to rule 10, the terms in square brackets are zero. The last term can be evaluated using rule 12, and the first by rule 13:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Tr}(\gamma^\mu\not{\mechanical{p_1}}\gamma^\nu\not{\mechanical{p_3}})
&=(\mechanical{p_1})_\lambda(\mechanical{p_3})_\sigma
\operatorname{Tr}(\gamma^\mu\gamma^\lambda\gamma^\nu\gamma^\sigma)\\
&=(\mechanical{p_1})_\lambda(\mechanical{p_3})_\sigma
4(g^{\mu\lambda}g^{\nu\sigma}-g^{\mu\nu}g^{\lambda\sigma}
+g^{\mu\sigma}g^{\lambda\nu})\\
&=4(\mechanical{p_1^\mu}\mechanical{p_3^\nu}
-g^{\mu\nu}(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_3})
+\mechanical{p_3^\mu}\mechanical{p_1^\nu}).
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\operatorname{Tr}[\gamma^\mu(\not{\mechanical{p_1}}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})
\gamma^\nu(\not{\mechanical{p_3}}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})]\\
&\quad=4[\mechanical{p_1^\mu}\mechanical{p_3^\nu}
+\mechanical{p_3^\mu}\mechanical{p_1^\nu}
+g^{\mu\nu}((\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})^2
-(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_3}))].
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.125}
$$

<!-- PDF page 248; printed page 240 -->

The second trace (in 7.124) is the same, with $\mechanical{m}\to\mechanical{M}$, $1\to2$, $3\to4$, and the Greek indices lowered. So

$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle|\mathcal M|^2\rangle
&=\frac{4\electric{g_e}^4}{(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3})^4}
[\mechanical{p_1^\mu}\mechanical{p_3^\nu}
+\mechanical{p_3^\mu}\mechanical{p_1^\nu}
+g^{\mu\nu}((\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})^2
-(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_3}))]\\
&\quad\times[\mechanical{p_{2\mu}}\mechanical{p_{4\nu}}
+\mechanical{p_{4\mu}}\mechanical{p_{2\nu}}
+g_{\mu\nu}((\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})^2
-(\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_4}))]\\
&=\frac{8\electric{g_e}^4}{(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{p_3})^4}
[(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2})(\mechanical{p_3}\cdot\mechanical{p_4})
+(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_4})(\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_3})\\
&\qquad-(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_3})(\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c})^2
-(\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_4})(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})^2
+2(\mechanical{m}\mechanical{M}\mechanical{c}^2)^2].
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.126}
$$

[^ep-7-7-overbar]: Observe that the overbar now serves two different functions. On a *spinor* it denotes the *adjoint*: $\signal{\bar\psi}\equiv\signal{\psi}^\dagger\gamma^0$ [eq. (7.58)]; on a $4\times4$ *matrix* it defines a new matrix: $\bar\Gamma\equiv\gamma^0\Gamma^\dagger\gamma^0$.

[^ep-7-7-permutations]: By “even permutation” I mean an even number of interchanges of two indices. Thus $\epsilon^{\mu\nu\lambda\sigma}=-\epsilon^{\nu\mu\lambda\sigma}=\epsilon^{\nu\lambda\mu\sigma}=-\epsilon^{\nu\lambda\sigma\mu}$, and so on. Putting it differently, $\epsilon^{\mu\nu\lambda\sigma}$ is antisymmetric in every pair of superscripts. It might seem strange that $\epsilon^{0123}$ is *minus* 1; why not make it *plus* 1? It's purely *conventional*, of course. Evidently, whoever established the definition wanted $\epsilon_{0123}$ to be plus 1, and from that it follows that $\epsilon^{0123}=-1$, since three spatial indices are raised. By the way, if you are used to working with the three-dimensional Levi–Civita symbol $\epsilon_{ijk}$ (see Problem 4.19), be warned that although an even permutation of *three* indices corresponds to preservation of cyclic order ($\epsilon_{ijk}=\epsilon_{jki}=\epsilon_{kij}$), this is *not* the case for *four* indices: $\epsilon^{\mu\nu\lambda\sigma}=-\epsilon^{\nu\lambda\sigma\mu}=\epsilon^{\lambda\sigma\mu\nu}=-\epsilon^{\sigma\mu\nu\lambda}$.
