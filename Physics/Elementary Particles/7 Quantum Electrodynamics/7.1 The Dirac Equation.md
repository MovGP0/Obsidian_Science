> [!TLDR]
> Gamma matrices factor the relativistic energy relation and give the first-order Dirac equation for a four-component spinor.

<!-- PDF page 221; printed page 213 -->

*In this chapter I introduce the Dirac equation, state the Feynman rules for quantum electrodynamics, develop some useful calculational tools, and derive some of the classic QED results. The treatment leans heavily on material from Chapters 2, 3, and 6, as well as on the spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ formalism in Chapter 4. In turn, Chapter 7 is the indispensable foundation for everything that follows (however, you may want to skip Example 7.8 and Section 7.9, together with the related passages in Chapters 8 and 9).*

Although the “ABC” model in Chapter 6 is a perfectly legitimate quantum field theory, it does not describe the real world, because the particles $A$, $B$, and $C$ have spin 0, whereas quarks and leptons carry spin $\tfrac{1}{2}$, and mediators carry spin 1. The inclusion of spin can be algebraically cumbersome; that's why I decided to introduce the Feynman calculus in the context of a “toy” theory free of such distractions. In *nonrelativistic* quantum mechanics particles are described by the *Schrödinger* equation; in *relativistic* quantum mechanics particles of spin 0 are described by the *Klein–Gordon* equation, particles of spin $\tfrac{1}{2}$ by the *Dirac* equation, and particles of spin 1 by the *Proca* equation. Once the Feynman rules have been established, however, the underlying field equation fades into the background—that's how we got through Chapter 6 without ever mentioning the Klein–Gordon equation. But for spin $\tfrac{1}{2}$ the very *notation* of the Feynman rules presupposes some familiarity with the Dirac equation. So for the next three sections we shall study the Dirac theory in its own right.

In Chapter 5 I “derived” the Schrödinger equation by starting with the *classical* energy-momentum relation:

<!-- PDF page 222; printed page 214 -->

$$
\frac{\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{2}}{2\mechanical{m}}+\energy{V}=\energy{E}
\tag{7.1}
$$

applying the quantum prescription

$$
\mechanical{\mathbf p}\to\frac{\energy{\hbar}}{i}\nabla,\qquad
\energy{E}\to i\energy{\hbar}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mechanical{t}}
\tag{7.2}
$$

and letting the resulting operator act on the “wave function,” $\signal{\psi}$:

$$
-\frac{\energy{\hbar}^{2}}{2\mechanical{m}}\nabla^{2}\signal{\Psi}
+\energy{V}\signal{\Psi}
=i\energy{\hbar}\frac{\partial\signal{\Psi}}{\partial\mechanical{t}}
\qquad\text{(Schrödinger equation)}.
\tag{7.3}
$$

The Klein–Gordon equation can be obtained in exactly the same way, beginning with the *relativistic* energy-momentum relation, $\energy{E}^{2}-\mechanical{\mathbf p}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}=\mechanical{m}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{4}$, or (better)

$$
\mechanical{p^\mu}\mechanical{p_\mu}-\mechanical{m}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}=0.
\tag{7.4}
$$

(I'll leave out the potential energy, from now on; we'll stick to *free* particles). Surprisingly, the quantum prescription (7.2) requires no relativistic modification; in four-vector notation, it reads

$$
\mechanical{p_\mu}\to i\energy{\hbar}\,\partial_\mu.
\tag{7.5}
$$

Here[^ep-7-1-gradient]

$$
\partial_\mu\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial\mechanical{x^\mu}}
\tag{7.6}
$$

which is to say

$$
\partial_0=\frac{1}{\mechanical{c}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mechanical{t}},\qquad
\partial_1=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mechanical{x}},\qquad
\partial_2=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mechanical{y}},\qquad
\partial_3=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mechanical{z}}.
\tag{7.7}
$$

Putting (7.5) into (7.4), and letting the derivatives act on a wave function $\signal{\psi}$, we obtain

$$
-\energy{\hbar}^{2}\partial^\mu\partial_\mu\signal{\psi}
-\mechanical{m}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}\signal{\psi}=0
\tag{7.8}
$$

or

$$
-\frac{1}{\mechanical{c}^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\signal{\psi}}{\partial\mechanical{t}^{2}}
+\nabla^{2}\signal{\psi}
=\left(\frac{\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}}{\energy{\hbar}}\right)^{2}\signal{\psi}
\qquad\text{(Klein–Gordon equation)}.
\tag{7.9}
$$

Schrödinger apparently discovered this equation even before the nonrelativistic one that bears his name; it was eventually rejected on the ground that (for reasons we need not go into) it was incompatible with the statistical interpretation of $\signal{\psi}$ [which says that $\lvert\signal{\psi}\rvert^{2}$ is the probability of finding the particle at the point ($\mechanical{x}$, $\mechanical{y}$, $\mechanical{z}$)]. The source of the difficulty was traced to the fact that the Klein–Gordon equation is second order in $\mechanical{t}$.[^ep-7-1-first] So Dirac set out to find an equation consistent with the relativistic energy-momentum formula, and yet first order in time. Ironically, in 1934 Pauli and Weisskopf showed that the statistical interpretation

<!-- PDF page 223; printed page 215 -->

itself is flawed in relativistic quantum theory,[^ep-7-1-pairs] and restored the Klein–Gordon equation to its rightful place, while keeping the Dirac equation for particles of spin $\tfrac{1}{2}$.

Dirac's basic strategy was to “factor” the energy-momentum relation (7.4). This would be easy if we had only $\mechanical{p^0}$ (that is, if $\mechanical{\mathbf p}$ were zero):

$$
(\mechanical{p^0})^{2}-\mechanical{m}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}
=(\mechanical{p^0}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})(\mechanical{p^0}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})=0.
\tag{7.10}
$$

We then obtain two first-order equations:

$$
(\mechanical{p^0}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})=0
\quad\text{or}\quad
(\mechanical{p^0}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})=0
\tag{7.11}
$$

either one of which guarantees that $\mechanical{p^\mu}\mechanical{p_\mu}-\mechanical{m}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}=0$. But it's a different matter when the other three components of $\mechanical{p^\mu}$ are included; in that case we are looking for something of the form

$$
(\mechanical{p^\mu}\mechanical{p_\mu}-\mechanical{m}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2})
=(\beta^\kappa\mechanical{p_\kappa}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})
(\gamma^\lambda\mechanical{p_\lambda}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})
\tag{7.12}
$$

where $\beta^\kappa$ and $\gamma^\lambda$ are eight coefficients to be determined.[^ep-7-1-longhand] Multiplying out the right-hand side, we have

$$
\beta^\kappa\gamma^\lambda\mechanical{p_\kappa}\mechanical{p_\lambda}
-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}(\beta^\kappa-\gamma^\kappa)\mechanical{p_\kappa}
-\mechanical{m}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}.
$$

We don't want any terms linear in $\mechanical{p_\kappa}$, so we must choose $\beta^\kappa=\gamma^\kappa$; to finish the job, we need to find coefficients $\gamma^\kappa$ such that

$$
\mechanical{p^\mu}\mechanical{p_\mu}=\gamma^\kappa\gamma^\lambda\mechanical{p_\kappa}\mechanical{p_\lambda}
$$

which is to say

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\mechanical{p^0})^{2}-(\mechanical{p^1})^{2}-(\mechanical{p^2})^{2}-(\mechanical{p^3})^{2}
={}&(\gamma^0)^{2}(\mechanical{p^0})^{2}
+(\gamma^1)^{2}(\mechanical{p^1})^{2}
+(\gamma^2)^{2}(\mechanical{p^2})^{2}
+(\gamma^3)^{2}(\mechanical{p^3})^{2}\\
&+(\gamma^0\gamma^1+\gamma^1\gamma^0)\mechanical{p_0}\mechanical{p_1}\\
&+(\gamma^0\gamma^2+\gamma^2\gamma^0)\mechanical{p_0}\mechanical{p_2}
+(\gamma^0\gamma^3+\gamma^3\gamma^0)\mechanical{p_0}\mechanical{p_3}\\
&+(\gamma^1\gamma^2+\gamma^2\gamma^1)\mechanical{p_1}\mechanical{p_2}
+(\gamma^1\gamma^3+\gamma^3\gamma^1)\mechanical{p_1}\mechanical{p_3}\\
&+(\gamma^2\gamma^3+\gamma^3\gamma^2)\mechanical{p_2}\mechanical{p_3}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{7.13}
$$

You see the problem: we could pick $\gamma^0=1$ and $\gamma^1=\gamma^2=\gamma^3=i$, but there doesn't seem to be any way to get rid of the “cross-terms”. At this point Dirac had a brilliant inspiration: what if the $\gamma$'s are *matrices*, instead of *numbers*? Since matrices don't commute, we just might be able to find a set such that

$$
\begin{gathered}
(\gamma^0)^{2}=1,\qquad
(\gamma^1)^{2}=(\gamma^2)^{2}=(\gamma^3)^{2}=-1,\\
\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu+\gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu=0,\qquad\text{for }\mu\ne\nu.
\end{gathered}
\tag{7.14}
$$

Or, more succinctly,

$$
\{\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu\}=2g^{\mu\nu}
\tag{7.15}
$$

<!-- PDF page 224; printed page 216 -->

where $g^{\mu\nu}$ is the Minkowski metric [eq. (3.13)], and curly brackets denote the *anticommutator*:

$$
\{A,B\}\equiv AB+BA.
\tag{7.16}
$$

You might try fiddling with this problem for yourself. It turns out that it can be done, although the smallest matrices that work are $4\times4$. There are a number of essentially equivalent sets of “gamma matrices”; we'll use the standard “Bjorken and Drell” convention:<sup>1</sup>

$$
\gamma^0=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\gamma^i=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sigma^i\\-\sigma^i&0\end{pmatrix},
\tag{7.17}
$$

where $\sigma^i$ ($i=1,2,3$) is the indicated Pauli matrix [eq. 4.26], 1 denotes the $2\times2$ unit matrix, and 0 is the $2\times2$ matrix of zeroes.[^ep-7-1-matrices]

As a $4\times4$ matrix equation, then, the relativistic energy-momentum relation *does* factor:

$$
(\mechanical{p^\mu}\mechanical{p_\mu}-\mechanical{m}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2})
=(\gamma^\kappa\mechanical{p_\kappa}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})
(\gamma^\lambda\mechanical{p_\lambda}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})=0.
\tag{7.18}
$$

We obtain the Dirac equation, now, by peeling off one term (it doesn't really matter which one, but this is the conventional choice—see Problem 7.10):

$$
\gamma^\mu\mechanical{p_\mu}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}=0.
\tag{7.19}
$$

Now we make the usual substitution $\mechanical{p_\mu}\to i\energy{\hbar}\,\partial_\mu$ [eq. (7.5)] and let the result act on the wave function $\signal{\psi}$:

$$
i\energy{\hbar}\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\signal{\psi}
-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}\signal{\psi}=0
\qquad\text{(Dirac equation)}.
\tag{7.20}
$$

Note that $\signal{\psi}$ is now a four-element column matrix:

$$
\signal{\psi}=\begin{pmatrix}\signal{\psi_1}\\\signal{\psi_2}\\\signal{\psi_3}\\\signal{\psi_4}\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{7.21}
$$

We call it a “bi-spinor,” or “Dirac spinor.” (Although it carries four components, this object is *not* a four-vector. In Section 7.3 I'll show you how it *does* transform when you change inertial systems; it's *not* going to be an ordinary Lorentz transformation.)

[^ep-7-1-gradient]: The gradient with respect to a *contravariant* position-time four-vector $\mechanical{x^\mu}$ is itself a *covariant* four-vector, hence the placement of the index. Written out in full, equation (7.5) says $(\energy{E}/\mechanical{c},-\mechanical{\mathbf p})\to i\energy{\hbar}((1/\mechanical{c})(\partial/\partial\mechanical{t}),\nabla)$. Of course, $\partial^\mu=\partial/\partial\mechanical{x_\mu}$ (See Problem 7.1.)
[^ep-7-1-first]: Notice that the Schrödinger equation (7.3) is *first* order in $\mechanical{t}$.
[^ep-7-1-pairs]: The essential point is that a relativistic theory must account for pair production and annihilation, and hence the number of particles is not a conserved quantity.
[^ep-7-1-longhand]: In case the notation confuses you, I'll write equation (7.12) out “long-hand”:

    $$
    \begin{aligned}
    &(\mechanical{p^0})^{2}-(\mechanical{p^1})^{2}-(\mechanical{p^2})^{2}-(\mechanical{p^3})^{2}-\mechanical{m}^{2}\mechanical{c}^{2}\\
    &\qquad=(\beta^0\mechanical{p^0}-\beta^1\mechanical{p^1}-\beta^2\mechanical{p^2}-\beta^3\mechanical{p^3}+\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c})
    (\gamma^0\mechanical{p^0}-\gamma^1\mechanical{p^1}-\gamma^2\mechanical{p^2}-\gamma^3\mechanical{p^3}-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}).
    \end{aligned}
    $$

[^ep-7-1-matrices]: When the context allows no room for ambiguity, I'll use 1 and 0 this way for $2\times2$ or $4\times4$ matrices; also, a unit matrix of the appropriate dimension is *implied*, when necessary, as on the right-hand side of equation (7.15). Incidentally, since $\sigma$ is not the spatial part of a four-vector, we do not distinguish upper and lower indices: $\sigma^i=\sigma_i$.
