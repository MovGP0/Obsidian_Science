> [!TLDR]
> Color factors give attractive singlet and triplet channels and repulsive octet and sextet channels at short range.

<!-- PDF page 292; printed page 284 -->

In this section we consider the interaction between two quarks (also a quark and an antiquark) in lowest-order QCD. Of course, we cannot observe quark-quark scattering directly in the laboratory (although hadron-hadron scattering is an indirect manifestation), so we won't be looking for cross sections here. Instead, we concentrate on the effective potentials between quarks—the QCD analog of the Coulomb potential in electrodynamics. We used such potentials, with a promise to derive them later, back in Chapter 5, in the analysis of quarkonium. Bear in mind that this is a perturbation theory calculation, valid only insofar as the coupling $\alpha_s$ is small. We cannot hope to get the confining term in the potential by this route—we are implicitly relying on asymptotic freedom, and all we're going to find is the short-range behavior. Nevertheless, we will obtain a very suggestive result: *Quarks attract one another most strongly when they are in the color singlet configuration* (indeed, in other arrangements they generally repel). At very short range, then, the color singlet is the “maximally attractive channel”—an indication that binding is more likely, at least, for singlet states.[^ep-9-2-binding]

## Case 1: Quark and Antiquark

Consider first the interaction of a quark and an antiquark, in QCD. We shall assume that they have different flavors, so the only diagram (in lowest order) is the one in Figure 9.1,[^ep-9-2-same-flavor] representing, for instance, $u+\bar d\to u+\bar d$. The amplitude is given by

<!-- PDF page 293; printed page 285 -->

![[elementary-particles-fig-9-1-quark-antiquark.svg|**Fig. 9.1** The quark–antiquark interaction.]]

$$
\begin{aligned}
-i\mathcal M={}&[\signal{\bar u}(3)\signal{c_3}^\dagger]
\left[-i\frac{g_s}{2}\lambda^\alpha\gamma^\mu\right]
[\signal{u}(1)\signal{c_1}]
\left[\frac{-ig_{\mu\nu}\delta^{\alpha\beta}}{\mechanical{q}^2}\right]\\
&\times[\signal{\bar v}(2)\signal{c_2}^\dagger]
\left[-i\frac{g_s}{2}\lambda^\beta\gamma^\nu\right]
[\signal{v}(4)\signal{c_4}]
\end{aligned}
\tag{9.20}
$$

Thus

$$
\mathcal M=\frac{-g_s^2}{4}\frac1{\mechanical{q}^2}
[\signal{\bar u}(3)\gamma^\mu\signal{u}(1)]
[\signal{\bar v}(2)\gamma_\mu\signal{v}(4)]
(\signal{c_3}^\dagger\lambda^\alpha\signal{c_1})
(\signal{c_2}^\dagger\lambda^\alpha\signal{c_4})
\tag{9.21}
$$

(summation over $\alpha$ implied). This is exactly what we had for electron-positron scattering (7.106), except that $\electric{g_e}$ is replaced by $g_s$ (of course), and we have in addition the “color factor”

$$
f=\tfrac14(\signal{c_3}^\dagger\lambda^\alpha\signal{c_1})
(\signal{c_2}^\dagger\lambda^\alpha\signal{c_4})
\tag{9.22}
$$

The *potential* describing the $q\bar q$ interaction is, therefore, the same as that acting in electrodynamics between two opposite charges (to wit: the Coulomb potential), only with $\electric{\alpha}$ replaced by $f\alpha_s$:

$$
\energy{V_{q\bar q}}(\mechanical{r})
=-f\frac{(\alpha_s\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c})}{\mechanical{r}}
\tag{9.23}
$$

Now, the color factor itself depends on the color state of the interacting quarks. From a quark and an antiquark we can make a color singlet (9.5) and a color octet (9.4) (all members of which yield the same $f$). I'll calculate the octet color factor first, because it's a little easier.<sup>2</sup>

### Example 9.1 Color Factor for Octet Configuration

A typical octet state (9.4) is $r\bar b$ (any of the others would do just as well; see Problem 9.6). Here the incoming quark is red, and the incoming antiquark is antiblue. Because color is conserved, the outgoing quark must also be red and the antiquark antiblue. Thus

$$
\signal{c_1}=\signal{c_3}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\signal{c_2}=\signal{c_4}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}
$$

and hence

$$
f=\frac14
\left[(1\;0\;0)\lambda^\alpha\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}\right]
\left[(0\;1\;0)\lambda^\alpha\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}\right]
=\tfrac14\lambda^\alpha_{11}\lambda^\alpha_{22}
$$

<!-- PDF page 294; printed page 286 -->

A glance at the $\lambda$ matrices reveals that the only ones with entries in the 11 and 22 positions are $\lambda^3$ and $\lambda^8$. So

$$
f=\tfrac14(\lambda^3_{11}\lambda^3_{22}+\lambda^8_{11}\lambda^8_{22})
=\tfrac14[(1)(-1)+(1/\sqrt3)(1/\sqrt3)]=-\tfrac16
\tag{9.24}
$$

### Example 9.2 Color Factor for Singlet Configuration

The color singlet state is (9.5)

$$
(1/\sqrt3)(r\bar r+b\bar b+g\bar g)
$$

If the incoming quarks are in the singlet state (as they would be for a meson, say) the color factor is a sum of three terms:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f=\frac14\cdot\frac1{\sqrt3}\biggl\{
&\left[\signal{c_3}^\dagger\lambda^\alpha
\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}\right]
[(1\;0\;0)\lambda^\alpha\signal{c_4}]\\
&+\left[\signal{c_3}^\dagger\lambda^\alpha
\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}\right]
[(0\;1\;0)\lambda^\alpha\signal{c_4}]\\
&+\left[\signal{c_3}^\dagger\lambda^\alpha
\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}\right]
[(0\;0\;1)\lambda^\alpha\signal{c_4}]
\biggr\}
\end{aligned}
$$

The outgoing quarks are necessarily also in the singlet state, and we get *nine* terms in all, which can be written compactly as follows:

$$
f=\frac14\cdot\frac1{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac1{\sqrt3}
(\lambda^\alpha_{ij}\lambda^\alpha_{ji})
=\frac1{12}\operatorname{Tr}(\lambda^\alpha\lambda^\alpha)
\tag{9.25}
$$

(summation over $i$ and $j$, from 1 to 3, implied in the second expression). Now

$$
\operatorname{Tr}(\lambda^\alpha\lambda^\beta)=2\delta^{\alpha\beta}
\tag{9.26}
$$

(Problem 9.3), so, with the summation over $\alpha$,

$$
\operatorname{Tr}(\lambda^\alpha\lambda^\alpha)=16
\tag{9.27}
$$

Evidently, then, for the color singlet

$$
f=\tfrac43
\tag{9.28}
$$

Putting equations (9.24) and (9.28) into equation (9.23), we conclude that the quark-antiquark potentials are

$$
\energy{V_{q\bar q}}(\mechanical{r})
=-\frac43\frac{(\alpha_s\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c})}{\mechanical{r}}
\qquad\text{(color singlet)}
\tag{9.29}
$$

$$
\energy{V_{q\bar q}}(\mechanical{r})
=\frac16\frac{(\alpha_s\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c})}{\mechanical{r}}
\qquad\text{(color octet)}
\tag{9.30}
$$

From the signs we see that the force is *attractive* in the color singlet but *repulsive* for the octet. This helps to explain why quark-antiquark binding (to form mesons) occurs in the singlet configuration but not in the octet (which would have produced colored mesons).

## Case 2: Quark and Quark

We turn now to the interaction of two quarks. Again, we shall assume that they have different flavors, so the only diagram (in lowest order) is the one indicated in Figure 9.2,[^ep-9-2-identical] representing, say, $u+d\to u+d$. The amplitude is

$$
\mathcal M=\frac{-g_s^2}{4}\frac1{\mechanical{q}^2}
[\signal{\bar u}(3)\gamma^\mu\signal{u}(1)]
[\signal{\bar u}(4)\gamma_\mu\signal{u}(2)]
(\signal{c_3}^\dagger\lambda^\alpha\signal{c_1})
(\signal{c_4}^\dagger\lambda^\alpha\signal{c_2})
\tag{9.31}
$$

This is the same as for electron-muon scattering (7.104), except that $\electric{g_e}$ is replaced by $g_s$, and there is a color factor

$$
f=\tfrac14(\signal{c_3}^\dagger\lambda^\alpha\signal{c_1})
(\signal{c_4}^\dagger\lambda^\alpha\signal{c_2})
\tag{9.32}
$$

The potential, therefore, takes the same form as that for like charges in electrodynamics:

$$
\energy{V_{qq}}(\mechanical{r})
=f\frac{(\alpha_s\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c})}{\mechanical{r}}
\tag{9.33}
$$

Again, the color factor depends on the configuration of the quarks. From two quarks, however, you can't make a singlet and an octet (as for $q\bar q$)—rather, we obtain a triplet (the antisymmetric combinations):

$$
\left\{
\begin{gathered}
(rb-br)/\sqrt2\\
bg-gb)/\sqrt2\\
(gr-rg)/\sqrt2
\end{gathered}
\right\}
\qquad\text{(triplet)}
\tag{9.34}
$$

and a sextet (the symmetric combinations):[^ep-9-2-representations]

$$
\left\{
\begin{gathered}
rr,\quad bb,\quad gg,\\
(rb+br)/\sqrt2,\quad(bg+gb)/\sqrt2,\quad(gr+rg)/\sqrt2
\end{gathered}
\right\}
\qquad\text{(sextet)}
\tag{9.35}
$$

<!-- PDF page 296; printed page 288 -->

### Example 9.3 Color Factor for Sextet Configuration

A typical sextet state is $rr$ (use any of the others if you prefer—you'll get the same result for $f$). In this case

$$
\signal{c_1}=\signal{c_2}=\signal{c_3}=\signal{c_4}
=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}
$$

and hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
f&=\frac14
\left[(1\;0\;0)\lambda^\alpha\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}\right]
\left[(1\;0\;0)\lambda^\alpha\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}\right]
=\frac14(\lambda^\alpha_{11}\lambda^\alpha_{11})\\
&=\frac14[\lambda^3_{11}\lambda^3_{11}+\lambda^8_{11}\lambda^8_{11}]
=\frac14\left[(1)(1)+\left(\frac1{\sqrt3}\right)\left(\frac1{\sqrt3}\right)\right]\\
&=\frac13
\end{aligned}
\tag{9.36}
$$

### Example 9.4 Color Factor for Triplet Configuration

A typical triplet state is $(rb-br)/\sqrt2$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
f=\frac14\cdot\frac1{\sqrt2}\cdot\frac1{\sqrt2}\biggl\{
&\left[(1\;0\;0)\lambda^\alpha\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}\right]
\left[(0\;1\;0)\lambda^\alpha\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}\right]\\
&-\left[(0\;1\;0)\lambda^\alpha\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}\right]
\left[(1\;0\;0)\lambda^\alpha\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}\right]\\
&-\left[(1\;0\;0)\lambda^\alpha\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}\right]
\left[(0\;1\;0)\lambda^\alpha\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}\right]\\
&-\left[(0\;1\;0)\lambda^\alpha\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}\right]
\left[(1\;0\;0)\lambda^\alpha\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}\right]
\biggr\}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
&=\tfrac18\{\lambda^\alpha_{11}\lambda^\alpha_{22}
-\lambda^\alpha_{21}\lambda^\alpha_{12}
-\lambda^\alpha_{12}\lambda^\alpha_{21}
+\lambda^\alpha_{22}\lambda^\alpha_{11}\}\\
&=\tfrac14(\lambda^\alpha_{11}\lambda^\alpha_{22}
-\lambda^\alpha_{12}\lambda^\alpha_{21})\\
&=\tfrac14(\lambda^3_{11}\lambda^3_{22}
+\lambda^8_{11}\lambda^8_{22}
-\lambda^1_{12}\lambda^1_{21}
-\lambda^2_{12}\lambda^2_{21})\\
&=\tfrac14(-1+\tfrac13-1-1)=-\tfrac23
\end{aligned}
\tag{9.37}
$$

Putting equations (9.36) and (9.37) into equation (9.33), we conclude that the quark-quark potentials are

$$
\energy{V_{qq}}(\mechanical{r})
=-\frac23\frac{(\alpha_s\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c})}{\mechanical{r}}
\qquad\text{(color triplet)}
\tag{9.38}
$$

$$
\energy{V_{qq}}(\mechanical{r})
=\frac13\frac{(\alpha_s\energy{\hbar}\mechanical{c})}{\mechanical{r}}
\qquad\text{(color sextet)}
\tag{9.39}
$$

In particular, the signs indicate that the force is *attractive* for the triplet and *repulsive* for the sextet. Of course, that's not too helpful as it stands, because

<!-- PDF page 297; printed page 289 -->

*neither* combination occurs in nature.[^ep-9-2-diquarks] However, it does have interesting implications for the binding of three quarks. This time we can make a singlet (completely antisymmetric), a decuplet (completely symmetric), and two octets (of mixed symmetry), as we found in Section 5.9 of Chapter 5.[^ep-9-2-three-quarks] Since the singlet is completely antisymmetric, every pair of quarks is in the (antisymmetric) triplet state—the *attractive* channel. In the decuplet, every pair is in the (symmetric) sextet state—they *repel*. As for the two octets, some pairs are triplet and some are sextet; we expect some attraction, then, and some repulsion. *Only in the singlet configuration*, though, *do we get complete mutual attraction* of the three quarks. Again, this is a comforting result: as in the case of mesons, the potential is most favorable for binding when the quarks are in the color singlet configuration.

[^ep-9-2-binding]: This is a very pleasing conclusion, but it does *not* prove that binding *must* occur in the color singlet, or that it *cannot* occur in other configurations. For this we would have to know the *long*-range behavior of the potential, about which, at present, we can only speculate.

[^ep-9-2-same-flavor]: In principle, for the *same* flavor (e.g., $u+u\to u+u$) we should include a second diagram:

    ![[elementary-particles-qcd-equal-flavor-annihilation.svg|Additional annihilation diagram for equal quark flavors.]]

    However, in the nonrelativistic limit of interest here this second diagram does not contribute anyway (see footnote, page 234), so in *practice* what we're doing applies just as well *whatever* the quark flavors. (See also Problem 9.7.)

[^ep-9-2-identical]: For identical quarks there is also the “crossed” diagram:

    ![[elementary-particles-qcd-identical-quarks-crossed.svg|Crossed exchange diagram for identical quarks.]]

    However, inclusion of this diagram, together with the statistical factor $S$ in the cross-section formula, leads to the same nonrelativistic limit (see footnote page 234), so in fact our potentials are correct even for same-flavor quarks.

[^ep-9-2-representations]: In group theoretical language, $3\otimes\bar3=1\oplus8$, but $3\otimes3=\bar3\oplus6$.

[^ep-9-2-diquarks]: If you don't heed the warning in footnote (*) on p. 284, you may be alarmed to find that two quarks in the triplet state attract one another. There is some comfort in the observation that the singlet $q\bar q$ coupling is *twice as strong*; but still, if this were the whole story we might well expect triplet $qq$ binding to occur, leading to free “diquark” states. There has, in fact, been some speculation about the possible existence of diquarks within nuclei.<sup>3</sup>

[^ep-9-2-three-quarks]: In Chapter 5 we were dealing with *flavor*, not *color*, but the *mathematics* is the same. Group theoretically, $3\otimes3\otimes3=1\oplus8\oplus8\oplus10$.
