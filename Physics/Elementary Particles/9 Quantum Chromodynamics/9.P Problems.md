## Problem 9.1

Why can't the “ninth gluon” be the photon? [*Answer:* The gluon would couple to all baryons with the same strength, not (as the photon does) in proportion to their charge. Since the mass and baryon numbers are approximately proportional in bulk matter, such a force would, in fact, look very much like an extra contribution to gravity. There was a flurry of interest in this possibility in early 1986. (E. Fischbach et al., *Phys. Rev. Lett.* **56**, 3 (1986). See, however, the comments in *Phys. Rev. Lett.* **56**, 2423 (1986).]

## Problem 9.2

Color $SU(3)$ transformations relabel “red,” “blue,” and “green” according to the transformation rule

$$
\signal{c}\to\signal{c'}=U\signal{c}
$$

where $U$ is any unitary $(UU^\dagger=1)$ $3\times3$ matrix of determinant 1, and $\signal{c}$ is a three-element column vector. For example

$$
U=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&1\\1&0&0\end{pmatrix}
$$

would take $r\to b$, $b\to g$, $g\to r$. The ninth gluon $(|9\rangle)$ is obviously invariant under $U$, but the octet gluons are *not*. Show that $|7\rangle$ and $|8\rangle$ go into linear combinations of one another:

$$
|7'\rangle=\alpha|7\rangle+\beta|8\rangle,\qquad
|8'\rangle=\gamma|7\rangle+\delta|8\rangle
$$

Find the numbers $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$, and $\delta$.

## Problem 9.11

There is a simple test for the gauge invariance of an amplitude $(\mathcal M)$ in QCD (or QED): Replace any gluon (or photon) polarization vector by its momentum $(\epsilon_3\to\mechanical{p_3}$, say), and you must get zero (see Problem 7.21). Show using this criterion that $\mathcal M=\mathcal M_1+\mathcal M_2+\mathcal M_3$ is gauge-invariant, but $\mathcal M_1+\mathcal M_2$ alone is *not*. [Thus the three-gluon vertex is essential in QCD to preserve gauge invariance. Notice, by contrast, that $\mathcal M_1+\mathcal M_2$ alone is gauge-invariant in QED (Example 7.8). The fact that $\lambda$ matrices do not commute makes the difference.]

## Problem 9.12

Construct the color singlet combination of two gluons (9.60). [One method is as follows:

Let

$$
\signal{c}=\begin{pmatrix}r\\b\\g\end{pmatrix}
$$

Under $SU(3)$, $\signal{c}\to\signal{c'}=U\signal{c}$, where $U$ is a unitary $(UU^\dagger=1)$ matrix of determinant 1. Similarly, let $\signal{d^\dagger}=(\bar r,\bar b,\bar g)$, transforming by the rule $\signal{d^\dagger}\to\signal{d'^\dagger}=\signal{d^\dagger}U^\dagger$. Form the matrix

$$
M\equiv\signal{c}\signal{d^\dagger}
=\begin{pmatrix}
r\bar r&r\bar b&r\bar g\\
b\bar r&b\bar b&b\bar g\\
g\bar r&g\bar b&g\bar g
\end{pmatrix}
$$

Note that $M'=\signal{c'}\signal{d'^\dagger}=UMU^\dagger$.
Remove the trace:

$$
N\equiv M-\frac13[\operatorname{Tr}(M)],\quad\text{so that }\operatorname{Tr}(N)=0
$$

> [!note]
> Note that $\operatorname{Tr}(M')=\operatorname{Tr}(M)=(r\bar r+b\bar b+g\bar g)$, so this combination is $SU(3)$-invariant; it is the *singlet* combination in $3\otimes\bar3=1\oplus8$, $N$ is the octet.

$$
N'=M'-\frac13[\operatorname{Tr}(M')]
=UMU^\dagger-\frac13[\operatorname{Tr}(M)]UU^\dagger
=UNU^\dagger
$$

The question is how to put together two 8's to make a 1; that is, how to make something bilinear in $N_1$ and $N_2$ which is invariant under $U$. The solution is

$$
s\equiv\operatorname{Tr}(N_1N_2)
$$

<!-- PDF page 307; printed page 299 -->

For

$$
s'=\operatorname{Tr}(N'_1N'_2)
=\operatorname{Tr}(UN_1U^\dagger UN_2U^\dagger)
=\operatorname{Tr}(U^\dagger UN_1N_2)
=\operatorname{Tr}(N_1N_2)=s.
$$

It remains to figure out what $s$ is in terms of the elements of $M_1$ and $M_2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Tr}(N_1N_2)
&=\operatorname{Tr}\left\{\left(M_1-\frac13[\operatorname{Tr}(M_1)]\right)
\left(M_2-\frac13[\operatorname{Tr}(M_2)]\right)\right\}\\
&=\operatorname{Tr}(M_1M_2)-\frac13[\operatorname{Tr}(M_1)][\operatorname{Tr}(M_2)]\\
&=\frac23[(r\bar r)_1(r\bar r)_2+(b\bar b)_1(b\bar b)_2+(g\bar g)_1(g\bar g)_2]\\
&\quad-\frac13[(r\bar r)_1(b\bar b)_2+(r\bar r)_1(g\bar g)_2
+(b\bar b)_1(r\bar r)_2+(b\bar b)_1(g\bar g)_2\\
&\qquad+(g\bar g)_1(r\bar r)_2+(g\bar g)_1(b\bar b)_2]\\
&\quad+[(r\bar b)_1(b\bar r)_2+(r\bar g)_1(g\bar r)_2\\
&\qquad+(b\bar r)_1(r\bar b)_2+(b\bar g)_1(g\bar b)_2
+(g\bar r)_1(r\bar g)_2+(g\bar b)_1(b\bar g)_2]\\
&=|1\rangle_1|1\rangle_2+|2\rangle_1|2\rangle_2
+|3\rangle_1|3\rangle_2+|4\rangle_1|4\rangle_2\\
&\quad+|5\rangle_1|5\rangle_2+|6\rangle_1|6\rangle_2
+|7\rangle_1|7\rangle_2+|8\rangle_1|8\rangle_2
=\sum_{n=1}^{8}|n\rangle_1|n\rangle_2
\end{aligned}
$$

This—the invariant product of two octets—is the $SU(3)$ analog to the dot product of two 3-vectors in $SU(2)$.]
