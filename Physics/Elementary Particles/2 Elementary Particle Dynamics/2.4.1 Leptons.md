> [!TLDR]
> Charged weak interactions connect a lepton to its neutrino; neutral weak interactions preserve the lepton species.

<!-- PDF page 73; printed page 65 -->

The fundamental charged vertex looks like this:

![[elementary-particles-weak-lepton-charged-vertex.svg|The fundamental charged weak vertex for a lepton.]]

<!-- PDF page 74; printed page 66 -->

A negative lepton (it could be $e^-$, $\mu^-$, or $\tau^-$) converts into the corresponding neutrino, with emission of a $W^-$ (or absorption of a $W^+$): $l^-\to\nu_l+W^-$.[^ep-2-4-1-positive] As always, we combine the primitive vertices together to generate more complicated reactions. For example, the process $\mu^-+\nu_e\to e^-+\nu_\mu$ would be represented by the diagram:

![[elementary-particles-weak-neutrino-muon-scattering.svg|Charged weak scattering of a muon and an electron neutrino.]]

Such a neutrino-muon scattering event would be hard to set up in the laboratory, but with a slight twist essentially the same diagram describes the decay of the muon, $\mu^-\to e^-+\nu_\mu+\bar\nu_e$, which happens all the time:

![[elementary-particles-weak-muon-decay.svg|The lowest-order muon decay diagram.]]

(Technically, this is only the lowest-order contribution to muon decay, but in weak interaction theory one almost never needs to consider higher-order corrections.)

The fundamental neutral vertex is:

![[elementary-particles-weak-lepton-neutral-vertex.svg|The fundamental neutral weak vertex for a lepton.]]

In this case $l$ can be any lepton (including neutrinos). The $Z$ mediates such processes as neutrino-electron scattering ($\nu_\mu+e^-\to\nu_\mu+e^-$):

![[elementary-particles-weak-neutrino-electron-scattering.svg|Neutral weak scattering of a muon neutrino and an electron.]]

Although charged weak processes were recognized from the start (beta decay itself is a charged process), the theoretical possibility of neutral weak processes was not appreciated until 1958. The Glashow-Weinberg-Salam (GWS) model

<!-- PDF page 75; printed page 67 -->

includes neutral weak processes as essential ingredients, and their existence was confirmed experimentally at CERN in 1973.<sup>5</sup> The reason it took so long for neutral weak processes to be discovered is twofold: (1) nobody was looking for them and (2) they tend to be masked by much stronger electromagnetic effects. For example, the $Z$ can be exchanged between two electrons, but so can the photon:

![[elementary-particles-weak-z-photon-electron-scattering.svg|The source Z-exchange and photon-exchange contributions to electron-electron scattering.]]

Presumably there is a minute correction to Coulomb’s law that’s attributable to the first diagram, but the photon-mediated process overwhelmingly dominates. Experiments at DESY (in Hamburg) studied the reaction $e^-+e^+\to\mu^-+\mu^+$ at very high energy and found unmistakable evidence of a contribution from the $Z$.<sup>6</sup> But to observe a *pure* neutral weak interaction one has to go to neutrino scattering, in which there is no competing electromagnetic mechanism, and neutrino experiments are notoriously difficult.

[^ep-2-4-1-positive]: This implies, of course that $l^+\to\bar\nu_l+W^+$ is also an allowed vertex.
