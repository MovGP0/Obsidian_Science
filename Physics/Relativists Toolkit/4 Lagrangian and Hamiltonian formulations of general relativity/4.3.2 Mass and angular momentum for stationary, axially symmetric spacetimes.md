> [!TLDR]
> For a stationary axially symmetric asymptotic metric, the Hamiltonian definitions recover its mass and angular-momentum parameters.

<!-- Source: PDF page 164, printed page 148. -->

To show that these definitions are in fact reasonable, we shall calculate $\mechanical{M}$ and $\mechanical{J}$ for an asymptotically-flat spacetime that is both stationary and axially symmetric. In the asymptotic region $\mechanical{r}\gg\mechanical{m}$, the metric of such a spacetime can be expressed as

$$
\mathrm d\mechanical{s}^2=-\left(1-\frac{2\mechanical{m}}{\mechanical{r}}\right)\mathrm d\mechanical{t}^2
+\left(1+\frac{2\mechanical{m}}{\mechanical{r}}\right)(\mathrm d\mechanical{r}^2+\mechanical{r}^2\mathrm d\Omega^2)
-\frac{4\mechanical{j}\sin^2\mechanical{\theta}}{\mechanical{r}}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{t}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{\phi}, \tag{4.83}
$$

where $\mechanical{m}$ and $\mechanical{j}$ are the spacetime’s mass and angular-momentum parameters, respectively. We will show that $\mechanical{M}=\mechanical{m}$ and $\mechanical{J}=\mechanical{j}$, and thus confirm that the Hamiltonian definitions are well founded. We note that the validity of this metric in the asymptotic region could always be used to define mass and angular momentum. Our Hamiltonian definitions are more powerful, however, because they do not involve a particular coordinate system, and they stay meaningful even when the spacetime is not stationary or axially symmetric.

We choose the hypersurfaces $\Sigma_t$ to be surfaces of constant $\mechanical{t}$, and $n_\alpha=-(1-\mechanical{m}/\mechanical{r})\partial_\alpha\mechanical{t}$ is the unit normal. (Throughout this calculation we work consistently to first order in $\mechanical{m}/\mechanical{r}$.) The induced metric on $\Sigma_t$ is given by

$$
h_{ab}\mathrm dy^a\mathrm dy^b=\left(1+\frac{2\mechanical{m}}{\mechanical{r}}\right)(\mathrm d\mechanical{r}^2+\mechanical{r}^2\mathrm d\Omega^2).
$$

The boundary $S_t$ is the two-sphere $\mechanical{r}=\mechanical{R}$, and the limit $\mechanical{R}\to\infty$ will be taken at the end of the calculation. Its unit normal is $r_a=(1+\mechanical{m}/\mechanical{r})\partial_a\mechanical{r}$ and

$$
\sigma_{AB}\mathrm d\theta^A\mathrm d\theta^B=\left(1+\frac{2\mechanical{m}}{\mechanical{R}}\right)\mechanical{R}^2\mathrm d\Omega^2
$$

gives the two-metric on $S_t$.

To evaluate $\mechanical{M}$ we must first calculate $k$. This is given by $k=r^a{}_{|a}$ and a brief calculation yields $k=2(1-2\mechanical{m}/\mechanical{R})/\mechanical{R}$. To this we must subtract $k_0$, the extrinsic curvature of a two-surface of *identical* intrinsic geometry, but embedded in flat space. On this surface,

<!-- Source: PDF page 165, printed page 149. -->

$$
\sigma^0_{AB}\mathrm d\theta^A\mathrm d\theta^B=\mechanical{R'}^2\mathrm d\Omega^2,
$$

where $\mechanical{R'}\equiv\mechanical{R}(1+\mechanical{m}/\mechanical{R})$ so that $\sigma^0_{AB}=\sigma_{AB}$. We have $k_0=2/\mechanical{R'}=2(1-\mechanical{m}/\mechanical{R})/\mechanical{R}$ and simple algebra yields $k-k_0=-2\mechanical{m}/\mechanical{R}^2$. On the other hand, $\sqrt{\sigma}\,\mathrm d^2\theta=\mechanical{R}^2(1+2\mechanical{m}/\mechanical{R})\sin\mechanical{\theta}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{\theta}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{\phi}$, and substitution into Eq. (4.81) yields

$$
\mechanical{M}=\mechanical{m}, \tag{4.84}
$$

the expected result.

To evaluate $\mechanical{J}$ we must first calculate $K_{ab}\phi^ar^b=K_{\phi r}(1-\mechanical{m}/\mechanical{r})$, where $K_{ab}=n_{\alpha;\beta}e^\alpha_ae^\beta_b$. (The second term in the integrand, $Kh_{ab}\phi^ar^b$, vanishes because the vectors $r^a$ and $\phi^a$ are orthogonal.) The relevant component of the extrinsic curvature is $K_{\phi r}=(1-\mechanical{m}/\mechanical{r})\Gamma^t_{\phi r}$. Using

$$
g^{tt}=-\left(1+\frac{2\mechanical{m}}{\mechanical{r}}\right),\qquad
g^{t\phi}=-\frac{2\mechanical{j}}{\mechanical{r}^3},
$$

we find that $\Gamma^t_{\phi r}=-3\mechanical{j}\sin^2\mechanical{\theta}/\mechanical{r}^2$ and this gives $K_{\phi r}=-3\mechanical{j}\sin^2\mechanical{\theta}/\mechanical{R}^2$. Substituting this into Eq. (4.82) yields $\mechanical{J}=(3\mechanical{j}/4)\int_0^\pi\sin^3\mechanical{\theta}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{\theta}$, or

$$
\mechanical{J}=\mechanical{j}, \tag{4.85}
$$

the expected result.
