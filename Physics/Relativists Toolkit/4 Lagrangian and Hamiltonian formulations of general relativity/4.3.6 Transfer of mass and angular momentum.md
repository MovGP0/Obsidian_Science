> [!TLDR]
> Contracting a conserved test stress-energy tensor with Killing vectors gives conserved energy and angular-momentum fluxes across hypersurfaces.

<!-- Source: PDF page 171, printed page 155. -->

We shall now derive expressions for the transfer of mass and angular momentum across a hypersurface $\Sigma$ in a stationary, axially symmetric spacetime.

To begin, consider the vector fields

$$
\energy{\varepsilon^\alpha}=-\energy{T^\alpha{}_\beta}\xi^\beta_{(t)},\qquad
\mechanical{\ell^\alpha}=\energy{T^\alpha{}_\beta}\xi^\beta_{(\phi)}, \tag{4.98}
$$

where $\energy{T^{\alpha\beta}}$ is a *test* stress-energy tensor that does not influence the spacetime geometry. According to the definition of the stress-energy tensor, $\energy{\varepsilon^\alpha}$ can be interpreted as an energy flux vector, while $\mechanical{\ell^\alpha}$ is interpreted as an angular-momentum flux vector.

To see this clearly, consider the simple case of dust, a perfect fluid with stress-energy tensor $\energy{T^{\alpha\beta}}=\energy{\rho}\mechanical{u^\alpha}\mechanical{u^\beta}$, where $\energy{\rho}$ is the rest-mass density and $\mechanical{u^\alpha}$ the four-velocity. Energy-momentum conservation implies that $\mechanical{u^\alpha}$ satisfies the geodesic equation, and that $\mechanical{j^\alpha}=\energy{\rho}\mechanical{u^\alpha}$ is a conserved vector: $\mechanical{j^\alpha}{}_{;\alpha}=0$. This vector can be interpreted as the dust’s momentum density, or equivalently, as a rest-mass flux vector. Then $\energy{\varepsilon^\alpha}=\energy{\tilde E}\mechanical{j^\alpha}$ and $\mechanical{\ell^\alpha}=\mechanical{\tilde L}\mechanical{j^\alpha}$, where $\energy{\tilde E}=-\mechanical{u_\alpha}\xi^\alpha_{(t)}$ is the conserved energy per unit rest mass and $\mechanical{\tilde L}=\mechanical{u_\alpha}\xi^\alpha_{(\phi)}$ the conserved angular momentum per unit rest mass. (As we have indicated, both $\energy{\tilde E}$ and $\mechanical{\tilde L}$ are constants of the motion.) These relations show quite clearly that $\energy{\varepsilon^\alpha}$ represents a flux of energy density, while $\mechanical{\ell^\alpha}$ is a flux of angular-momentum density.

The vectors $\energy{\varepsilon^\alpha}$ and $\mechanical{\ell^\alpha}$ are divergence-free. For example,

$$
\energy{\varepsilon^\alpha}{}_{;\alpha}
=-\energy{T^{\alpha\beta}}{}_{;\alpha}\xi_{(t)\beta}
+\energy{T^{\alpha\beta}}\xi_{(t)\beta;\alpha}=0;
$$

the first term vanishes by virtue of energy-momentum conservation, and the second vanishes because $\xi_{(t)\beta;\alpha}$ is an antisymmetric tensor field. This implies that the integral of $\energy{\varepsilon^\alpha}$ or $\mechanical{\ell^\alpha}$ over a hypersurface $\partial\mathcal V$ enclosing a four-dimensional region $\mathcal V$ is identically zero. For example,

$$
\oint_{\partial\mathcal V}\energy{\varepsilon^\alpha}\,\mathrm d\Sigma_\alpha=0.
$$

This equation states that the *total transfer* of energy across a closed hypersurface $\partial\mathcal V$ is zero. This is clearly a statement of conservation of total energy — or total mass.

<!-- Source: PDF page 172, printed page 156. -->

The boundary $\partial\mathcal V$ can be partitioned into any number of pieces. If one such piece is the hypersurface $\Sigma$, then the integral of $\energy{\varepsilon^\alpha}$ over $\Sigma$ represents the mass transferred across this piece of $\partial\mathcal V$. Thus,

$$
\mechanical{\Delta M}=-\int_\Sigma\energy{T^\alpha{}_\beta}\xi^\beta_{(t)}\,\mathrm d\Sigma_\alpha \tag{4.99}
$$

is the mass transferred across the hypersurface $\Sigma$, and similarly,

$$
\mechanical{\Delta J}=\int_\Sigma\energy{T^\alpha{}_\beta}\xi^\beta_{(\phi)}\,\mathrm d\Sigma_\alpha \tag{4.100}
$$

is the angular momentum transferred across $\Sigma$.

For illustration, let us return to our previous example, and let us choose $\Sigma$ to be spacelike and orthogonal to the vector field $\mechanical{u^\alpha}$. Then $\mathrm d\Sigma_\alpha=-\mechanical{u_\alpha}\sqrt{h}\,\mathrm d^3y$ and we find that $\mechanical{\Delta M}=\int_\Sigma\energy{\tilde E}\energy{\rho}\sqrt{h}\,\mathrm d^3y$ and $\mechanical{\Delta J}=\int_\Sigma\mechanical{\tilde L}\energy{\rho}\sqrt{h}\,\mathrm d^3y$. The first equation states that the transfer of energy across $\Sigma$ is the integral of $\energy{\tilde E}\energy{\rho}$, the energy density. The second equation comes with a very similar interpretation.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\energy{\varepsilon^\alpha}$ | — | Energy flux vector | The test-matter energy flow associated with time translations. |
| $\mechanical{\ell^\alpha}$ | — | Angular-momentum flux vector | The test-matter angular-momentum flow associated with rotations. |
| $\energy{T^{\alpha\beta}},\energy{T^\alpha{}_\beta}$ | — | Test stress-energy | The matter tensor that leaves the background geometry fixed. |
| $\energy{\rho}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{m}^{-2}}$ | Rest-mass density | The density of the pressureless dust. |
| $\mechanical{u^\alpha}$ | — | Four-velocity | The geodesic velocity of the dust. |
| $\mechanical{j^\alpha}$ | — | Rest-mass flux | The conserved mass-current vector. |
| $\energy{\tilde E}$ | $1$ | Specific energy | The conserved energy per unit rest mass. |
| $\mechanical{\tilde L}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{m}}$ | Specific angular momentum | The conserved angular momentum per unit rest mass. |
| $\mechanical{\Delta M}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{m}}$ | Transferred mass | The total mass crossing the selected hypersurface. |
| $\mechanical{\Delta J}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{m}^2}$ | Transferred angular momentum | The total angular momentum crossing the selected hypersurface. |
| $\xi^\alpha_{(t)},\xi^\alpha_{(\phi)}$ | — | Killing vectors | The time-translation and rotation symmetry vectors. |
| $\mathrm d\Sigma_\alpha$ | — | Directed surface element | The element through which the flux is integrated. |
| $y^a$ | — | Coordinates | The spatial integration coordinates. |
