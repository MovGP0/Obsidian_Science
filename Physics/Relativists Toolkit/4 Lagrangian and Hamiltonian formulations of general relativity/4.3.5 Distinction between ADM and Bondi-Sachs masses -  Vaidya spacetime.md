> [!TLDR]
> In outgoing Vaidya spacetime, ADM mass equals the initial mass and stays constant, while Bondi–Sachs mass follows the decreasing retarded-time mass function.

<!-- Source: PDF page 168, printed page 152. -->

For stationary spacetimes, the ADM and Bondi–Sachs masses are identical: there is no distinction. For the dynamical spacetime of an isolated body emitting gravitational (or other types of) radiation, the two notions of mass are distinct. For such a system the Bondi–Sachs mass decreases according to Eq. (4.93), while the ADM mass stays constant.

The metric of a radiating spacetime is difficult to write down; usually it is expressed as a messy expansion in powers of $1/\mechanical{r}$. We shall not attempt to deal with these complications here. For the purpose of illustrating the difference between the ADM and Bondi–Sachs masses, we shall instead adopt a simple spherically-symmetric model. Consider the Schwarzschild metric expressed in terms of the null coordinate $\mechanical{u}=\mechanical{t}-\mechanical{r}-2\mechanical{M}\ln(\mechanical{r}/2\mechanical{M}-1)$, and allow the mass parameter $\mechanical{M}$ to become a function of retarded time: $\mechanical{M}\to\mechanical{m}(\mechanical{u})$. This new metric is given by

$$
\mathrm d\mechanical{s}^2=-f\,\mathrm d\mechanical{u}^2-2\,\mathrm d\mechanical{u}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{r}+\mechanical{r}^2\mathrm d\Omega^2,\qquad
f=1-2\mechanical{m}(\mechanical{u})/\mechanical{r}, \tag{4.94}
$$

and it is a good candidate to represent a radiating spacetime. To see if it makes a sensible solution to the Einstein field equations, let us examine the Einstein tensor, whose only nonvanishing component is $G_{uu}=-(2/\mechanical{r}^2)(\mathrm d\mechanical{m}/\mathrm d\mechanical{u})$. This means that the stress-energy tensor must be of the form

<!-- Source: PDF page 169, printed page 153. -->

$$
\energy{T_{\alpha\beta}}=-\frac{\mathrm d\mechanical{m}/\mathrm d\mechanical{u}}{4\pi\mechanical{r}^2}\mechanical{\ell_\alpha}\mechanical{\ell_\beta}, \tag{4.95}
$$

where $\mechanical{\ell_\alpha}=-\partial_\alpha\mechanical{u}$ is tangent to radial, outgoing null geodesics. This stress-energy tensor describes a pressureless fluid with energy density $\energy{\rho}=(-\mathrm d\mechanical{m}/\mathrm d\mechanical{u})/(4\pi\mechanical{r}^2)$ moving with a four-velocity $\mechanical{\ell^\alpha}$. Such a fluid is usually referred to as *null dust*; it gives a good description of radiation in the high-frequency, geometric-optics approximation. It is easy to check that the form $(\text{function of }\mechanical{u})/\mechanical{r}^2$ for the energy density is dictated by energy-momentum conservation. You may also verify that all the standard energy conditions are satisfied by $\energy{T_{\alpha\beta}}$ if $\mathrm d\mechanical{m}/\mathrm d\mechanical{u}<0$, that is, if $\mechanical{m}$ decreases with increasing retarded time. We conclude that the metric of Eq. (4.94), called the *outgoing Vaidya metric*, makes a physically reasonable solution to the Einstein field equations.

We wish to compute the ADM and Bondi–Sachs masses for the Vaidya spacetime. The first step is to select a spacelike hypersurface $\Sigma$ bounded by a closed two-surface $S$; this hypersurface must asymptotically coincide with a surface $\mechanical{\bar t}=\mathrm{constant}$ of Minkowski spacetime. A suitable choice is to let $\Sigma$ be a surface of constant $\mechanical{t}\equiv\mechanical{u}+\mechanical{r}$, for which the unit normal

$$
n_\alpha=-(2-f)^{-1/2}\partial_\alpha(\mechanical{u}+\mechanical{r})
$$

is everywhere timelike. From Eq. (4.94) we obtain that the induced metric on $\Sigma$ is

$$
h_{ab}\mathrm dy^a\mathrm dy^b=(2-f)\mathrm d\mechanical{r}^2+\mechanical{r}^2\mathrm d\Omega^2.
$$

For $S$ we choose the two-sphere $\mechanical{r}=\mechanical{R}$, where $\mechanical{R}$ is a constant much larger than the maximum value of $2\mechanical{m}(\mechanical{u})$; eventually we will take the limit $\mechanical{R}\to\infty$. Recall that spatial infinity corresponds to keeping $\mechanical{t}$ fixed while taking the limit (which means that $\mechanical{u}\to-\infty$), whereas null infinity corresponds to keeping $\mechanical{u}$ fixed while taking the limit. The unit normal on $S$ is $r_a=(2-f)^{1/2}\partial_a\mechanical{r}$, and the induced metric is $\sigma_{AB}\mathrm d\theta^A\mathrm d\theta^B=\mechanical{R}^2\mathrm d\Omega^2$.

First we calculate

$$
\mechanical{M}(S)\equiv-\frac{1}{8\pi}\oint_S(k-k_0)\sqrt{\sigma}\,\mathrm d^2\theta
$$

for the bounded two-surface $S$; the two different limits to infinity will be taken next. The extrinsic curvature of $S$ embedded in $\Sigma$ is calculated as

$$
k=r^a{}_{|a}=\frac{2}{\mechanical{R}}\left[1+\frac{2\mechanical{m}(\mechanical{u})}{\mechanical{R}}\right]^{-1/2}
=\frac{2}{\mechanical{R}}\left[1-\frac{\mechanical{m}(\mechanical{u})}{\mechanical{R}}+O(\mechanical{R}^{-2})\right],
$$

<!-- Source: PDF page 170, printed page 154. -->

and the extrinsic curvature of $S$ embedded in flat space is $k_0=2/\mechanical{R}$. Subtracting, we have that $k-k_0=-2\mechanical{m}(\mechanical{u})/\mechanical{R}^2+O(\mechanical{R}^{-3})$, and integrating over $S$ yields $\mechanical{M}(S)=\mechanical{m}(\mechanical{u})+O(\mechanical{R}^{-1})$.

We may now take the limit $\mechanical{R}\to\infty$. As was mentioned previously, the ADM mass is obtained by keeping $\mechanical{t}=\mechanical{u}+\mechanical{R}$ fixed while taking the limit. This gives

$$
\mechanical{M_{\mathrm{ADM}}}(\mechanical{t})=\mechanical{m}(-\infty), \tag{4.96}
$$

and we see that the ADM mass is a constant, equal to the initial value of the mass function. We may therefore say that $\mechanical{M_{\mathrm{ADM}}}$ represents *all* the mass initially present in the spacetime. (This interpretation is quite general and not limited to this specific example.) For the Bondi–Sachs mass we must keep $\mechanical{u}$ fixed while taking the limit. This gives

$$
\mechanical{M_{\mathrm{BS}}}(\mechanical{u})=\mechanical{m}(\mechanical{u}), \tag{4.97}
$$

and we see that the Bondi–Sachs mass is identified with the mass function of the Vaidya spacetime. It decreases in response to the outflow of radiation described by the stress-energy tensor of Eq. (4.95). Notice that the field equation

$$
\frac{\mathrm d\mechanical{m}}{\mathrm d\mechanical{u}}
=-4\pi\mechanical{r}^2\energy{T_{uu}}
=-4\pi\mechanical{r}^2(-\energy{T_u{}^r})
\equiv-4\pi\mechanical{r}^2\energy{F}
$$

is compatible with the general mass-loss formula of Eq. (4.93).

It may appear paradoxical that the ADM mass of a dynamical spacetime should be a constant. This, however, is what should be expected of a *radiating* spacetime (Fig. 4.5). The ADM mass represents all the mass present on a spacelike hypersurface of constant $\mechanical{t}$. This hypersurface intersects the central body whose mass does decrease as a consequence of radiation loss. But this does not mean that the ADM mass should decrease, because the hypersurface intersects also the radiation, and the ADM mass accounts for both forms of energy. The net result is a conserved quantity. On the other hand, the Bondi–Sachs mass represents all the mass present on a null hypersurface of constant $\mechanical{u}$. Because this hypersurface fails to intersect any of the radiation that was emitted prior to the retarded time $\mechanical{u}$, the net result is a quantity that decreases with increasing retarded time.
