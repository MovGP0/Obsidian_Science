> [!TLDR]
> Komar surface integrals give mass and angular momentum for stationary axially symmetric spacetimes and can be converted to matter integrals.

<!-- Source: PDF page 165, printed page 149. -->

An appealing feature of the Hamiltonian definitions for mass and angular momentum is that they do not involve a specific choice of coordinates. Alternative definitions that share this property can be produced for stationary and axially symmetric spacetimes. These are known as the *Komar formulae*, and they are

$$
\mechanical{M}=-\frac{1}{8\pi}\lim_{S_t\to\infty}\oint_{S_t}\nabla^\alpha\xi^\beta_{(t)}\,\mathrm dS_{\alpha\beta} \tag{4.86}
$$

and

$$
\mechanical{J}=\frac{1}{16\pi}\lim_{S_t\to\infty}\oint_{S_t}\nabla^\alpha\xi^\beta_{(\phi)}\,\mathrm dS_{\alpha\beta}. \tag{4.87}
$$

Here, $\xi^\alpha_{(t)}$ is the spacetime’s timelike Killing vector and $\xi^\alpha_{(\phi)}$ is the rotational Killing vector; they both satisfy Killing’s equation, $\xi_{\alpha;\beta}+\xi_{\beta;\alpha}=0$. The surface element is given by (Section 3.2.3)

$$
\mathrm dS_{\alpha\beta}=-2n_{[\alpha}r_{\beta]}\sqrt{\sigma}\,\mathrm d^2\theta, \tag{4.88}
$$

where $n_\alpha$ and $r_\alpha$ are the timelike and spacelike normals to $S_t$, respectively.

<!-- Source: PDF page 166, printed page 150. -->

To establish that these formulae do indeed give $\mechanical{M}=\mechanical{m}$ and $\mechanical{J}=\mechanical{j}$, we must prove that for the spacetime of Eq. (4.83), the relations

$$
-2\nabla^\alpha\xi^\beta_{(t)}n_\alpha r_\beta=-2\mechanical{m}/\mechanical{r}^2=k-k_0,\qquad
\nabla^\alpha\xi^\beta_{(\phi)}n_\alpha r_\beta=K_{ab}\phi^ar^b
$$

hold in the limit $\mechanical{r}\to\infty$. We begin with the first relation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
-2\nabla^\alpha\xi^\beta_{(t)}n_\alpha r_\beta
&=2\xi^\alpha_{(t);\beta}n_\alpha r^\beta\\
&=2\Gamma^\alpha_{\beta\gamma}n_\alpha r^\beta\xi^\gamma_{(t)}\\
&=-2(1-2\mechanical{m}/\mechanical{r})\Gamma^t_{rt}\\
&=-2\mechanical{m}/\mechanical{r}^2,
\end{aligned}
$$

as required. We have used Killing’s equation in the first line, and inserted $n_\alpha=-(1-\mechanical{m}/\mechanical{r})\partial_\alpha\mechanical{t}$, $r^\alpha=r^ae^\alpha_a=(1-\mechanical{m}/\mechanical{r})\partial x^\alpha/\partial\mechanical{r}$, and $\Gamma^t_{rt}=\mechanical{m}(1+2\mechanical{m}/\mechanical{r})/\mechanical{r}^2$ in the following steps. To establish the second relation requires less work:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla^\alpha\xi^\beta_{(\phi)}n_\alpha r_\beta
&=-\xi^\alpha_{(\phi);\beta}n_\alpha r^\beta\\
&=\xi^\alpha_{(\phi)}n_{\alpha;\beta}r^\beta\\
&=n_{\alpha;\beta}(\phi^ae^\alpha_a)(r^be^\beta_b)\\
&=(n_{\alpha;\beta}e^\alpha_ae^\beta_b)\phi^ar^b\\
&=K_{ab}\phi^ar^b.
\end{aligned}
$$

Once again, Killing’s equation was used in the first line. The second line follows from the fact that the Killing vector is orthogonal to $n^\alpha$. In the third line the vectors $\xi^\alpha_{(\phi)}$ and $r^\beta$ were decomposed into the basis $e^\alpha_a$. Finally, the last line follows from the definition of the extrinsic curvature. These computations prove that the definitions of Eq. (4.86) and (4.87) do indeed imply $\mechanical{M}=\mechanical{m}$ and $\mechanical{J}=\mechanical{j}$. We see that for stationary and axially symmetric spacetimes, the Komar formulae are equivalent to our Hamiltonian definitions for mass and angular momentum.

The Komar formulae can be turned into hypersurface integrals by invoking Stokes’ theorem (Section 3.3.3),

$$
\oint_S B^{\alpha\beta}\mathrm dS_{\alpha\beta}=2\int_\Sigma B^{\alpha\beta}{}_{;\beta}\,\mathrm d\Sigma_\alpha,
$$

where $B^{\alpha\beta}$ is an antisymmetric tensor field and $S$ is the two-dimensional boundary of the hypersurface $\Sigma$. This is possible because when $\xi^\alpha$ is a Killing vector, the tensor $B^{\alpha\beta}=\nabla^\alpha\xi^\beta$ is necessarily antisymmetric. We have

$$
B^{\alpha\beta}{}_{;\beta}=(\nabla^\alpha\xi^\beta)_{;\beta}=-(\nabla^\beta\xi^\alpha)_{;\beta}=-\Box\xi^\alpha,
$$

<!-- Source: PDF page 167, printed page 151. -->

where $\Box=\nabla^\alpha\nabla_\alpha$. Using the fact that all Killing vectors satisfy $\Box\xi^\alpha=-R^\alpha{}_\beta\xi^\beta$ (Section 1.13, Problem 9), we have established the identity

$$
\oint_S\nabla^\alpha\xi^\beta\,\mathrm dS_{\alpha\beta}=2\int_\Sigma R^\alpha{}_\beta\xi^\beta\,\mathrm d\Sigma_\alpha.
$$

Because the hypersurface $\Sigma$ is spacelike, we have that $\mathrm d\Sigma_\alpha=-n_\alpha\sqrt{h}\,\mathrm d^3y$. Using the Einstein field equations we then obtain

$$
\oint_S\nabla^\alpha\xi^\beta\,\mathrm dS_{\alpha\beta}
=-16\pi\int_\Sigma\left(\energy{T_{\alpha\beta}}-\frac12\energy{T}g_{\alpha\beta}\right)n^\alpha\xi^\beta\sqrt{h}\,\mathrm d^3y.
$$

Finally, combining this with Eqs. (4.86) and (4.87), we arrive at

$$
\mechanical{M}=2\int_\Sigma\left(\energy{T_{\alpha\beta}}-\frac12\energy{T}g_{\alpha\beta}\right)n^\alpha\xi^\beta_{(t)}\sqrt{h}\,\mathrm d^3y \tag{4.89}
$$

and

$$
\mechanical{J}=-\int_\Sigma\left(\energy{T_{\alpha\beta}}-\frac12\energy{T}g_{\alpha\beta}\right)n^\alpha\xi^\beta_{(\phi)}\sqrt{h}\,\mathrm d^3y. \tag{4.90}
$$

In these equations, $\Sigma$ stands for *any* spacelike hypersurface that extends to spatial infinity. If $\Sigma$ had two boundaries instead of just one, then an additional contribution from the inner boundary would appear on the right-hand side of Eqs. (4.89) and (4.90). Such a situation arises when the stationary, axially symmetric spacetime contains a black hole (see Section 5.5.3).

It is a remarkable fact that $\mechanical{M}$ and $\mechanical{J}$ are defined fundamentally in terms of integrals over a closed two-surface at infinity. These quantities should therefore be thought of as properties of the asymptotic structure of spacetime. It is only in the case of stationary, axially symmetric spacetimes that $\mechanical{M}$ and $\mechanical{J}$ can be defined as hypersurface integrals.
