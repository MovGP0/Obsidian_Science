> [!TLDR]
> The directed non-null surface element combines the induced volume with the unit normal and an orientation convention.

<!-- PDF page 80; printed page 64 -->

If $\Sigma$ is not null, then

$$
d\Sigma\equiv|h|^{1/2}\,d^3y,
\tag{3.14}
$$

where $h=\det[h_{ab}]$, is an invariant three-dimensional volume element on the hypersurface. To avoid confusing this with the four-dimensional volume element $\sqrt{-g}\,d^4x$, we shall refer to $d\Sigma$ as a *surface element*. The combination $n_\alpha\,d\Sigma$ is a *directed* surface element that points in the direction of increasing $\Phi$. In the null case these quantities are not defined, because $h=0$ and $n_\alpha$ does not exist.

To see how Eq. (3.14) must be generalized so as to incorporate also the null case, we consider the infinitesimal vector field

$$
d\Sigma_\mu=\epsilon_{\mu\alpha\beta\gamma}e_1^\alpha e_2^\beta e_3^\gamma\,d^3y,
\tag{3.15}
$$

where $\epsilon_{\mu\alpha\beta\gamma}=\sqrt{-g}\,[\mu\ \alpha\ \beta\ \gamma]$ is the Levi-Civita tensor of Section 1.8. We will show below that

$$
d\Sigma_\alpha=\varepsilon n_\alpha\,d\Sigma
\tag{3.16}
$$

when the hypersurface is not null. Thus, apart from a factor $\varepsilon=\pm1$, $d\Sigma_\alpha$ is a directed surface element on $\Sigma$. Notice that when $\Sigma$ is spacelike, the factor $\varepsilon=-1$ makes $d\Sigma_\alpha$ a past-directed vector; this is unfortunately a potential source of confusion. Notice also that Eq. (3.15) remains meaningful even when the hypersurface is null. By continuity, therefore, $d\Sigma_\alpha$ is also a directed surface element on a null hypersurface.

Because $d\Sigma_\alpha$ is proportional to the completely antisymmetric Levi-Civita tensor, its sign depends on the ordering of the coordinates $y^1,y^2$, and $y^3$. But this ordering is a priori arbitrary, and we need a convention to remove the sign ambiguity.

<!-- PDF page 81; printed page 65 -->

We shall choose an ordering that makes the scalar $f\equiv\epsilon_{\mu\alpha\beta\gamma}n^\mu e_1^\alpha e_2^\beta e_3^\gamma$ a positive quantity. Notice that this convention was already in force when we went from Eq. (3.15) to Eq. (3.16): $n^\alpha\,d\Sigma_\alpha=d\Sigma>0$.

As a first example of how this works, consider a hypersurface of constant $\mechanical{t}$ in Minkowski spacetime. If $\Phi=\mechanical{t}$, then $n_\alpha=-\partial_\alpha\mechanical{t}$ is the future-directed normal vector. If we choose the ordering $y^a=(\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z})$ we find that $f=\epsilon_{txyz}=1$ has the correct sign. Equation (3.15) implies $d\Sigma_\mu=\delta_\mu^t\,d\mechanical{x}\,d\mechanical{y}\,d\mechanical{z}=-n_\mu\,d\mechanical{x}\,d\mechanical{y}\,d\mechanical{z}$, which is compatible with Eq. (3.16).

For our second example we choose a surface of constant $\mechanical{x}$ in Minkowski spacetime. We take $\Phi=\mechanical{x}$, and $n_\alpha=\partial_\alpha\mechanical{x}$ points in the direction of increasing $\Phi$. We impose the ordering $y^a=(\mechanical{y},\mechanical{t},\mechanical{z})$ because $f=\epsilon_{xytz}=-\epsilon_{xtyz}=\epsilon_{txyz}=1$ has then the correct sign. [Notice that the more tempting ordering $y^a=(\mechanical{t},\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z})$ would produce the wrong sign.] With this choice, Eq. (3.15) implies $d\Sigma_\mu=\delta_\mu^x\,d\mechanical{t}\,d\mechanical{y}\,d\mechanical{z}=n_\mu\,d\mechanical{t}\,d\mechanical{y}\,d\mechanical{z}$, which is compatible with Eq. (3.16).

We now turn to a derivation of Eq. (3.16). It is clear that $d\Sigma_\mu$ must be proportional to $n_\mu$, because $e_a^\mu\epsilon_{\mu\alpha\beta\gamma}e_1^\alpha e_2^\beta e_3^\gamma=0$ by virtue of the antisymmetric property of the Levi-Civita tensor. So we may write

$$
\epsilon_{\mu\alpha\beta\gamma}e_1^\alpha e_2^\beta e_3^\gamma=\varepsilon f n_\mu,
$$

where $f=\epsilon_{\mu\alpha\beta\gamma}n^\mu e_1^\alpha e_2^\beta e_3^\gamma$. Because $f$ is a scalar, we can evaluate it in any convenient coordinate system $x^\alpha$. We choose our coordinates so that $x^0\equiv\Phi$, and on $\Sigma$ we identify $x^a$ with the intrinsic coordinates $y^a$. Then $f\stackrel{*}{=}\sqrt{-g}\,n^\Phi$. In these coordinates $g^{\Phi\Phi}\stackrel{*}{=}g^{\alpha\beta}\Phi_{,\alpha}\Phi_{,\beta}$, and $n_\Phi\stackrel{*}{=}\varepsilon|g^{\Phi\Phi}|^{-1/2}$ is the only nonvanishing component of the normal. It follows that $n^\Phi\stackrel{*}{=}g^{\Phi\alpha}n_\alpha=g^{\Phi\Phi}n_\Phi\stackrel{*}{=}|g^{\Phi\Phi}|^{1/2}$, and we have that $f\stackrel{*}{=}|g\,g^{\Phi\Phi}|^{1/2}$. We now use the definition of the matrix inverse to write $g^{\Phi\Phi}\stackrel{*}{=}\operatorname{cofactor}(g_{\Phi\Phi})/g$, where the cofactor of a matrix element is the determinant obtained after eliminating the row and column to which the element belongs. This determinant is $h$ and we conclude that

$$
f=|h|^{1/2}.
$$

While this result was obtained in the special coordinates $x^\alpha$, it is valid in all coordinate systems because $h$, like $h_{ab}$, is a scalar with respect to four-dimensional coordinate transformations. This result shows that when $\Sigma$ is not null, Eq. (3.16) is indeed equivalent to Eq. (3.15).
