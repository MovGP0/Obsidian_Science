> [!TLDR]
> Gauss' theorem converts a vector divergence integral over spacetime into a flux integral over its closed boundary.

<!-- PDF page 85; printed page 69 -->

We consider a finite region $\mathcal V$ of the spacetime manifold, bounded by a closed hypersurface $\partial\mathcal V$ (Fig. 3.3). The signature of the hypersurface is not restricted; it may have segments that are timelike, spacelike, or null. We will show that for any vector field $A^\alpha$ defined within $\mathcal V$,

$$
\int_{\mathcal V}A^\alpha{}_{;\alpha}\sqrt{-g}\,d^4x
=\oint_{\partial\mathcal V}A^\alpha\,d\Sigma_\alpha,
\tag{3.22}
$$

where $d\Sigma_\alpha$ is the surface element defined by Eq. (3.15).

To derive this result, known as *Gauss' theorem*, we construct the following coordinate system in $\mathcal V$. We imagine a nest of closed hypersurfaces foliating $\mathcal V$, with the boundary $\partial\mathcal V$ forming the outer layer of the nest. (Picture this as the many layers of an onion.) We let $x^0$ be a constant on each one of these hypersurfaces, with $x^0=1$ designating $\partial\mathcal V$ and $x^0=0$ the zero-volume hypersurface at the ‘centre’ of $\mathcal V$. While $x^0$ grows ‘radially outward’ from this ‘centre’, we take the remaining coordinates $x^a$ to be angular coordinates on the closed hypersurfaces

![[relativists-toolkit-fig-3-3-gauss-stokes.svg|**Fig. 3.3** Proof of the Gauss–Stokes theorem.]]

$x^0=\text{constant}$. The coordinates $y^a$ on $\partial\mathcal V$ are then identified with these angular coordinates.

Using such coordinates, the left-hand side of Eq. (3.22) becomes

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{\mathcal V}A^\alpha{}_{;\alpha}\sqrt{-g}\,d^4x
&=\int_{\mathcal V}(\sqrt{-g}\,A^\alpha)_{,\alpha}\,d^4x\\
&\stackrel{*}{=}\int dx^0\oint(\sqrt{-g}\,A^0)_{,0}\,d^3x
+\int dx^0\oint(\sqrt{-g}\,A^a)_{,a}\,d^3x\\
&\stackrel{*}{=}\int dx^0\,\frac{d}{dx^0}\oint\sqrt{-g}\,A^0\,d^3x\\
&\stackrel{*}{=}\left.\oint\sqrt{-g}\,A^0\,d^3x\right|_{x^0=0}^{x^0=1}\\
&\stackrel{*}{=}\oint_{\partial\mathcal V}\sqrt{-g}\,A^0\,d^3y.
\end{aligned}
$$

In the first line we have used the divergence formula for the vector field $A^\alpha$. The second integral of the second line vanishes because $x^a$ are angular coordinates and the integration is over a closed three-dimensional surface. (Understanding this statement requires some thought. Try working through the three-dimensional version of the proof, using spherical coordinates in flat space.) In the fourth line, the contribution at $x^0=0$ vanishes because the ‘hypersurface’ $x^0=0$ has zero volume.

It is easy to check that $d\Sigma_\alpha\stackrel{*}{=}\delta_\alpha^0\sqrt{-g}\,d^3y$ in the specified coordinates, giving

$$
\oint_{\partial\mathcal V}A^\alpha\,d\Sigma_\alpha
\stackrel{*}{=}\oint_{\partial\mathcal V}A^0\sqrt{-g}\,d^3y
$$

for the right-hand side of Eq. (3.22). The two sides are therefore equal in the specified coordinate system; because Eq. (3.22) is a tensorial equation, this suffices to establish the validity of the theorem.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $d\Sigma_\alpha$ | — | Directed hypersurface element | The oriented boundary element with coordinate-dependent component units. |
| $\sqrt{-g}\,d^4x$ | $\unit{\mathrm{m^4}}$ | Proper four-volume element | The invariant spacetime measure in geometrized units. |
| $x^\alpha,x^0,x^a,y^a$ | — | Coordinates | The arbitrary coordinates adapted to the nested hypersurfaces and their boundary. |
