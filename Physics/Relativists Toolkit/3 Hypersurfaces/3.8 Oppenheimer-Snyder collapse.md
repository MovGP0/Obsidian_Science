> [!TLDR]
> A collapsing homogeneous dust ball joins smoothly to a Schwarzschild exterior when its boundary follows geodesics and the enclosed mass matches the exterior mass.

<!-- Source: PDF page 106, printed page 90. -->

In 1939, J. Robert Oppenheimer and his student Hartland Snyder published the first solution to the Einstein field equations that describes the process of gravitational collapse to a black hole. For simplicity they modelled the collapsing star as a spherical ball of pressureless matter with a uniform density. (A perfect fluid with negligible pressure is usually called *dust*.) The metric inside the dust is a Friedmann–Robertson–Walker (FRW) solution, while the metric outside is the Schwarzschild solution (Fig. 3.6). The question considered here is whether these metrics can be joined smoothly at their common boundary, the surface of the collapsing star.

The metric inside the collapsing dust (which occupies the region $\mathcal V^-$) is given by

$$
\mathrm d\mechanical{s}_-^2=-\mathrm d\mechanical{\tau}^2+\mechanical{a}^2(\mechanical{\tau})(\mathrm d\mechanical{\chi}^2+\sin^2\mechanical{\chi}\,\mathrm d\Omega^2), \tag{3.58}
$$

where $\mechanical{\tau}$ is proper time on comoving world lines (along which $\mechanical{\chi}$, $\mechanical{\theta}$, and $\mechanical{\phi}$ are all constant), and $\mechanical{a}(\mechanical{\tau})$ is the scale factor. By virtue of the Einstein field equations, this satisfies

$$
\mechanical{\dot a}^2+1=\frac{8\pi}{3}\energy{\rho}\mechanical{a}^2, \tag{3.59}
$$

where an overdot denotes differentiation with respect to $\mechanical{\tau}$. By virtue of energy-momentum conservation in the absence of pressure, the dust’s mass density $\energy{\rho}$ satisfies

$$
\energy{\rho}\mechanical{a}^3=\mathrm{constant}\equiv\frac{3}{8\pi}\mechanical{a_{\max}}, \tag{3.60}
$$

where $\mechanical{a_{\max}}$ is the maximum value of the scale factor. The solution to Eqs. (3.59) and (3.60) has the parametric form

$$
\mechanical{a}(\eta)=\tfrac12\mechanical{a_{\max}}(1+\cos\eta),\qquad
\mechanical{\tau}(\eta)=\tfrac12\mechanical{a_{\max}}(\eta+\sin\eta);
$$

the collapse begins at $\eta=0$ when $\mechanical{a}=\mechanical{a_{\max}}$, and it ends at $\eta=\pi$ when $\mechanical{a}=0$. The hypersurface $\Sigma$ coincides with the surface of the collapsing star, which is located at $\mechanical{\chi}=\mechanical{\chi_0}$ in our comoving coordinates.

The metric outside the dust (in the region $\mathcal V^+$) is given by

$$
\mathrm d\mechanical{s}_+^2=-f\,\mathrm d\mechanical{t}^2+f^{-1}\mathrm d\mechanical{r}^2+\mechanical{r}^2\mathrm d\Omega^2,\qquad
f=1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{r}, \tag{3.61}
$$

where $\mechanical{M}$ is the gravitational mass of the collapsing star. As seen from the outside, $\Sigma$ is described by the parametric equations $\mechanical{r}=\mechanical{R}(\mechanical{\tau})$, $\mechanical{t}=\mechanical{T}(\mechanical{\tau})$, where $\mechanical{\tau}$ is proper time for observers comoving with the surface. Clearly, this is the same $\mechanical{\tau}$ that appears in the metric of Eq. (3.58).

It is convenient to choose $y^a=(\mechanical{\tau},\mechanical{\theta},\mechanical{\phi})$ as coordinates on $\Sigma$. It follows that $e^\alpha_\tau=\mechanical{u^\alpha}$, where $\mechanical{u^\alpha}$ is the four-velocity of an observer comoving with the surface of the collapsing star.

We now calculate the induced metric. As seen from $\mathcal V^-$ the metric on $\Sigma$ is

$$
\mathrm d\mechanical{s}_\Sigma^2=-\mathrm d\mechanical{\tau}^2+\mechanical{a}^2(\mechanical{\tau})\sin^2\mechanical{\chi_0}\,\mathrm d\Omega^2.
$$

As seen from $\mathcal V^+$, on the other hand,

$$
\mathrm d\mechanical{s}_\Sigma^2=-\left(F\mechanical{\dot T}^2-F^{-1}\mechanical{\dot R}^2\right)\mathrm d\mechanical{\tau}^2+\mechanical{R}^2(\mechanical{\tau})\mathrm d\Omega^2,
$$

where $F=1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R}$. Because the induced metric must be the same on both sides of the hypersurface, we have

$$
\mechanical{R}(\mechanical{\tau})=\mechanical{a}(\mechanical{\tau})\sin\mechanical{\chi_0},\qquad
F\mechanical{\dot T}^2-F^{-1}\mechanical{\dot R}^2=1. \tag{3.62}
$$

<!-- Source: PDF page 108, printed page 92. -->

The first equation determines $\mechanical{R}(\mechanical{\tau})$ and the second equation can be solved for $\mechanical{\dot T}$:

$$
F\mechanical{\dot T}=\sqrt{\mechanical{\dot R}^2+F}\equiv\beta(\mechanical{R},\mechanical{\dot R}). \tag{3.63}
$$

This equation can be integrated for $\mechanical{T}(\mechanical{\tau})$ and the motion of the boundary in $\mathcal V^+$ is completely determined.

The unit normal to $\Sigma$ can be obtained from the relations $n_\alpha\mechanical{u^\alpha}=0$, $n_\alpha n^\alpha=1$. As seen from $\mathcal V^-$, $\mechanical{u^\alpha_-}\partial_\alpha=\partial_\tau$ and $n^-_\alpha\mathrm dx^\alpha=\mechanical{a}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{\chi}$; we have chosen $n^\chi>0$ so that $n^\alpha$ is directed toward $\mathcal V^+$. As seen from $\mathcal V^+$, $\mechanical{u^\alpha_+}\partial_\alpha=\mechanical{\dot T}\partial_t+\mechanical{\dot R}\partial_r$ and $n^+_\alpha\mathrm dx^\alpha=-\mechanical{\dot R}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{t}+\mechanical{\dot T}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{r}$, with a consistent choice for the sign.

The extrinsic curvature is defined on either side of $\Sigma$ by $K_{ab}=n_{\alpha;\beta}e^\alpha_ae^\beta_b$. The nonvanishing components are $K_{\tau\tau}=n_{\alpha;\beta}\mechanical{u^\alpha}\mechanical{u^\beta}=-n_\alpha\mechanical{u^\alpha}{}_{;\beta}\mechanical{u^\beta}=-\mechanical{a^\alpha}n_\alpha$ (where $\mechanical{a^\alpha}$ is the acceleration of an observer comoving with the surface), $K_{\theta\theta}=n_{\theta;\theta}$, and $K_{\phi\phi}=n_{\phi;\phi}$. A straightforward calculation reveals that as seen from $\mathcal V^-$,

$$
K^\tau_{-\tau}=0,\qquad K^\theta_{-\theta}=K^\phi_{-\phi}=\mechanical{a}^{-1}\cot\mechanical{\chi_0}; \tag{3.64}
$$

the first result follows immediately from the fact that the comoving world lines of a FRW spacetime are geodesics. As seen from $\mathcal V^+$,

$$
K^\tau_{+\tau}=\dot\beta/\mechanical{\dot R},\qquad
K^\theta_{+\theta}=K^\phi_{+\phi}=\beta/\mechanical{R}, \tag{3.65}
$$

where $\beta(\mechanical{R},\mechanical{\dot R})$ is defined by Eq. (3.63).

To have a smooth transition at the surface of the collapsing star, we demand that $K_{ab}$ be the same on both sides of the hypersurface. It is therefore necessary for $\mechanical{u^\alpha_+}$ to satisfy the geodesic equation ($\mechanical{a^\alpha_+}=0$) in $\mathcal V^+$. It is easy to check that the geodesic equation produces $\mechanical{\dot R}^2+F=\energy{\tilde E}^2$, where $\energy{\tilde E}=-\mechanical{u_t}$ is the (conserved) energy parameter of the comoving observer. This relation implies $\beta=\energy{\tilde E}$, and the fact that $\beta$ is a constant enforces $K^\tau_{+\tau}=0$, as required. On the other hand, $[K^\theta{}_\theta]=0$ gives $\cot\mechanical{\chi_0}/\mechanical{a}=\beta/\mechanical{R}=\energy{\tilde E}/(\mechanical{a}\sin\mechanical{\chi_0})$, or

$$
\beta=\energy{\tilde E}=\cos\mechanical{\chi_0}. \tag{3.66}
$$

We have found that the requirement for a smooth transition at $\Sigma$ is that the hypersurface be generated by geodesics of both $\mathcal V^-$ and $\mathcal V^+$, and that the parameters $\energy{\tilde E}$ and $\mechanical{\chi_0}$ be related by Eq. (3.66). With the help of Eqs. (3.59), (3.62), and (3.63) we may turn Eq. (3.66) into

$$
\mechanical{M}=\frac{4\pi}{3}\energy{\rho}\mechanical{R}^3, \tag{3.67}
$$

which equates the gravitational mass of the collapsing star to the product of its density and volume. This relation has an immediate intuitive meaning, and it neatly summarizes the complete solution to the Oppenheimer–Snyder problem.
