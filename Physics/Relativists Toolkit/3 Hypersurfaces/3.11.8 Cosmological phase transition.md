> [!TLDR]
> A null shell with surface tension changes a model universe from expansion in two directions to isotropic expansion and supplies energy to the cosmological fluid.

<!-- Source: PDF page 128, printed page 112. -->

In this third (and final) application of the formalism, we consider an intriguing (but entirely artificial) cosmological scenario according to which the universe was initially expanding in two directions only, but was then made to expand isotropically by a sudden explosive event.

The $\mathcal V^-$ region of spacetime is the one in which the universe is expanding in the $x$ and $y$ directions only. Its metric is

$$
\mathrm d\mechanical{s}_-^2=-\mathrm d\mechanical{t}^2+\mechanical{a}^2(\mechanical{t})(\mathrm dx^2+\mathrm dy^2)+\mathrm d\mechanical{z}^2,
$$

and the scale factor is assumed to be given by $\mechanical{a}(\mechanical{t})\propto\mechanical{t}^{1/2}$. The cosmological fluid moves with a four-velocity $\mechanical{u}^\alpha=\partial x^\alpha/\partial\mechanical{t}$, and it has a density and (isotropic) pressure given by $\energy{\rho_-}=\mechanical{p_-}=1/(32\pi\mechanical{t}^2)$, respectively.

In the $\mathcal V^+$ region of spacetime, the universe expands uniformly in all three directions. Here the metric is

$$
\mathrm d\mechanical{s}_+^2=-\mathrm d\mechanical{t}^2+\mechanical{a}^2(\mechanical{t})(\mathrm dx^2+\mathrm dy^2+\mathrm d\mechanical{z}^2),
$$

with the same scale factor $\mechanical{a}(\mechanical{t})$ as in $\mathcal V^-$, and the cosmological fluid has a density and pressure given by $\energy{\rho_+}=3\mechanical{p_+}=3/(32\pi\mechanical{t}^2)$, respectively; this corresponds to a radiation-dominated universe.

The history of the explosive event that changes the metric from $g^-_{\alpha\beta}$ to $g^+_{\alpha\beta}$ traces a null hypersurface $\Sigma$ in spacetime. This surface moves in the positive $\mechanical{z_\pm}$ direction and as we shall see, it supports a singular stress-energy tensor. The ‘agent’ that alters the course of the universe’s expansion is therefore a null shell.

As seen from $\mathcal V^-$, the hypersurface is described by $\mechanical{t}=\mechanical{z_-}+\mathrm{constant}$, and the vector $\mechanical{k}^\alpha\partial_\alpha=\partial_t+\partial_z$ is tangent to the null generators, which are parameterized by $\mechanical{t}$. In fact, because $\mechanical{k}^\alpha{}_{;\beta}\mechanical{k}^\beta=0$ we have that $\mechanical{t}$ is an affine parameter on this side of the hypersurface. The coordinates $x$ and $y$ are constant on the generators, and we use them, together with $\mechanical{t}$, as intrinsic coordinates on $\Sigma$. We therefore have $y^a=(\mechanical{t},\theta^A)$, $\theta^A=(x,y)$, and the shell’s induced metric is

<!-- Source: PDF page 129, printed page 113. -->

$$
\sigma_{AB}\mathrm d\theta^A\mathrm d\theta^B=\mechanical{a}^2(\mechanical{t})(\mathrm dx^2+\mathrm dy^2).
$$

The remaining basis vectors are $e^\alpha_x\partial_\alpha=\partial_x$, $e^\alpha_y\partial_\alpha=\partial_y$, and $N_\alpha\mathrm dx^\alpha=-\frac12(\mathrm d\mechanical{t}+\mathrm d\mechanical{z_-})$. The nonvanishing components of the transverse curvature are

$$
C^-_{AB}=\frac{1}{4\mechanical{t}}\sigma_{AB}.
$$

We note that on the $\mathcal V^-$ side of $\Sigma$, the null generators have an expansion given by $\mechanical{\theta}=\mechanical{k}^\alpha{}_{;\alpha}=1/\mechanical{t}$, and that $\energy{T_{\alpha\beta}}\mechanical{k}^\alpha \mechanical{k}^\beta=\energy{\rho_-}+\mechanical{p_-}=1/(16\pi\mechanical{t}^2)$, where $\energy{T^{\alpha\beta}}$ is the stress-energy tensor of the cosmological fluid.

As seen from $\mathcal V^+$, the description of the hypersurface is obtained by integrating $\mathrm d\mechanical{t}=\mechanical{a}(\mechanical{t})\,\mathrm d\mechanical{z_+}$, and $\mechanical{k}^\alpha\partial_\alpha=\partial_t+\mechanical{a}^{-1}\partial_z$ is tangent to the null generators. We note that $\mechanical{t}$ is *not* an affine parameter on this side of the hypersurface: we have that $\mechanical{k}^\alpha{}_{;\beta}\mechanical{k}^\beta=(2\mechanical{t})^{-1}\mechanical{k}^\alpha$. The remaining basis vectors are $e^\alpha_x\partial_\alpha=\partial_x$, $e^\alpha_y\partial_\alpha=\partial_y$, $N_\alpha\mathrm dx^\alpha=-\frac12(\mathrm d\mechanical{t}+\mechanical{a}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{z_+})$, and the nonvanishing components of the transverse curvature are now

$$
C^+_{tt}=\frac{1}{2\mechanical{t}},\qquad
C^+_{AB}=\frac{1}{4\mechanical{t}}\sigma_{AB}.
$$

On this side of $\Sigma$, the generators have an expansion also given by $\mechanical{\theta}=1/\mechanical{t}$ (since continuity of $\mechanical{\theta}$ is implied by continuity of the induced metric), and $\energy{T_{\alpha\beta}}\mechanical{k}^\alpha \mechanical{k}^\beta=\energy{\rho_+}+\mechanical{p_+}=1/(8\pi\mechanical{t}^2)$.

The fact that $\mechanical{t}$ is an affine parameter on one side of the hypersurface only tells us that the shell must possess a surface pressure. In fact, continuity of $C_{AB}$ across the shell implies that $\mechanical{p}$ is the *only* nonvanishing surface quantity. It is given by

$$
\mechanical{p}=-\frac{1}{16\pi\mechanical{t}},
$$

the negative sign indicating that this surface quantity would be better described as a *tension*, not a pressure. The shell’s surface stress-energy tensor is $\energy{S^{\alpha\beta}}=\mechanical{p}\sigma^{AB}e^\alpha_Ae^\alpha_B$. If we select observers comoving with the cosmological fluid as our preferred observers to make measurements on the shell, then $-\mechanical{k}_\alpha \mechanical{u}^\alpha=1$ and the full stress-energy tensor of the singular hypersurface is $\energy{T_\Sigma^{\alpha\beta}}=\energy{S^{\alpha\beta}}\delta(\mechanical{t}-\mechanical{t_\Sigma})$, with $\mechanical{t_\Sigma}$ denoting the time at which a given observer crosses the shell. We see that for these observers, $-\mechanical{p}$ is the physically-measured surface tension.

Finally, we note that the expressions $-\mechanical{p}=1/(16\pi\mechanical{t})$, $\mechanical{\theta}=1/\mechanical{t}$, and $[\energy{T_{\alpha\beta}}\mechanical{k}^\alpha \mechanical{k}^\beta]=1/(16\pi\mechanical{t}^2)$ are compatible with the general relation $-\mechanical{p}\mechanical{\theta}=[\energy{T_{\alpha\beta}}\mechanical{k}^\alpha \mechanical{k}^\beta]$ derived in Section 3.11.5. This shows that the energy released by the shell as it expands is absorbed by the cosmological fluid, whose density increases by a factor of $\energy{\rho_+}/\energy{\rho_-}=3$; this energy is provided by the shell’s surface tension.
