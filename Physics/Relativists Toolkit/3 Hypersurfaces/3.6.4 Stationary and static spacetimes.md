> [!TLDR]
> A static spacetime has a hypersurface-orthogonal timelike Killing vector, and its orthogonal hypersurfaces have zero extrinsic curvature.

<!-- Source: PDF page 98, printed page 82. -->

A spacetime is said to be *stationary* if it admits a timelike Killing vector $t^\alpha$. This means that in a coordinate system $(\mechanical{t},x^a)$ in which $t^\alpha\stackrel{*}{=}\delta^\alpha_t$, the metric does not depend on the coordinate $\mechanical{t}$: $g_{\alpha\beta,t}\stackrel{*}{=}0$ (see Section 1.5). For example, a rotating star gives rise to a stationary spacetime if its mass and angular velocity do not change with time.

A stationary spacetime is also *static* if the metric does not change under a time reversal, $\mechanical{t}\to-\mechanical{t}$. For example, the spacetime of a rotating star is not static because a time reversal changes the direction of rotation. In the specified coordinate system, invariance of the metric under a time reversal implies $g_{ta}\stackrel{*}{=}0$. This, in turn, implies that the Killing vector is proportional to a gradient: $t_\alpha\stackrel{*}{=}g_{tt}\partial_\alpha\mechanical{t}$. Thus, *a spacetime is static if the timelike Killing vector field is hypersurface orthogonal*.

We may show that if a spacetime is static, then $K_{ab}=0$ on those hypersurfaces $\Sigma$ that are orthogonal to the Killing vector; these hypersurfaces therefore represent moments of time symmetry. If $\Sigma$ is orthogonal to $t^\alpha$, then its unit normal must be given by $n_\alpha=\mu t_\alpha$, where $1/\mu^2=-t^\alpha t_\alpha$. This implies that $n_{\alpha;\beta}=\mu t_{\alpha;\beta}+t_\alpha\mu_{,\beta}$, and $n_{(\alpha;\beta)}=t_{(\alpha}\mu_{,\beta)}$ because $t_\alpha$ is a Killing vector. That $K_{ab}=0$ follows immediately from Eq. (3.35) and the fact that $t_\alpha$ is orthogonal to $e^\alpha_a$.
