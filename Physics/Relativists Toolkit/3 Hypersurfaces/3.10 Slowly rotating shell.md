> [!TLDR]
> A slowly rotating spherical shell has a perfect-fluid surface stress-energy tensor and drags inertial frames inside it.

<!-- Source: PDF page 110, printed page 94. -->

Our next application of the thin-shell formalism is concerned with the spacetime of a slowly rotating, spherical shell. We take the exterior metric to be the slow-rotation limit of the Kerr solution,

$$
\mathrm d\mechanical{s}_+^2=-f\,\mathrm d\mechanical{t}^2+f^{-1}\mathrm d\mechanical{r}^2+\mechanical{r}^2\mathrm d\Omega^2-\frac{4\mechanical{M}\mechanical{a}}{\mechanical{r}}\sin^2\mechanical{\theta}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{t}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{\phi}. \tag{3.71}
$$

Here, $f=1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{r}$ with $\mechanical{M}$ denoting the shell’s gravitational mass, and $\mechanical{a}=\mechanical{J}/\mechanical{M}\ll\mechanical{M}$, where $\mechanical{J}$ is the shell’s angular momentum. Throughout this section we will work consistently to first order in $\mechanical{a}$.

The metric of Eq. (3.71) is cut off at $\mechanical{r}=\mechanical{R}$, which is where the shell is located. As viewed from the exterior, the shell’s induced metric is

$$
\mathrm d\mechanical{s}_\Sigma^2=-(1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R})\mathrm d\mechanical{t}^2+\mechanical{R}^2\mathrm d\Omega^2-\frac{4\mechanical{M}\mechanical{a}}{\mechanical{R}}\sin^2\mechanical{\theta}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{t}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{\phi}.
$$

It is possible to remove the off-diagonal term by going to a rotating frame of reference. We therefore introduce a new angular coordinate $\mechanical{\psi}$ related to $\mechanical{\phi}$ by

$$
\mechanical{\psi}=\mechanical{\phi}-\mechanical{\Omega}\mechanical{t}, \tag{3.72}
$$

where $\mechanical{\Omega}$ is the angular velocity of the new frame with respect to the inertial frame of Eq. (3.71). We anticipate that $\mechanical{\Omega}$ will be proportional to $\mechanical{a}$, and this allows us to approximate $\mathrm d\mechanical{\phi}^2$ by $\mathrm d\mechanical{\psi}^2+2\mechanical{\Omega}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{t}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{\psi}$. Substituting this into $\mathrm d\mechanical{s}_\Sigma^2$ returns a diagonal metric if $\mechanical{\Omega}$ is chosen to be

$$
\mechanical{\Omega}=\frac{2\mechanical{M}\mechanical{a}}{\mechanical{R}^3}. \tag{3.73}
$$

The induced metric then becomes

$$
h_{ab}\mathrm dy^a\mathrm dy^b=-(1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R})\mathrm d\mechanical{t}^2+\mechanical{R}^2(\mathrm d\mechanical{\theta}^2+\sin^2\mechanical{\theta}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{\psi}^2). \tag{3.74}
$$

It is now clear that the shell has a spherical geometry. As Eq. (3.74) indicates, we will use the coordinates $y^a=(\mechanical{t},\mechanical{\theta},\mechanical{\psi})$ on the shell.

We take spacetime to be flat inside the shell, and we write the Minkowski metric in the form

$$
\mathrm d\mechanical{s}_-^2=-(1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R})\mathrm d\mechanical{t}^2+\mathrm d\mechanical{\rho}^2+\mechanical{\rho}^2(\mathrm d\mechanical{\theta}^2+\sin^2\mechanical{\theta}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{\psi}^2), \tag{3.75}
$$

where $\mechanical{\rho}$ is a radial coordinate. This metric must be cut off at $\mechanical{\rho}=\mechanical{R}$ and joined to the exterior metric of Eq. (3.71). The shell’s intrinsic metric, as computed from the interior, agrees with Eq. (3.74). Continuity of the induced metric is therefore established, and we must now turn to the extrinsic curvature.

We first compute the extrinsic curvature as seen from the shell’s exterior. In the metric of Eq. (3.71), the shell’s unit normal is $n_\alpha=f^{-1/2}\partial_\alpha\mechanical{r}$. The parametric equations of the hypersurface are $\mechanical{t}=t$, $\mechanical{\theta}=\theta$, and $\mechanical{\phi}=\mechanical{\psi}+\mechanical{\Omega}\mechanical{t}$, and they have the generic form $x^\alpha=x^\alpha(y^a)$. These allow us to compute the tangent vectors $e^\alpha_a=\partial x^\alpha/\partial y^a$ and we obtain $e^\alpha_t\partial_\alpha=\partial_t+\mechanical{\Omega}\partial_\phi$, $e^\alpha_\theta\partial_\alpha=\partial_\theta$, and $e^\alpha_\psi\partial_\alpha=\partial_\phi$. From all this we find that the nonvanishing components of the extrinsic curvature are

$$
\begin{aligned}
K^t{}_t&=\frac{\mechanical{M}}{\mechanical{R}^2\sqrt{1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R}}},\\
K^t{}_\psi&=-\frac{3\mechanical{M}\mechanical{a}\sin^2\mechanical{\theta}}{\mechanical{R}^2\sqrt{1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R}}},\\
K^\psi{}_t&=\frac{3\mechanical{M}\mechanical{a}}{\mechanical{R}^4}\sqrt{1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R}},\\
K^\theta{}_\theta&=\frac{1}{\mechanical{R}}\sqrt{1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R}}=K^\psi{}_\psi.
\end{aligned}
$$

As now seen from the shell’s interior, the unit normal is $n_\alpha=\partial_\alpha\mechanical{\rho}$ and the tangent vectors are $e^\alpha_t\partial_\alpha=\partial_t$, $e^\alpha_\theta\partial_\alpha=\partial_\theta$, and $e^\alpha_\psi\partial_\alpha=\partial_\psi$. From this we find that $K^\theta{}_\theta=1/\mechanical{R}=K^\psi{}_\psi$ are the only two nonvanishing components of the extrinsic curvature. This could have been obtained directly by setting $\mechanical{M}=0$ in our previous results.

We have a discontinuity in the extrinsic curvature, and Eq. (3.56) allows us to calculate $\energy{S^{ab}}$, the shell’s surface stress-energy tensor. After a few lines of algebra we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
\energy{S^t{}_t}&=-\frac{1}{4\pi\mechanical{R}}\left(1-\sqrt{1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R}}\right),\\
\energy{S^t{}_\psi}&=\frac{3\mechanical{M}\mechanical{a}\sin^2\mechanical{\theta}}{8\pi\mechanical{R}^2\sqrt{1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R}}},\\
\energy{S^\psi{}_t}&=-\frac{3\mechanical{M}\mechanical{a}}{8\pi\mechanical{R}^4}\sqrt{1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R}},\\
\energy{S^\theta{}_\theta}&=\frac{1-\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R}-\sqrt{1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R}}}{8\pi\mechanical{R}\sqrt{1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R}}}=\energy{S^\psi{}_\psi}.
\end{aligned}
$$

These results give us a complete description of the surface stress-energy tensor, but they are not terribly illuminating. Can we make sense of this mess?

We will attempt to cast $\energy{S^{ab}}$ in a perfect-fluid form,

$$
\energy{S^{ab}}=\energy{\sigma}\mechanical{u^a}\mechanical{u^b}+\mechanical{p}\left(h^{ab}+\mechanical{u^a}\mechanical{u^b}\right), \tag{3.76}
$$

in terms of a velocity field $\mechanical{u^a}$, a surface density $\energy{\sigma}$, and a surface pressure $\mechanical{p}$. How do we find these quantities? First we notice that Eq. (3.76) implies $\energy{S^a{}_b}\mechanical{u^b}=-\energy{\sigma}\mechanical{u^a}$, which shows that $\mechanical{u^a}$ is a normalized eigenvector of the surface stress-energy tensor, with eigenvalue $-\energy{\sigma}$. This gives us three equations for three unknowns, the density and the two independent components of the velocity field. Once those have been obtained, the pressure is found by projecting $\energy{S^{ab}}$ in the directions orthogonal to $\mechanical{u^a}$. The rest is just a matter of algebra.

We can save ourselves some work if we recognize that the shell must move rigidly in the $\mechanical{\psi}$ direction, with a uniform angular velocity $\mechanical{\omega}$. Its velocity vector can then be expressed as

$$
\mechanical{u^a}=\mechanical{\gamma}\left(t^a+\mechanical{\omega}\psi^a\right), \tag{3.77}
$$

where $t^a=\partial y^a/\partial\mechanical{t}$ and $\psi^a=\partial y^a/\partial\mechanical{\psi}$ are Killing vectors of the induced metric $h_{ab}$. In Eq. (3.77), $\mechanical{\omega}=\mathrm d\mechanical{\psi}/\mathrm d\mechanical{t}$ is the shell’s angular velocity in the rotating frame of Eq. (3.72), and $\mechanical{\gamma}$ is determined by the normalization condition $h_{ab}\mechanical{u^a}\mechanical{u^b}=-1$. We can simplify things further if we anticipate that $\mechanical{\omega}$ will be proportional to $\mechanical{a}$. For example, neglecting $O(\mechanical{\omega}^2)$ terms when normalizing $\mechanical{u^a}$ gives

$$
\mechanical{\gamma}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R}}}. \tag{3.78}
$$

With these assumptions, we find that the eigenvalue equation produces $\mechanical{\omega}=-\energy{S^\psi{}_t}/(-\energy{S^t{}_t}+\energy{S^\psi{}_\psi})$ and $\energy{\sigma}=-\energy{S^t{}_t}$. After simplification the first equation becomes

$$
\mechanical{\omega}=\frac{6\mechanical{M}\mechanical{a}}{\mechanical{R}^3}\frac{1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R}}{\left(1-\sqrt{1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R}}\right)\left(1+3\sqrt{1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R}}\right)}, \tag{3.79}
$$

and the second is

$$
\energy{\sigma}=\frac{1}{4\pi\mechanical{R}}\left(1-\sqrt{1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R}}\right). \tag{3.80}
$$

We now have the surface density and the velocity field. The surface pressure can easily be obtained by projecting $\energy{S^{ab}}$ in the directions orthogonal to $\mechanical{u^a}$: $\mechanical{p}=\frac12(h_{ab}+\mechanical{u_a}\mechanical{u_b})\energy{S^{ab}}=\frac12(\energy{S}+\energy{\sigma})$, where $\energy{S}=h_{ab}\energy{S^{ab}}$. This gives $\mechanical{p}=\energy{S^\theta{}_\theta}$, and

$$
\mechanical{p}=\frac{1-\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R}-\sqrt{1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R}}}{8\pi\mechanical{R}\sqrt{1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R}}}. \tag{3.81}
$$

The shell’s material is therefore a perfect fluid of density $\energy{\sigma}$, pressure $\mechanical{p}$, and angular velocity $\mechanical{\omega}$. When $\mechanical{R}$ is much larger than $2\mechanical{M}$, Eqs. (3.79)–(3.81) reduce to $\mechanical{\omega}\simeq3\mechanical{a}/(2\mechanical{R}^2)$, $\energy{\sigma}\simeq\mechanical{M}/(4\pi\mechanical{R}^2)$, and $\mechanical{p}\simeq\mechanical{M}^2/(16\pi\mechanical{R}^3)$, respectively.

The spacetime of a slowly rotating shell offers us a unique opportunity to explore the rather strange relativistic effects associated with rotation. We conclude this section with a brief description of these effects.

The metric of Eq. (3.71) is the metric outside the shell, and it is expressed in a coordinate system that goes easily into a Cartesian frame at infinity. This is the frame of the ‘fixed stars,’ and it is this frame which sets the standard of no rotation. The metric of Eq. (3.75), on the other hand, is the metric inside the shell, and it is expressed in a coordinate system that is rotating with respect to the frame of the fixed stars. The transformation is given by Eq. (3.72), and it shows that an observer at constant $\mechanical{\psi}$ moves with an angular velocity $\mathrm d\mechanical{\phi}/\mathrm d\mechanical{t}=\mechanical{\Omega}$. Inertial observers inside the shell are therefore rotating with respect to the fixed stars, with an angular velocity $\mechanical{\Omega_{\mathrm{in}}}\equiv\mechanical{\Omega}$. According to Eq. (3.73), this is

$$
\mechanical{\Omega_{\mathrm{in}}}=\frac{2\mechanical{M}\mechanical{a}}{\mechanical{R}^3}. \tag{3.82}
$$

This angular motion is induced by the rotation of the shell, and the effect is known as the *dragging of inertial frames*. It was first discovered in 1918 by Thirring and Lense.

The shell’s angular velocity $\mechanical{\omega}$, as computed in Eq. (3.79), is measured in the rotating frame. As measured in the nonrotating frame, the shell’s angular velocity is $\mechanical{\Omega_{\mathrm{shell}}}=\mathrm d\mechanical{\phi}/\mathrm d\mechanical{t}=\mathrm d\mechanical{\psi}/\mathrm d\mechanical{t}+\mechanical{\Omega}=\mechanical{\omega}+\mechanical{\Omega_{\mathrm{in}}}$. According to Eqs. (3.79) and (3.82), this is

$$
\mechanical{\Omega_{\mathrm{shell}}}=\frac{2\mechanical{M}\mechanical{a}}{\mechanical{R}^3}
\frac{1+2\sqrt{1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R}}}{\left(1-\sqrt{1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R}}\right)\left(1+3\sqrt{1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{R}}\right)}. \tag{3.83}
$$

When $\mechanical{R}$ is much larger than $2\mechanical{M}$, $\mechanical{\Omega_{\mathrm{in}}}/\mechanical{\Omega_{\mathrm{shell}}}\simeq4\mechanical{M}/(3\mechanical{R})$, and the internal observers rotate at a small fraction of the shell’s angular velocity. As $\mechanical{R}$ approaches $2\mechanical{M}$, however, the ratio approaches unity, and the internal observers find themselves corotating with the shell. This is a rather striking manifestation of frame dragging. (The phrase ‘Mach’s principle’ is often attached to this phenomenon.) This spacetime, admittedly, is highly idealized, and you may wonder whether corotation could ever occur in a realistic situation. You will see in Chapter 5 that the answer is yes: A very similar phenomenon occurs in the vicinity of a rotating black hole.
