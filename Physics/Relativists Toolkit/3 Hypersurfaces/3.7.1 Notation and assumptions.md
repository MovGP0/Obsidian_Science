> [!TLDR]
> A continuous coordinate system and signed normal distance define jumps and distributional derivatives across the hypersurface.

<!-- Source: PDF page 101, printed page 85. -->

We assume that the same coordinates $y^a$ can be installed on both sides of the hypersurface, and we choose $n^\alpha$, the unit normal to $\Sigma$, to point from $\mathcal V^-$ to $\mathcal V^+$. We suppose that a continuous coordinate system $x^\alpha$, distinct from $x^\alpha_\pm$, can be introduced on both sides of the hypersurface. These coordinates overlap with $x^\alpha_+$ in an open region of $\mathcal V^+$ that contains $\Sigma$, and they also overlap with $x^\alpha_-$ in an open region of $\mathcal V^-$ that contains $\Sigma$. (We introduce these coordinates for our short-term convenience only; the final formulation of the junction conditions will not involve them.)

We imagine $\Sigma$ to be pierced by a congruence of geodesics that intersect it orthogonally. We take $\mechanical{\ell}$ to denote proper distance (or proper time) along the geodesics, and we adjust the parameterization so that $\mechanical{\ell}=0$ when the geodesics cross the hypersurface; our convention is that $\mechanical{\ell}$ is negative in $\mathcal V^-$ and positive in $\mathcal V^+$. We can think of $\mechanical{\ell}$ as a scalar field: The point $P$ identified by the coordinates $x^\alpha$ is linked to $\Sigma$ by a member of the congruence, and $\mechanical{\ell}(x^\alpha)$ is the proper distance (or proper time) from $\Sigma$ to $P$ along this geodesic. Our construction implies that a displacement away from the hypersurface along one of the geodesics is described by $\mathrm dx^\alpha=n^\alpha\mathrm d\mechanical{\ell}$, and that

$$
n_\alpha=\varepsilon\partial_\alpha\mechanical{\ell}; \tag{3.46}
$$

we also have $n^\alpha n_\alpha=\varepsilon$.

We will use the language of *distributions*. We introduce the Heaviside distribution $\Theta(\mechanical{\ell})$, equal to $+1$ if $\mechanical{\ell}>0$, $0$ if $\mechanical{\ell}<0$, and indeterminate if $\mechanical{\ell}=0$. We note the following properties:

$$
\Theta^2(\mechanical{\ell})=\Theta(\mechanical{\ell}),\qquad
\Theta(\mechanical{\ell})\Theta(-\mechanical{\ell})=0,\qquad
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\mechanical{\ell}}\Theta(\mechanical{\ell})=\delta(\mechanical{\ell}),
$$

where $\delta(\mechanical{\ell})$ is the Dirac distribution. We also note that the product $\Theta(\mechanical{\ell})\delta(\mechanical{\ell})$ is *not defined* as a distribution.

The following notation will be useful:

$$
[A]\equiv A(\mathcal V^+)\big|_\Sigma-A(\mathcal V^-)\big|_\Sigma,
$$

where $A$ is any tensorial quantity defined on both sides of the hypersurface; $[A]$ is therefore the *jump* of $A$ across $\Sigma$. We note the relations

$$
[n^\alpha]=[e^\alpha_a]=0, \tag{3.47}
$$

where $e^\alpha_a=\partial x^\alpha/\partial y^a$. The first follows from the relation $\mathrm dx^\alpha=n^\alpha\mathrm d\mechanical{\ell}$ and the continuity of both $\mechanical{\ell}$ and $x^\alpha$ across $\Sigma$; the second follows from the fact that the coordinates $y^a$ are the same on both sides of the hypersurface.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\mechanical{\ell}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{m}}$ | Proper distance or time | The signed distance or time along an orthogonal geodesic. |
| $n^\alpha$ | — | Unit normal | The normal directed from the minus region to the plus region. |
