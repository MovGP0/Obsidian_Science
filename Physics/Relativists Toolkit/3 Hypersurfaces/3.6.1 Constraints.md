> [!TLDR]
> The normal projections of Einstein's equations constrain the induced metric and extrinsic curvature on an initial spacelike hypersurface.

<!-- Source: PDF page 95, printed page 79. -->

In Newtonian mechanics, a complete solution to the equations of motion requires the specification of initial values for the position and velocity of each moving body. In field theories, a complete solution to the field equations requires the specification of the field and its time derivative at one instant of time.

<!-- Source: PDF page 96, printed page 80. -->

A similar statement can be made for general relativity. Because the Einstein field equations are second-order partial differential equations, we would expect that a complete solution requires the specification of $g_{\alpha\beta}$ and $g_{\alpha\beta,t}$ at one instant of time. While this is essentially correct, it is desirable to convert this decidedly noncovariant statement into something more geometrical.

The *initial-value problem* of general relativity starts with the selection of a spacelike hypersurface $\Sigma$ which represents an ‘instant of time.’ This hypersurface can be chosen freely. On this hypersurface we place arbitrary coordinates $y^a$.

The spacetime metric $g_{\alpha\beta}$, when evaluated on $\Sigma$, has components that characterize displacements *away from* the hypersurface. (For example, $g_{tt}$ is such a component if $\Sigma$ is a surface of constant $\mechanical{t}$.) These components cannot be given meaning in terms of the geometric properties of $\Sigma$ alone. To provide meaningful initial values for the spacetime metric, we must consider displacements *within* the hypersurface only. In other words, the initial values for $g_{\alpha\beta}$ can only be the six components of the *induced metric* $h_{ab}=g_{\alpha\beta}e^\alpha_ae^\beta_b$; the remaining four components are arbitrary, and this reflects the complete freedom in choosing the spacetime coordinates $x^\alpha$.

Similarly, the initial values for the ‘time derivative’ of the metric must be described by a three-tensor that carries information about the derivative of the metric in the direction normal to the hypersurface. Because $K_{ab}=\frac12(\mathcal L_n g_{\alpha\beta})e^\alpha_ae^\beta_b$, the extrinsic curvature is clearly an appropriate choice.

The *initial-value problem* of general relativity therefore consists in specifying two symmetric tensor fields, $h_{ab}$ and $K_{ab}$, on a spacelike hypersurface $\Sigma$. In the complete spacetime, $h_{ab}$ is recognized as the induced metric on the hypersurface, while $K_{ab}$ is the extrinsic curvature. These tensors cannot be chosen freely: They must satisfy the *constraint equations* of general relativity. These are given by Eqs. (3.41) and (3.42), together with the Einstein field equations $G_{\alpha\beta}=8\pi\energy{T_{\alpha\beta}}$:

$$
{}^3R+K^2-K^{ab}K_{ab}=16\pi\energy{T_{\alpha\beta}}n^\alpha n^\beta\equiv16\pi\energy{\rho} \tag{3.44}
$$

and

$$
K^b{}_{a|b}-K_{,a}=8\pi\energy{T_{\alpha\beta}}e^\alpha_an^\beta\equiv8\pi\mechanical{j_a}. \tag{3.45}
$$

The remaining components of the Einstein field equations provide *evolution equations* for $h_{ab}$ and $K_{ab}$; these will be considered in Chapter 4.

| $K$ | $\unit{\mathrm{m}^{-1}}$ | Extrinsic-curvature trace | The trace of the extrinsic curvature. |

| $\energy{\rho}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{m}^{-2}}$ | Energy density | The normal projection of the stress-energy tensor. |
