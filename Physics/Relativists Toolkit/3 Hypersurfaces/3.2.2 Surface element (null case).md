> [!TLDR]
> A null directed surface element combines generator distance, transverse area, and the null normal.

<!-- PDF page 81; printed page 65 -->

As we have seen in Section 3.1.2, when $\Sigma$ is null we identify $y^1$ with $\mechanical{\lambda}$, the parameter on the hypersurface's null generators, and the remaining coordinates, denoted

<!-- PDF page 82; printed page 66 -->

$\theta^A$, are constant on the generators. Then $e_1^\alpha=\mechanical{k}^\alpha$, $d^3y=d\mechanical{\lambda}\,d^2\theta$, and we may write the directed surface element as

$$
d\Sigma_\mu=\mechanical{k}^\nu\,dS_{\mu\nu}\,d\mechanical{\lambda},
\tag{3.17}
$$

where

$$
dS_{\mu\nu}=\epsilon_{\mu\nu\beta\gamma}e_2^\beta e_3^\gamma\,d^2\theta
\tag{3.18}
$$

is interpreted as an element of two-dimensional surface area. We will show below that this can also be expressed as

$$
dS_{\alpha\beta}=2\mechanical{k}_{[\alpha}N_{\beta]}\sqrt\sigma\,d^2\theta,
\tag{3.19}
$$

where $N_\alpha$ is the auxiliary null vector field introduced in Eq. (3.13), and $\sigma=\det[\sigma_{AB}]$, with $\sigma_{AB}$ the two-metric defined by Eq. (3.11). Combining Eq. (3.19) with Eq. (3.17) yields

$$
d\Sigma_\alpha=-\mechanical{k}_\alpha\sqrt\sigma\,d^2\theta\,d\mechanical{\lambda}.
\tag{3.20}
$$

The interpretation of this result is clear: Apart from a minus sign, the surface element is directed along $\mechanical{k}_\alpha$, the normal to the null hypersurface; the factor $d\mechanical{\lambda}$ represents an element of parameter-distance along the null generators, and $\sqrt\sigma\,d^2\theta$ is an element of *cross-sectional area* – an element of two-dimensional surface area in the directions transverse to the generators.

There is also an ordering issue with the coordinates $\theta^A$, and our convention shall be that the scalar $f\equiv\epsilon_{\mu\nu\beta\gamma}N^\mu\mechanical{k}^\nu e_2^\beta e_3^\gamma$ must be a positive quantity. Notice that this convention was already in force when we went from Eq. (3.18) to Eq. (3.19): $N^\alpha\mechanical{k}^\beta\,dS_{\alpha\beta}=\sqrt\sigma\,d^2\theta>0$.

As an example, consider a surface $\mechanical{u}=\text{constant}$ in Minkowski space, where $\mechanical{u}=\mechanical{t}-\mechanical{x}$. The normal vector is $\mechanical{k}_\alpha=-\partial_\alpha(\mechanical{t}-\mechanical{x})$, and we may choose the ordering $\theta^A=(\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z})$. Then $N_\alpha=-\tfrac12\partial_\alpha(\mechanical{t}+\mechanical{x})$ satisfies all the requirements for an auxiliary vector field. It is easy to check that with these choices, $f=1$ (which is properly positive). We obtain $dS_{tx}=d\mechanical{y}\,d\mechanical{z}=-dS_{xt}$, and since $\mechanical{t}$ can be identified with the affine parameter $\mechanical{\lambda}$, Eq. (3.17) implies $d\Sigma_t=d\mechanical{t}\,d\mechanical{y}\,d\mechanical{z}=-d\Sigma_x$. These results are compatible with Eq. (3.20).

Let us consider a more complicated example: the light cone of Section 3.1.4. The vectors $\mechanical{k}^\alpha$, $N^\alpha$, and $e_a^\alpha$ are displayed in that section, and the cone's intrinsic coordinates are $y^a=(\mechanical{\lambda},\mechanical{\theta},\mechanical{\phi})$. We want to compute $d\Sigma_\mu$ for this hypersurface, starting with the definition of Eq. (3.15). We know that $d\Sigma_\mu$ must point in the direction of the normal, so that $d\Sigma_\mu=-f\mechanical{k}_\mu\,d\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\phi}\,d\mechanical{\lambda}$, where $f=\epsilon_{\mu\nu\beta\gamma}N^\mu\mechanical{k}^\nu e_2^\beta e_3^\gamma$. If we let $N^\mu\equiv e_0^\mu$ and $\mechanical{k}^\nu\equiv e_1^\nu$ we can write this as $f=[\mu\ \nu\ \beta\ \gamma]e_0^\mu e_1^\nu e_2^\beta e_3^\gamma\equiv\det\mathbf E$, where $\mathbf E$ is the matrix constructed by lining up the four basis vectors. Its

<!-- PDF page 83; printed page 67 -->

determinant is easy to compute, and we obtain $f=\mechanical{\lambda}^2\sin\mechanical{\theta}=\sqrt\sigma$. We therefore have $d\Sigma_\mu=-\mechanical{k}_\mu\sqrt\sigma\,d^2\theta\,d\mechanical{\lambda}$, which is just the same statement as in Eq. (3.20).

We must now give a proper derivation of Eq. (3.19). The steps are somewhat similar to those leading to Eq. (3.16). We begin by noting that the tensor $\epsilon_{\mu\nu\beta\gamma}e_2^\beta e_3^\gamma$ is orthogonal to $e_A^\alpha$ and antisymmetric in the indices $\mu$ and $\nu$. It may be expressed as

$$
\epsilon_{\mu\nu\beta\gamma}e_2^\beta e_3^\gamma
=2f\mechanical{k}_{[\mu}N_{\nu]}
=f(\mechanical{k}_\mu N_\nu-N_\mu\mechanical{k}_\nu),
$$

where $f=\epsilon_{\mu\nu\beta\gamma}N^\mu\mechanical{k}^\nu e_2^\beta e_3^\gamma>0$. To evaluate $f$ we choose our coordinates such that $x^0\equiv\Phi$ and $x^a\equiv y^a=(\mechanical{\lambda},\theta^A)$ on $\Sigma$. In these coordinates, $\mechanical{k}_\Phi\stackrel{*}{=}-1$ and $\mechanical{k}^\lambda\stackrel{*}{=}1$ are the only nonvanishing components of the normal vector, $N^\Phi\stackrel{*}{=}1$ comes as a consequence of the normalization condition $N^\alpha\mechanical{k}_\alpha=-1$, and $g^{\Phi\Phi}\stackrel{*}{=}0$ follows from the fact that $\mechanical{k}_\alpha$ is null. Using this information we deduce that $f\stackrel{*}{=}\sqrt{-g}$, and we must now compute the metric determinant in the specified coordinates. For this purpose we note that the completeness relations of Eq. (3.13) imply the following structure for the inverse metric:

$$
\mathbf g^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}
0&1&0\\
1&-2N^\lambda&-N^A\\
0&-N^A&\sigma^{AB}
\end{pmatrix};
$$

this immediately implies $\det\mathbf g^{-1}=-\det[\sigma^{AB}]$, or $\sqrt{-g}\stackrel{*}{=}\sqrt\sigma$. We therefore have

$$
f=\sqrt\sigma,
$$

which holds in *any* coordinate system $x^\alpha$. This establishes that Eq. (3.19) is indeed equivalent to Eq. (3.18). This, in turn, implies that Eq. (3.20) is equivalent to Eq. (3.15) when $\Sigma$ is null and coordinates $y^a=(\mechanical{\lambda},\theta^A)$ are placed on the hypersurface.
