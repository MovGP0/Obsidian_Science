> [!TLDR]
> The Kerr curvature singularity is a ring; the interior of its coordinate disk is regular away from the equatorial plane.

<!-- Source: PDF page 211, printed page 195. -->

We have seen that the Kerr spacetime possesses a curvature singularity at

$$
\rho^2\equiv\mechanical{r}^2+\mechanical{a}^2\cos^2\mechanical{\theta}=0.
$$

<!-- Source: PDF page 212, printed page 196. -->

According to this equation, the singularity occurs only in the equatorial plane $(\mechanical{\theta}=\pi/2)$, at $\mechanical{r}=0$. The Kerr–Schild coordinates can help us make sense of this statement. The relations $\mechanical{x}^2+\mechanical{y}^2=(\mechanical{r}^2+\mechanical{a}^2)\sin^2\mechanical{\theta}$ and $\mechanical{z}=\mechanical{r}\cos\mechanical{\theta}$ indicate that the ‘point’ $\mechanical{r}=0$ corresponds in fact to the entire disk $\mechanical{x}^2+\mechanical{y}^2\le\mechanical{a}^2$ in the plane $\mechanical{z}=0$. The points interior to the disk correspond to angles such that $\sin^2\mechanical{\theta}<1$. The boundary,

$$
\mechanical{x}^2+\mechanical{y}^2=\mechanical{a}^2,
$$

corresponds to the equatorial plane, and this is where the Kerr metric is singular. The curvature singularity of the Kerr spacetime is therefore located on a ring of (coordinate) radius $\mechanical{a}$ in the $\mechanical{x}$-$\mechanical{y}$ plane. This singularity can be avoided: Observers at $\mechanical{r}=0$ can stay away from the equatorial plane, and they never have to encounter the singularity; such observers end up going *through* the ring.

## Physical quantities

| Symbol | Unit | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\mechanical{a}$ | $\unit{\mathrm m}$ | Specific angular momentum | The Kerr spin parameter and coordinate radius of the singular ring. |
| $\mechanical{r},\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z}$ | $\unit{\mathrm m}$ | Spatial coordinates | The radial and Kerr–Schild Cartesian coordinates. |
| $\mechanical{\theta}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{rad}}$ | Polar angle | The equatorial plane has polar angle π/2. |
