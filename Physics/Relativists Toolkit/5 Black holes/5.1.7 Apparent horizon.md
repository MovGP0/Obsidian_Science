> [!TLDR]
> An apparent horizon bounds the trapped region of a spatial slice, where future-directed ingoing and outgoing null congruences both contract.

<!-- Source: PDF page 187, printed page 171. -->

Another important property of the surface $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{M}$ has to do with the behaviour of outgoing light rays in a neighbourhood of this surface. Here, the term *outgoing* will refer specifically to those rays which move on curves $\mechanical{U}=\mathrm{constant}$. This is potentially confusing because the radial coordinate $\mechanical{r}$ does not necessarily increase along those rays; in fact, $\mechanical{r}$ increases only if $\mechanical{U}<0$ (outside the black hole) and it decreases if $\mechanical{U}>0$ (inside the black hole). While the term ‘outgoing’ should perhaps be reserved to designate rays along which $\mechanical{r}$ always increases, this terminology is nevertheless widely used. Similarly, we will use the term *ingoing* to designate light rays which move on curves $\mechanical{V}=\mathrm{constant}$. If $\mechanical{V}>0$, then $\mechanical{r}$ decreases along the ingoing rays; if $\mechanical{V}<0$, $\mechanical{r}$ increases.

We will show that the expansion of a congruence of outgoing light rays (as defined above) changes sign at $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{M}$. (This should be obvious just from the fact that $\mechanical{r}$ increases along the geodesics that are outside $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{M}$, but decreases along the geodesics that are inside.) Outgoing light rays have

$$
\mechanical{k_\alpha}=-\partial_\alpha\mechanical{U} \tag{5.14}
$$

as their (affinely parameterized) tangent vector, and their expansion is calculated as $\mechanical{\theta}=\mechanical{k^\alpha}{}_{;\alpha}=|g|^{-1/2}(|g|^{1/2}\mechanical{k^\alpha})_{,\alpha}$. In Kruskal coordinates $\mechanical{k^V}=|g_{UV}|^{-1}$ is the only nonvanishing component of $\mechanical{k^\alpha}$, and $|g|^{1/2}=|g_{UV}|\mechanical{r}^2\sin\mechanical{\theta}$. This gives $\mechanical{\theta}=2\mechanical{r}_{,V}/|\mechanical{r}g_{UV}|$, and using Eq. (5.8) and (5.9) we obtain

$$
\mechanical{\theta}=\mechanical{k^\alpha}{}_{;\alpha}=-\frac{\mechanical{U}}{2\mechanical{M}\mechanical{r}}. \tag{5.15}
$$

As was previously claimed, the expansion is positive for $\mechanical{U}<0$ (in the past of $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{M}$) and negative for $\mechanical{U}>0$ (in the future of $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{M}$). The expansion therefore changes sign at $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{M}$, and in this context, this surface is called an *apparent horizon*. (A similar calculation would reveal that for ingoing light rays, the expansion is negative everywhere in regions **I** and **II**.)

![[relativists-toolkit-fig-5-6-trapped-surface.svg|**Fig. 5.6** Trapped surfaces and apparent horizon of a spacelike hypersurface.]]

To give a proper definition to the term ‘apparent horizon’, we must first introduce the notion of a *trapped surface* (Fig. 5.6). Let $\Sigma$ be a spacelike hypersurface. A trapped surface on $\Sigma$ is a closed, two-dimensional surface $S$ such that for both congruences (ingoing and outgoing) of future-directed null geodesics orthogonal to $S$, the expansion $\mechanical{\theta}$ is negative everywhere on $S$. (It should be clear that each two-sphere $\mechanical{U},\mechanical{V}=\mathrm{constant}$ in region **II** of the Kruskal diagram is a trapped surface.) Let $\mathcal T$ be the portion of $\Sigma$ that contains trapped surfaces; this is known as the *trapped region* of $\Sigma$. The boundary of the trapped region, $\partial\mathcal T$, is what is defined to be the *apparent horizon* of the spacelike hypersurface $\Sigma$. (In Schwarzschild spacetime this would be any two-sphere at $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{M}$.) Notice that the apparent horizon is a marginally trapped surface: For one congruence of null geodesics orthogonal to $\partial\mathcal T$, $\mechanical{\theta}=0$. Notice also that the apparent horizon designates a specific two-surface $S$ on a given hypersurface $\Sigma$. The apparent horizon can generally be extended toward the future (and past) of $\Sigma$, because hypersurfaces to the future (and past) of $\Sigma$ also contain apparent horizons. The union of all these apparent horizons forms a three-dimensional surface $\mathcal A$ called the *trapping horizon* of the spacetime. (In Schwarzschild spacetime this would be the entire hypersurface $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{M}$.) In the following we will not distinguish between the two-dimensional apparent horizon and the three-dimensional trapping horizon; we will refer to both as the apparent horizon. (This sloppiness of language is not uncommon.)
