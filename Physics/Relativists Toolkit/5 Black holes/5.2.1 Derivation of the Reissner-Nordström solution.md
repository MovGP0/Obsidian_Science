> [!TLDR]
> The Einstein–Maxwell equations give the charged black-hole metric and identify its charge through Gauss’ law.

<!-- Source: PDF page 192, printed page 176. -->

The Reissner–Nordström (RN) metric describes a static, spherically symmetric black hole of mass $\mechanical{M}$ possessing an electric charge $\electric{Q}$. We begin our discussion with a derivation of this solution to the Einstein–Maxwell equations.

We assume that the electromagnetic-field tensor $\electric{F^{\alpha\beta}}$ has no components along the $\mechanical{\theta}$ and $\mechanical{\phi}$ directions; this ensures that the field is purely electric when measured by stationary observers. Under this assumption the only nonvanishing component is $\electric{F^{tr}}$. Maxwell’s equations in vacuum are $0=\electric{F^{\alpha\beta}}{}_{;\beta}=\lvert g\rvert^{-1/2}(\lvert g\rvert^{1/2}\electric{F^{\alpha\beta}})_{,\beta}$.

<!-- Source: PDF page 193, printed page 177. -->

Using the metric of Eq. (5.2), this implies $(e^\psi\mechanical{r}^2\electric{F^{tr}})'=0$, or

$$
\electric{F^{tr}}=e^{-\psi}\frac{\electric{Q}}{\mechanical{r}^2},
$$

where $\electric{Q}$ is a constant of integration, to be interpreted as the black-hole charge. The stress-energy tensor for the electromagnetic field is

$$
\energy{T^\alpha_\beta}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\left(\electric{F^{\alpha\mu}}\electric{F_{\beta\mu}}-\frac14\delta^\alpha_\beta\electric{F^{\mu\nu}}\electric{F_{\mu\nu}}\right),
$$

and a few steps of algebra yield

$$
\energy{T^\alpha_\beta}=\frac{\electric{Q}^2}{8\pi\mechanical{r}^4}\operatorname{diag}(-1,-1,1,1). \tag{5.20}
$$

The Einstein field equations (5.4) imply $\mechanical{m}'=\electric{Q}^2/2\mechanical{r}^2$, or $\mechanical{m}(\mechanical{r})=\mechanical{M}-\electric{Q}^2/2\mechanical{r}$. The fact that $\energy{T^t_t}=\energy{T^r_r}$ implies $\psi'=0$, so that $\psi$ can be set to zero without loss of generality. The RN solution is therefore

$$
\mathrm d\mechanical{s}^2=-\left(1-\frac{2\mechanical{M}}{\mechanical{r}}+\frac{\electric{Q}^2}{\mechanical{r}^2}\right)\mathrm d\mechanical{t}^2+\left(1-\frac{2\mechanical{M}}{\mechanical{r}}+\frac{\electric{Q}^2}{\mechanical{r}^2}\right)^{-1}\mathrm d\mechanical{r}^2+\mechanical{r}^2\mathrm d\Omega^2, \tag{5.21}
$$

with an electromagnetic-field tensor whose only nonvanishing component is

$$
\electric{F^{tr}}=\frac{\electric{Q}}{\mechanical{r}^2}. \tag{5.22}
$$

Here, $\mechanical{M}$ is total (ADM) mass of the spacetime and $\electric{Q}$ is the black hole’s electric charge.

To see that $\electric{Q}$ is indeed the charge, consider a nonsingular charge distribution on a spacelike hypersurface $\Sigma$, described by a current density $\electric{j^\alpha}$. An appropriate definition for total charge is $\electric{Q}=\int_\Sigma\electric{j^\alpha}\,\mathrm d\Sigma_\alpha$, or $\electric{Q}=(4\pi)^{-1}\int_\Sigma\electric{F^{\alpha\beta}}{}_{;\beta}\,\mathrm d\Sigma_\alpha$ after using Maxwell’s equations. Using Stokes’ theorem (Section 3.5.3), we rewrite this as an integral over a closed two-surface $S$ bounding the charge distribution. This yields Gauss’ law,

$$
\electric{Q}=\frac{1}{8\pi}\oint_S\electric{F^{\alpha\beta}}\,\mathrm dS_{\alpha\beta}. \tag{5.23}
$$

The advantage of this expression for the total charge is that it is applicable even when the charge distribution is singular, which is the case in the present application. Also, this definition of total charge is in the same spirit as the previously encountered definitions for total mass and angular momentum (Section 4.3). Substituting Eq. (5.22) and evaluating for a two-sphere of constant $\mechanical{t}$ and $\mechanical{r}$ confirms that the $\electric{Q}$ appearing in Eq. (5.22) is indeed the black hole’s electric charge.
