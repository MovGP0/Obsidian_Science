> [!TLDR]
> A radial geodesic turns inside the RN inner horizon; two null-coordinate patches follow its inward and outward motion.

<!-- Source: PDF page 197, printed page 181. -->

The discovery of black-hole tunnels is so bizarre that it should be backed up by a solid calculation. Here we consider the geodesic motion of a free-falling observer in the RN spacetime. It is assumed that the motion proceeds entirely in the radial direction and that initially, it is directed inward.

We will first work with the $(\mechanical{v},\mechanical{r})$ coordinates, in which the metric takes the form

$$
\mathrm d\mechanical{s}^2=-f\,\mathrm d\mechanical{v}^2+2\,\mathrm d\mechanical{v}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{r}+\mechanical{r}^2\mathrm d\Omega^2, \tag{5.27}
$$

where $f=1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{r}+\electric{Q}^2/\mechanical{r}^2$. The observer’s four-velocity is $\mechanical{u^\alpha}\partial_\alpha=\mechanical{\dot v}\partial_v+\mechanical{\dot r}\partial_r$, where an overdot denotes differentiation with respect to proper time $\mechanical{\tau}$. The quantity $\energy{\tilde E}=-\mechanical{u_\alpha}t^\alpha=-\mechanical{u_v}$, the observer’s energy per unit mass, is a constant of the motion. In terms of $\mechanical{\dot v}$ and $\mechanical{\dot r}$ this is given by $\energy{\tilde E}=f\mechanical{\dot v}-\mechanical{\dot r}$. On the other hand, the normalization condition $\mechanical{u^\alpha}\mechanical{u_\alpha}=-1$ gives $f\mechanical{\dot v}^2-2\mechanical{\dot v}\mechanical{\dot r}=1$, and these equations imply

$$
\mechanical{\dot r_{\mathrm{in}}}=-(\energy{\tilde E}^2-f)^{1/2},\qquad
\mechanical{\dot v_{\mathrm{in}}}=\frac{\energy{\tilde E}-(\energy{\tilde E}^2-f)^{1/2}}{f}, \tag{5.28}
$$

where the sign in front of the square root was chosen appropriately for an ingoing observer.

<!-- Source: PDF page 198, printed page 182. -->

The equation for $\mechanical{\dot r}$ can also be written in the form

$$
\mechanical{\dot r}^2+f=\energy{\tilde E}^2, \tag{5.29}
$$

which comes with a nice interpretation as an energy equation (Fig. 5.10). Its message is clear: After crossing the outer and inner horizons the observer reaches a turning point $(\mechanical{\dot r}=0)$ at a radius $\mechanical{r_{\min}}<\mechanical{r_-}$ such that $f(\mechanical{r_{\min}})=\energy{\tilde E}^2$. The motion, which initially was inward, turns outward and the observer eventually emerges out of the black hole, into a new external universe. During the outward portion of the motion, the observer’s four-velocity is given by

$$
\mechanical{\dot r_{\mathrm{out}}}=+(\energy{\tilde E}^2-f)^{1/2},\qquad
\mechanical{\dot v_{\mathrm{out}}}=\frac{\energy{\tilde E}+(\energy{\tilde E}^2-f)^{1/2}}{f}, \tag{5.30}
$$

with the opposite sign in front of the square root.

Let us examine the behaviour of $\mechanical{\dot v}$ as the observer traverses a horizon. When the motion is inward we have that $\mechanical{\dot v}\simeq(2\energy{\tilde E})^{-1}$ in the limit $f\to0$. This means that $\mechanical{v}$ stays finite during the first crossings of the outer and inner horizons. When the motion is outward, $\mechanical{\dot v}\simeq2\energy{\tilde E}/f$ in the limit $f\to0$, and this means that the coordinates $(\mechanical{v},\mechanical{r})$ become singular during the second crossing of the inner horizon. The observer’s motion cannot be followed beyond this point, unless new coordinates are introduced.

Let us therefore switch to the coordinates $(\mechanical{u},\mechanical{r})$, in which the RN metric takes the form

$$
\mathrm d\mechanical{s}^2=-f\,\mathrm d\mechanical{u}^2-2\,\mathrm d\mechanical{u}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{r}+\mechanical{r}^2\mathrm d\Omega^2. \tag{5.31}
$$

**Fig. 5.11** Eddington–Finkelstein patches for the Reissner–Nordström spacetime.

In these coordinates, and during the outward portion of the motion (after the bounce at $\mechanical{r}=\mechanical{r_{\min}}$), the four-velocity is given by

$$
\mechanical{\dot r_{\mathrm{out}}}=+(\energy{\tilde E}^2-f)^{1/2},\qquad
\mechanical{\dot u_{\mathrm{out}}}=\frac{\energy{\tilde E}-(\energy{\tilde E}^2-f)^{1/2}}{f}. \tag{5.32}
$$

We have that $\mechanical{\dot u}\simeq(2\energy{\tilde E})^{-1}$ when $f\to0$, which shows that $\mechanical{u}$ stays finite during the second crossings of the inner and outer horizons.

We see that a large portion of the RN spacetime is covered by the two coordinate patches employed here (Fig. 5.11). This includes two asymptotically-flat regions connected by a black-hole tunnel that contains two copies of the outer horizon, and two copies of the inner horizon. The complete spacetime is obtained by tessellation, using the patches $(\mechanical{v},\mechanical{r})$ and $(\mechanical{u},\mechanical{r})$ as tiles; this gives rise to the diagram of Fig. 5.9. Because the complete spacetime contains an infinite number of black-hole tunnels, an infinite number of coordinate patches is required for its description.

The presence of black-hole tunnels in the RN spacetime is now well established. These tunnels, of course, have a lot to do with the occurrence of a turning point in the motion of our free-falling observer. This is a rather striking feature of the RN spacetime. While turning points are a familiar feature of Newtonian mechanics, in this context they are always associated with the presence of an angular-momentum term in the effective potential: The centrifugal force is repulsive and it prevents an observer from reaching the centre at $\mechanical{r}=0$. This, however, cannot explain what is happening here, because the motion was restricted from the start to be radial – there is no angular momentum present to produce a repulsive force. The gravitational field alone must be responsible for the repulsion, and we are forced to conclude

<!-- Source: PDF page 200, printed page 184. -->

that *inside the inner horizon, the gravitational force becomes repulsive!* It is this repulsive gravity that ultimately is responsible for the black-hole tunnels.

Such a surprising conclusion can perhaps be understood better if we recall our previous expression for the mass function:

$$
\mechanical{m}(\mechanical{r})=\mechanical{M}-\frac{\electric{Q}^2}{2\mechanical{r}}. \tag{5.33}
$$

This relation shows that $\mechanical{m}(\mechanical{r})$ becomes *negative* if $\mechanical{r}$ is sufficiently small, and clearly this negative mass will produce a repulsive gravitational force. How can we explain this behaviour for the mass function? We recall that $\mechanical{m}(\mechanical{r})$ measures the mass inside a sphere of radius $\mechanical{r}$. In general this will be smaller than the total mass $\mechanical{M}\equiv\mechanical{m}(\infty)$, because a sphere of finite radius $\mechanical{r}$ excludes a certain amount – equal to $\electric{Q}^2/(2\mechanical{r})$ – of electrostatic energy. If the radius is sufficiently small, then $\electric{Q}^2/(2\mechanical{r})>\mechanical{M}$ and $\mechanical{m}(\mechanical{r})<0$. You may check that this always occurs within the inner horizon.

The conclusion that the RN spacetime contains black-hole tunnels is firm. Should we then feel confident that a trip inside a charged black hole will lead us to a new universe? This answer is no. The reason is that the existence of such tunnels depends very sensitively on the assumed symmetries of the RN spacetime, namely, staticity and spherical symmetry. These symmetries would not be exact in a realistic black hole, and slight perturbations have a dramatic effect on the hole’s internal structure. The tunnels are unstable, and they do not appear in realistic situations. (More will be said on this in Section 5.7, Problem 3.)
