> [!TLDR]
> Surface gravity is the redshifted holding force per unit mass at the horizon and also fixes its Killing-generator normalization.

<!-- Source: PDF page 200, printed page 184. -->

In Section 5.2.2, a quantity $\mechanical{\kappa_+}\equiv\frac12 f'(\mechanical{r_+})$ was introduced during the construction of Kruskal coordinates adapted to the outer horizon. We shall name this quantity the *surface gravity* of the black hole, and henceforth denote it simply by $\mechanical{\kappa}$. As we shall see in Section 5.5, the surface gravity provides an important characterization of black holes, and it plays a key role in the laws of black-hole mechanics. For the RN black hole the surface gravity is given explicitly by

$$
\mechanical{\kappa}=\frac{\mechanical{r_+}-\mechanical{r_-}}{2\mechanical{r_+}^2}
=\frac{\sqrt{\mechanical{M}^2-\electric{Q}^2}}{\mechanical{r_+}^2}, \tag{5.34}
$$

where we have used Eq. (5.24). Notice that $\mechanical{\kappa}=0$ for an extreme RN black hole and that

$$
\mechanical{\kappa}=\frac{1}{4\mechanical{M}} \tag{5.35}
$$

for a Schwarzschild black hole.

<!-- Source: PDF page 201, printed page 185. -->

The name ‘surface gravity’ deserves a justification. Consider, in a static and spherically symmetric spacetime with metric

$$
\mathrm d\mechanical{s}^2=-f\,\mathrm d\mechanical{t}^2+f^{-1}\mathrm d\mechanical{r}^2+\mechanical{r}^2\mathrm d\Omega^2, \tag{5.36}
$$

a particle of unit mass held stationary at a radius $\mechanical{r}$. (Here, $f$ is not necessarily required to have the RN form, but this will be the case of interest.) The four-velocity of the stationary particle is $\mechanical{u^\alpha}=f^{-1/2}t^\alpha$ and its acceleration is $\mechanical{a^\alpha}=\mechanical{u^\alpha}{}_{;\beta}\mechanical{u^\beta}$. The only nonvanishing component is $\mechanical{a^r}=\frac12 f'$ and the magnitude of the acceleration vector is

$$
\mechanical{a}(\mechanical{r})=(g_{\alpha\beta}\mechanical{a^\alpha}\mechanical{a^\beta})^{1/2}=\frac12f^{-1/2}f'(\mechanical{r}). \tag{5.37}
$$

This is the force required to hold the particle at $\mechanical{r}$ if the force is applied locally, at the particle’s position. This, not surprisingly, diverges in the limit $\mechanical{r}\to\mechanical{r_+}$. But suppose instead that the particle is held in place by an observer at infinity, by means of an infinitely long, massless string. What is $\mechanical{a_\infty}(\mechanical{r})$, the force applied by this observer?

To answer this we consider the following thought experiment. Let the observer at infinity raise the string by a small proper distance $\delta\mechanical{s}$, thereby doing an amount $\delta\energy{W_\infty}=\mechanical{a_\infty}\delta\mechanical{s}$ of work. At the particle’s position the displacement is also $\delta\mechanical{s}$, but the work done is $\delta\energy{W}=\mechanical{a}\delta\mechanical{s}$. (You may justify this statement by working in a local Lorentz frame at $\mechanical{r}$.) Suppose now that the work $\delta\energy{W}$ is converted into radiation that is then collected at infinity. The received energy is redshifted by a factor $f^{1/2}$, so that $\delta\energy{E_\infty}=f^{1/2}\mechanical{a}\delta\mechanical{s}$. But energy conservation demands that the energy extracted be equal to the energy put in, so that $\delta\energy{E_\infty}=\delta\energy{W_\infty}$. This implies

$$
\mechanical{a_\infty}(\mechanical{r})=f^{1/2}\mechanical{a}(\mechanical{r})=\frac12f'(\mechanical{r}). \tag{5.38}
$$

This is the force applied by the observer at infinity. This quantity is well behaved in the limit $\mechanical{r}\to\mechanical{r_+}$, and it is appropriate to call $\mechanical{a_\infty}(\mechanical{r_+})$ the surface gravity of the black hole. Thus,

$$
\mechanical{\kappa}\equiv\mechanical{a_\infty}(\mechanical{r_+})=\frac12f'(\mechanical{r_+}). \tag{5.39}
$$

The surface gravity is therefore the force required of an observer at infinity to hold a particle (of unit mass) stationary at the event horizon.

The surface gravity can also be defined in terms of the Killing vector $t^\alpha$. We have seen in Section 5.1.9 that the event horizon of a static spacetime is also a Killing horizon, so that $t^\alpha$ is tangent to the horizon’s null generators. Because $t^\alpha$ is orthogonal to itself on the horizon, it is also *normal* to the horizon. But $\Phi\equiv-t^\alpha t_\alpha=0$ on the horizon, and since the normal vector is proportional to

<!-- Source: PDF page 202, printed page 186. -->

$\Phi_{,\alpha}$, there must exist a scalar $\mechanical{\kappa}$ such that

$$
(-t^\mu t_\mu)_{;\alpha}=2\mechanical{\kappa}t_\alpha \tag{5.40}
$$

on the horizon. A brief calculation confirms that this $\mechanical{\kappa}$ is the surface gravity: Using the coordinates $(\mechanical{v},\mechanical{r})$ we have that $t^\alpha\partial_\alpha=\partial_v$ and $t_\alpha\mathrm dx^\alpha=\mathrm d\mechanical{r}$ on the horizon; with $\Phi=-g_{vv}=f$ we obtain $\Phi_{,\alpha}=f'\partial_\alpha\mechanical{r}$, which is just Eq. (5.40) with $\mechanical{\kappa}=\frac12f'(\mechanical{r_+})$. This calculation reveals also that the horizon’s null generators are parameterized by $\mechanical{v}$.

Because $t^\alpha$ is tangent to the horizon’s null generators, it must satisfy the geodesic equation at $\mechanical{r}=\mechanical{r_+}$. This comes as an immediate consequence of Eq. (5.40) and Killing’s equation: On the horizon,

$$
t^\alpha{}_{;\beta}t^\beta=\mechanical{\kappa}t^\alpha, \tag{5.41}
$$

and we see that $\mechanical{v}$ is not an affine parameter on the generators. An affine parameter $\mechanical{\lambda}$ can be obtained by integrating the equation $\mathrm d\mechanical{\lambda}/\mathrm d\mechanical{v}=e^{\mechanical{\kappa}\mechanical{v}}$ (Section 1.3). This gives $\mechanical{\lambda}=V/\mechanical{\kappa}$, where $V=e^{\mechanical{\kappa}\mechanical{v}}$ is one of the Kruskal coordinates adapted to the event horizon – it was denoted $V_+$ in Section 5.2.2. It follows that on the horizon, the null vector

$$
\mechanical{k^\alpha}=V^{-1}t^\alpha \tag{5.42}
$$

satisfies the geodesic equation in affine-parameter form.

It is possible to derive an explicit formula for $\mechanical{\kappa}$. Because the congruence of null generators is necessarily hypersurface orthogonal, Frobenius’ theorem (Section 2.4.3) guarantees that the relation

$$
t_{[\alpha;\beta}t_{\gamma]}=0
$$

is satisfied on the event horizon. Using Killing’s equation, this implies

$$
t_{\alpha;\beta}t_\gamma+t_{\gamma;\alpha}t_\beta+t_{\beta;\gamma}t_\alpha=0,
$$

and contracting with $t^{\alpha;\beta}$ yields

$$
\begin{aligned}
t^{\alpha;\beta}t_{\alpha;\beta}t_\gamma
&=-t_{\gamma;\alpha}t^\alpha{}_{;\beta}t^\beta+t_{\beta;\gamma}t^\beta{}_{;\alpha}t^\alpha\\
&=-\mechanical{\kappa}t_{\gamma;\alpha}t^\alpha+\mechanical{\kappa}t_{\beta;\gamma}t^\beta\\
&=-2\mechanical{\kappa}^2t_\gamma.
\end{aligned}
$$

We have obtained

$$
\mechanical{\kappa}^2=-\frac12t^{\alpha;\beta}t_{\alpha;\beta}, \tag{5.43}
$$

<!-- Source: PDF page 203, printed page 187. -->

in which it is understood that the right-hand side is evaluated at $\mechanical{r}=\mechanical{r_+}$. Equations (5.40), (5.41), and (5.43) can all be regarded as fundamental definitions of the surface gravity; they are of course all equivalent.
