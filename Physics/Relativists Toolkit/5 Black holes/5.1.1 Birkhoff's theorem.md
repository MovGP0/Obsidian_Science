> [!TLDR]
> Birkhoff's theorem states that every spherical vacuum exterior is the static Schwarzschild spacetime, even when the spherical source changes with time.

<!-- Source: PDF page 179, printed page 163. -->

The Schwarzschild metric,

$$
\mathrm d\mechanical{s}^2=-\left(1-\frac{2\mechanical{M}}{\mechanical{r}}\right)\mathrm d\mechanical{t}^2
+\left(1-\frac{2\mechanical{M}}{\mechanical{r}}\right)^{-1}\mathrm d\mechanical{r}^2+\mechanical{r}^2\mathrm d\Omega^2, \tag{5.1}
$$

<!-- Source: PDF page 180, printed page 164. -->

is the unique solution to the Einstein field equations that describes the vacuum spacetime outside a spherically symmetric body of mass $\mechanical{M}$. While this object could have a time-dependent mass distribution, the external spacetime is necessarily static and its metric is given by Eq. (5.1). This statement, known as *Birkhoff’s theorem*, implies that a spherical mass distribution cannot emit gravitational waves.

The proof of the theorem goes as follows. The metric of a spherically symmetric spacetime can always be cast in the form

$$
\mathrm d\mechanical{s}^2=-e^{2\psi}f\,\mathrm d\mechanical{t}^2+f^{-1}\mathrm d\mechanical{r}^2+\mechanical{r}^2\mathrm d\Omega^2, \tag{5.2}
$$

involving the two arbitrary functions $\psi(\mechanical{t},\mechanical{r})$ and $f(\mechanical{t},\mechanical{r})$. (This statement is not quite true: it could happen that $\mechanical{t}$ and $\mechanical{r}$ fail to be good coordinates in some region of the spacetime, and this would invalidate this form of the metric in that region. We will encounter such cases shortly, but they can be ignored for the time being.) It is convenient to also introduce a mass function $\mechanical{m}(\mechanical{t},\mechanical{r})$ defined by

$$
f=1-\frac{2\mechanical{m}}{\mechanical{r}}. \tag{5.3}
$$

For the metric of Eq. (5.2), the Einstein field equations are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial\mechanical{m}}{\partial\mechanical{r}}&=4\pi\mechanical{r}^2(-\energy{T_t{}^t}),\qquad
\frac{\partial\mechanical{m}}{\partial\mechanical{t}}=-4\pi\mechanical{r}^2(-\energy{T_t{}^r}),\\
\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\mechanical{r}}&=4\pi\mechanical{r}f^{-1}(-\energy{T_t{}^t}+\energy{T_r{}^r}).
\end{aligned} \tag{5.4}
$$

The first two equations motivate the name ‘mass’ for the function $\mechanical{m}(\mechanical{t},\mechanical{r})$, as $-\energy{T_t{}^t}$ represents the density of mass-energy and $-\energy{T_t{}^r}$ its outward flux; they imply that in vacuum, $\mechanical{m}(\mechanical{t},\mechanical{r})=\mechanical{M}$, a constant. The third gives $\psi'=0$, and $\psi(\mechanical{t},\mechanical{r})$ can be set equal to zero without loss of generality. The Schwarzschild solution is thereby recovered.
