> [!TLDR]
> Kruskal null coordinates remove the Schwarzschild horizon's coordinate singularity and reveal the maximally extended spacetime.

<!-- Source: PDF page 180, printed page 164. -->

The difficulties of the Schwarzschild metric at $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{M}$ are well known. While the spacetime is perfectly well behaved there, the coordinates $(\mechanical{t},\mechanical{r})$ become singular at $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{M}$ — they are no longer in a one-to-one correspondence with spacetime events. This problem can be circumvented by introducing another coordinate system. The following construction originates from the independent work of Kruskal (1960) and Szekeres (1960).

Consider a swarm of massless particles moving radially in the Schwarzschild spacetime — $\mechanical{t}$ and $\mechanical{r}$ vary, but not $\mechanical{\theta}$ and $\mechanical{\phi}$. It is easy to check that ingoing particles move along curves $\mechanical{v}=\mathrm{constant}$, while outgoing particles move along curves $\mechanical{u}=\mathrm{constant}$, where

**Fig. 5.1** Spacetime diagram based on the (u, v) coordinates.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{u}&=\mechanical{t}-\mechanical{r^*},\qquad
\mechanical{v}=\mechanical{t}+\mechanical{r^*},\\
\mechanical{r^*}&=\int\frac{\mathrm d\mechanical{r}}{1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{r}}
=\mechanical{r}+2\mechanical{M}\ln\left|\frac{\mechanical{r}}{2\mechanical{M}}-1\right|.
\end{aligned} \tag{5.5}
$$

In a spacetime diagram using $\mechanical{v}$ (advanced time) and $\mechanical{u}$ (retarded time) as oblique coordinates (both oriented at 45 degrees), the massless particles propagate at 45 degrees, just as in flat spacetime (Fig. 5.1). The null coordinates $(\mechanical{u},\mechanical{v})$ are therefore well suited to the description of (radial) null geodesics. In these coordinates the Schwarzschild metric takes the form

$$
\mathrm d\mechanical{s}^2=-(1-2\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{r})\,\mathrm d\mechanical{u}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{v}+\mechanical{r}^2\mathrm d\Omega^2. \tag{5.6}
$$

Here, $\mechanical{r}$ appears no longer as a coordinate, but as the function of $\mechanical{u}$ and $\mechanical{v}$ defined implicitly by $\mechanical{r^*}(\mechanical{r})=\frac12(\mechanical{v}-\mechanical{u})$. In these coordinates the surface $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{M}$ appears at $\mechanical{v}-\mechanical{u}=-\infty$, and it is still the locus of a coordinate singularity.

To see how this coordinate singularity might be removed, we focus our attention on a small neighbourhood of the surface $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{M}$, in which the relation $\mechanical{r^*}(\mechanical{r})$ can be approximated by $\mechanical{r^*}\simeq2\mechanical{M}\ln|\mechanical{r}/2\mechanical{M}-1|$. This implies that $\mechanical{r}/2\mechanical{M}\simeq1\pm e^{\mechanical{r^*}/2\mechanical{M}}=1\pm e^{(\mechanical{v}-\mechanical{u})/4\mechanical{M}}$, and $f\simeq\pm e^{(\mechanical{v}-\mechanical{u})/4\mechanical{M}}$. Here and below, the upper sign refers to the part of the neighbourhood corresponding to $\mechanical{r}>2\mechanical{M}$, while the lower sign refers to $\mechanical{r}<2\mechanical{M}$. The metric (5.6) becomes

$$
\mathrm d\mechanical{s}^2\simeq\mp\left(e^{-\mechanical{u}/4\mechanical{M}}\mathrm d\mechanical{u}\right)
\left(e^{\mechanical{v}/4\mechanical{M}}\mathrm d\mechanical{v}\right)+\mechanical{r}^2\mathrm d\Omega^2.
$$

<!-- Source: PDF page 182, printed page 166. -->

This expression motivates the introduction of a new set of null coordinates, $\mechanical{U}$ and $\mechanical{V}$, defined by

$$
\mechanical{U}=\mp e^{-\mechanical{u}/4\mechanical{M}},\qquad
\mechanical{V}=e^{\mechanical{v}/4\mechanical{M}}. \tag{5.7}
$$

In terms of these the metric will be well behaved near $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{M}$. Going back to the exact expression (5.5) for $\mechanical{r^*}$, we have that $e^{\mechanical{r^*}/2\mechanical{M}}=e^{(\mechanical{v}-\mechanical{u})/4\mechanical{M}}=\mp\mechanical{U}\mechanical{V}$, or

$$
e^{\mechanical{r}/2\mechanical{M}}\left(\frac{\mechanical{r}}{2\mechanical{M}}-1\right)=-\mechanical{U}\mechanical{V}, \tag{5.8}
$$

which implicitly gives $\mechanical{r}$ as a function of $\mechanical{U}\mechanical{V}$. You may check that the Schwarzschild metric is now given by

$$
\mathrm d\mechanical{s}^2=-\frac{32\mechanical{M}^3}{\mechanical{r}}e^{-\mechanical{r}/2\mechanical{M}}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{U}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{V}+\mechanical{r}^2\mathrm d\Omega^2. \tag{5.9}
$$

This is manifestly regular at $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{M}$. The coordinates $\mechanical{U}$ and $\mechanical{V}$ are called *null Kruskal coordinates*. In a Kruskal diagram (a map of the $\mechanical{U}$–$\mechanical{V}$ plane; see Fig. 5.2), outgoing light rays move along curves $\mechanical{U}=\mathrm{constant}$, while ingoing light rays move along curves $\mechanical{V}=\mathrm{constant}$.

In the Kruskal coordinates, a surface of constant $\mechanical{r}$ is described by an equation of the form $\mechanical{U}\mechanical{V}=\mathrm{constant}$, which corresponds to a two-branch hyperbola in the $\mechanical{U}$–$\mechanical{V}$ plane. For example, $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{M}$ becomes $\mechanical{U}\mechanical{V}=0$, while $\mechanical{r}=0$ becomes $\mechanical{U}\mechanical{V}=1$. There are *two* copies of each surface $\mechanical{r}=\mathrm{constant}$ in a Kruskal diagram. For example, $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{M}$ can be either $\mechanical{U}=0$ or $\mechanical{V}=0$. The Kruskal coordinates therefore reveal the existence of a much larger manifold than the portion covered by the original Schwarzschild coordinates. In a Kruskal diagram, this portion is labelled **I**. The Kruskal coordinates do not only allow the continuation of the metric through $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{M}$ into region **II**, they also allow continuation into regions **III** and **IV**.
