> [!TLDR]
> In an accreting Vaidya black hole, the apparent horizon is spacelike during growth and lies inside the null event horizon.

<!-- Source: PDF page 188, printed page 172. -->

The event and apparent horizons of the Schwarzschild spacetime coincide, and it may not be clear why the two concepts need to be distinguished. This coincidence, however, is a consequence of the fact that the spacetime is stationary; for more general black-hole spacetimes the event and apparent horizons are distinct hypersurfaces. To illustrate this we introduce a simple, non-stationary black-hole spacetime.

<!-- Source: PDF page 189, printed page 173. -->

We express the Schwarzschild metric in terms of ingoing Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates,

$$
\mathrm d\mechanical{s}^2=-f\,\mathrm d\mechanical{v}^2+2\,\mathrm d\mechanical{v}\,\mathrm d\mechanical{r}+\mechanical{r}^2\mathrm d\Omega^2, \tag{5.16}
$$

and we allow the mass function to depend on advanced time $\mechanical{v}$:

$$
f=1-\frac{2\mechanical{m}(\mechanical{v})}{\mechanical{r}}. \tag{5.17}
$$

This gives the *ingoing Vaidya metric*, a solution to the Einstein field equations with stress-energy tensor

$$
\energy{T_{\alpha\beta}}=\frac{\mathrm d\mechanical{m}/\mathrm d\mechanical{v}}{4\pi\mechanical{r}^2}\mechanical{\ell_\alpha}\mechanical{\ell_\beta}, \tag{5.18}
$$

where $\mechanical{\ell_\alpha}=-\partial_\alpha\mechanical{v}$ is tangent to ingoing null geodesics. This stress-energy tensor describes *null dust*, a pressureless fluid with energy density $(\mathrm d\mechanical{m}/\mathrm d\mechanical{v})/(4\pi\mechanical{r}^2)$ and four-velocity $\mechanical{\ell^\alpha}$. (A similar, outgoing Vaidya solution was considered in Section 4.3.5. A notable difference between these solutions is that here, the mass function must *increase* for $\energy{T_{\alpha\beta}}$ to satisfy the standard energy conditions.)

Consider the following situation. A black hole, initially of mass $\mechanical{m_1}$, is irradiated (with ingoing null dust) during a finite interval of advanced time (between $\mechanical{v_1}$ and $\mechanical{v_2}$) so that its mass increases to $\mechanical{m_2}$. Such a spacetime is described by the Vaidya metric, with a mass function given by

$$
\mechanical{m}(\mechanical{v})=
\begin{cases}
\mechanical{m_1}&\mechanical{v}\le\mechanical{v_1},\\
\mechanical{m_{12}}(\mechanical{v})&\mechanical{v_1}<\mechanical{v}<\mechanical{v_2},\\
\mechanical{m_2}&\mechanical{v}\ge\mechanical{v_2},
\end{cases}
$$

where $\mechanical{m_{12}}(\mechanical{v})$ increases smoothly from $\mechanical{m_1}$ to $\mechanical{m_2}$. We would like to determine the physical significance of the surfaces $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{m_1}$, $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{m_{12}}(\mechanical{v})$, and $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{m_2}$, and find the precise location of the event horizon.

It should be clear that $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{m_1}$ and $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{m_2}$ describe the apparent horizon when $\mechanical{v}\le\mechanical{v_1}$ and $\mechanical{v}\ge\mechanical{v_2}$, respectively. More generally, we will show that the apparent horizon of the Vaidya spacetime is always located at $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{m}(\mechanical{v})$.

The null vector field $\mechanical{k_\alpha}\mathrm dx^\alpha=-f\,\mathrm d\mechanical{v}+2\,\mathrm d\mechanical{r}$ is tangent to a congruence of outgoing null geodesics. It does not, however, satisfy the geodesic equation in affine-parameter form: As a brief calculation reveals, $\mechanical{k_\alpha}{}_{;\beta}\mechanical{k^\beta}=\mechanical{\kappa}\mechanical{k_\alpha}$ where $\mechanical{\kappa}=2\mechanical{m}(\mechanical{v})/\mechanical{r}^2$. To calculate the expansion of the outgoing null geodesics, we need to introduce an affine parameter $\mechanical{\lambda^*}$ and a rescaled tangent vector $\mechanical{k^\alpha_*}$. (The calculation can also be handled via the results of Section 2.6, Problem 8.) As was shown in Section 1.3, the desired relation between these vectors is $\mechanical{k^\alpha_*}=e^{-\Gamma}\mechanical{k^\alpha}$, where $\mathrm d\Gamma/\mathrm d\mechanical{\lambda}=\mechanical{\kappa}(\mechanical{\lambda})$ with $\mechanical{\lambda}$ denoting the original parameter. We have

<!-- Source: PDF page 190, printed page 174. -->

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{\theta}&=\mechanical{k^\alpha_*}{}_{;\alpha}\\
&=e^{-\Gamma}\left(\mechanical{k^\alpha}{}_{;\alpha}-\Gamma_{,\alpha}\mechanical{k^\alpha}\right)\\
&=e^{-\Gamma}\left(\mechanical{k^\alpha}{}_{;\alpha}-\frac{\mathrm d\Gamma}{\mathrm d\mechanical{\lambda}}\right)\\
&=e^{-\Gamma}\left(\mechanical{k^\alpha}{}_{;\alpha}-\mechanical{\kappa}\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

Here, the factor $\mechanical{k^\alpha}{}_{;\alpha}-\mechanical{\kappa}$ is the congruence’s expansion when measured in terms of the initial parameter $\mechanical{\lambda}$ — it is equal to $(\delta\mechanical{A})^{-1}\mathrm d(\delta\mechanical{A})/\mathrm d\mechanical{\lambda}$, where $\delta\mechanical{A}$ is the congruence’s cross-sectional area. The factor $e^{-\Gamma}$ converts it to $(\delta\mechanical{A})^{-1}\mathrm d(\delta\mechanical{A})/\mathrm d\mechanical{\lambda^*}$, and this operation does not affect the sign of $\mechanical{\theta}$. A simple computation gives $\mechanical{k^\alpha}{}_{;\alpha}=2(\mechanical{r}-\mechanical{m})/\mechanical{r}^2$ and we arrive at

$$
e^\Gamma\mechanical{\theta}=\frac{2}{\mechanical{r}^2}[\mechanical{r}-2\mechanical{m}(\mechanical{v})].
$$

So $\mechanical{\theta}=0$ on the surface $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{m}(\mechanical{v})$, and we conclude that the apparent horizon begins at $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{m_1}$ for $\mechanical{v}\le\mechanical{v_1}$, follows $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{m_{12}}(\mechanical{v})$ in the interval $\mechanical{v_1}<\mechanical{v}<\mechanical{v_2}$, and remains at $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{m_2}$ for $\mechanical{v}\ge\mechanical{v_2}$.

We may now show that while the apparent horizon is a null hypersurface before $\mechanical{v}=\mechanical{v_1}$ and after $\mechanical{v}=\mechanical{v_2}$, it is *spacelike* in the interval $\mechanical{v_1}\le\mechanical{v}\le\mechanical{v_2}$. This follows at once from the fact that if $\Phi\equiv\mechanical{r}-2\mechanical{m}(\mechanical{v})=0$ describes the apparent horizon, then

$$
g^{\alpha\beta}\Phi_{,\alpha}\Phi_{,\beta}=-4\frac{\mathrm d\mechanical{m}}{\mathrm d\mechanical{v}}
$$

is negative (so that the normal $\Phi_{,\alpha}$ is timelike) if $\mathrm d\mechanical{m}/\mathrm d\mechanical{v}>0$ (so that the energy conditions are satisfied). We therefore see that the apparent horizon is null when the spacetime is stationary, but that it is spacelike otherwise.

Where is the event horizon? Clearly it must coincide with the surface $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{m_2}$ in the future of $\mechanical{v}=\mechanical{v_2}$. But what is its extension to the past of $\mechanical{v}=\mechanical{v_2}$? Because the event horizon is defined as a *causal boundary* in spacetime, it must be a null hypersurface generated by null geodesics (more will be said on this in Section 5.4). The event horizon can therefore not coincide with the apparent horizon in the past of $\mechanical{v}=\mechanical{v_2}$. Instead, its location is determined by finding the outgoing null geodesics of the Vaidya spacetime that connect smoothly with the generators of the surface $\mechanical{r}=2\mechanical{m_2}$. (See Fig. 5.7; a particular example is worked out in Section 5.7, Problem 2.) It is clear that the generators of the event horizon have to be expanding in the past of $\mechanical{v}=\mechanical{v_2}$ if they are to be stationary (in the sense that $\mechanical{\theta}=0$) in the future.

Indeed, supposing that the null energy condition is satisfied (which will be true if $\mathrm d\mechanical{m}/\mathrm d\mechanical{v}>0$), the focusing theorem (Section 2.4) implies that the congruence formed by the null generators of the event horizon will be focused by the infalling null dust; a zero expansion in the future of $\mechanical{v}=\mechanical{v_2}$ guarantees a positive expansion in the past. The event horizon is therefore generated by those null geodesics that undergo just the right amount of focusing, so that after encountering the last of the infalling matter, their expansion goes to zero.

![[relativists-toolkit-fig-5-7-irradiated-horizons.svg|**Fig. 5.7** Black hole irradiated with ingoing null dust.]]

The event horizon coincides with the apparent horizon only in the future of $\mechanical{v}=\mechanical{v_2}$. In the past, because the apparent horizon has a spacelike segment while the event horizon is everywhere null, the apparent horizon lies within the event horizon, that is, inside the black hole (Fig. 5.7). As we shall see in Section 5.4, this observation is quite general.

It is a remarkable property of the event horizon that the entire future history of the spacetime must be known before its position can be determined: The black hole’s final state must be known before the horizon’s null generators can be identified. This *teleological* property is not shared by the apparent horizon, whose location at any given time (as represented by a spacelike hypersurface) depends only on the properties of the spacetime at that time.
