> [!TLDR]
> The expansion scalar equals the fractional rate of change of a congruence's proper cross-sectional volume.

<!-- PDF page 59; printed page 43 -->

We now prove that $\mechanical{\theta}$ is equal to the fractional rate of change of $\delta\mechanical{V}$, the congruence's cross-sectional volume:

$$
\mechanical{\theta}=\frac{1}{\delta\mechanical{V}}\frac{d}{d\mechanical{t}}\delta\mechanical{V}.
\tag{2.21}
$$

Although this may already be obvious from Eqs. (2.16) and (2.17), it is still instructive to go through a formal proof. The first step is to introduce the notions of cross-section, and cross-sectional volume.

Select a particular geodesic $\gamma$ from the congruence, and on this geodesic, pick a point $P$ at which $\mechanical{\tau}=\mechanical{\tau}_P$. Construct, in a small neighbourhood around $P$, a small set $\delta\Sigma(\mechanical{\tau}_P)$ of points $P'$ such that (i) through each of these points there passes another geodesic from the congruence, and (ii) at each point $P'$, $\mechanical{\tau}$ is also equal to $\mechanical{\tau}_P$. This set forms a three-dimensional region, a small segment of the hypersurface $\mechanical{\tau}=\mechanical{\tau}_P$ (Fig. 2.6). We assume that the parameterization has been adjusted so that $\gamma$ intersects $\delta\Sigma(\mechanical{\tau}_P)$ orthogonally. (There is no requirement that other geodesics do, as the congruence may not be hypersurface orthogonal.) We shall call $\delta\Sigma(\mechanical{\tau}_P)$ the congruence's *cross section* around the geodesic $\gamma$, at proper time $\mechanical{\tau}=\mechanical{\tau}_P$. We want to calculate the volume of this hypersurface segment, and compare it with the volume of $\delta\Sigma(\mechanical{\tau}_Q)$, where $Q$ is a neighbouring point on $\gamma$.

We introduce coordinates on $\delta\Sigma(\mechanical{\tau}_P)$ by assigning a label $y^a$ $(a=1,2,3)$ to each point $P'$ in the set. Recalling that through each of these points there passes a geodesic from the congruence, we see that we may use $y^a$ to label the geodesics themselves. By demanding that each geodesic keep its label as it moves away from $\delta\Sigma(\mechanical{\tau}_P)$, we simultaneously obtain a coordinate system $y^a$ in $\delta\Sigma(\mechanical{\tau}_Q)$ or any other cross section. This construction therefore defines a coordinate system $(\mechanical{\tau},y^a)$ in a neighbourhood of the geodesic $\gamma$, and there exists a transformation between this system and the one originally in use: $x^\alpha=x^\alpha(\mechanical{\tau},y^a)$. Because $y^a$ is constant along the geodesics, we have

$$
\mechanical{u}^\alpha=\left(\frac{\partial x^\alpha}{\partial\mechanical{\tau}}\right)_{y^a}.
\tag{2.22}
$$

On the other hand, the vectors

$$
e_a^\alpha=\left(\frac{\partial x^\alpha}{\partial y^a}\right)_{\mechanical{\tau}}
\tag{2.23}
$$

are tangent to the cross sections. These relations imply $\mathcal L_u e_a^\alpha=0$, and we also have $\mechanical{u}_\alpha e_a^\alpha=0$ holding on $\gamma$ (and only $\gamma$).

We now introduce a three-tensor $h_{ab}$ defined by

$$
h_{ab}=g_{\alpha\beta}e_a^\alpha e_b^\beta.
\tag{2.24}
$$

(A three-tensor is a tensor with respect to coordinate transformations $y^a\to y^{a'}$, but a scalar with respect to transformations $x^\alpha\to x^{\alpha'}$.) This acts as a metric tensor on $\delta\Sigma(\mechanical{\tau})$: For displacements confined to the cross section (so that $d\mechanical{\tau}=0$), $x^\alpha=x^\alpha(y^a)$ and

$$
\begin{aligned}
d\mechanical{s}^2&=g_{\alpha\beta}\,dx^\alpha\,dx^\beta\\
&=g_{\alpha\beta}\left(\frac{\partial x^\alpha}{\partial y^a}\,dy^a\right)
\left(\frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial y^b}\,dy^b\right)\\
&=(g_{\alpha\beta}e_a^\alpha e_b^\beta)\,dy^a\,dy^b\\
&=h_{ab}\,dy^a\,dy^b.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus, $h_{ab}$ is the three-dimensional metric on the congruence's cross sections. Because $\gamma$ is orthogonal to its cross sections $(\mechanical{u}_\alpha e_a^\alpha=0)$, we have that $h_{ab}=h_{\alpha\beta}e_a^\alpha e_b^\beta$ on $\gamma$, where $h_{\alpha\beta}=g_{\alpha\beta}+\mechanical{u}_\alpha\mechanical{u}_\beta$ is the transverse metric. If we define $h^{ab}$ to be the inverse of $h_{ab}$, then it is easy to check that

$$
h^{\alpha\beta}=h^{ab}e_a^\alpha e_b^\beta
\tag{2.25}
$$

on $\gamma$.

<!-- PDF page 61; printed page 45 -->

The three-dimensional volume element on the cross sections, or *cross-sectional volume*, is $\delta\mechanical{V}=\sqrt h\,d^3y$, where $h\equiv\det[h_{ab}]$. Because the coordinates $y^a$ are comoving (since each geodesic moves with a constant value of its coordinates), $d^3y$ does not change as the cross section $\delta\Sigma(\mechanical{\tau})$ evolves from $\mechanical{\tau}=\mechanical{\tau}_P$ to $\mechanical{\tau}=\mechanical{\tau}_Q$. A change in $\delta\mechanical{V}$ therefore comes entirely from a change in $\sqrt h$:

$$
\frac{1}{\delta\mechanical{V}}\frac{d}{d\mechanical{\tau}}\delta\mechanical{V}
=\frac{1}{\sqrt h}\frac{d}{d\mechanical{\tau}}\sqrt h
=\frac12h^{ab}\frac{dh_{ab}}{d\mechanical{\tau}}.
$$

We must now calculate the rate of change of the three-metric:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dh_{ab}}{d\mechanical{\tau}}
&=(g_{\alpha\beta}e_a^\alpha e_b^\beta)_{;\mu}\mechanical{u}^\mu\\
&=g_{\alpha\beta}(e_a^\alpha{}_{;\mu}\mechanical{u}^\mu)e_b^\beta
+g_{\alpha\beta}e_a^\alpha(e_b^\beta{}_{;\mu}\mechanical{u}^\mu)\\
&=g_{\alpha\beta}(\mechanical{u}^\alpha{}_{;\mu}e_a^\mu)e_b^\beta
+g_{\alpha\beta}e_a^\alpha(\mechanical{u}^\beta{}_{;\mu}e_b^\mu)\\
&=\mechanical{u}_{\beta;\alpha}e_a^\alpha e_b^\beta
+\mechanical{u}_{\alpha;\beta}e_a^\alpha e_b^\beta\\
&=(\mechanical{B}_{\alpha\beta}+\mechanical{B}_{\beta\alpha})e_a^\alpha e_b^\beta.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2.26}
$$

Multiplying by $h^{ab}$ and evaluating on $\gamma$, so that Eq. (2.25) may be used, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
h^{ab}\frac{dh_{ab}}{d\mechanical{\tau}}
&=(\mechanical{B}_{\alpha\beta}+\mechanical{B}_{\beta\alpha})(h^{ab}e_a^\alpha e_b^\beta)\\
&=2\mechanical{B}_{\alpha\beta}h^{\alpha\beta}\\
&=2\mechanical{B}_{\alpha\beta}g^{\alpha\beta}\\
&=2\mechanical{\theta}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This establishes that

$$
\mechanical{\theta}=\frac{1}{\sqrt h}\frac{d}{d\mechanical{\tau}}\sqrt h,
\tag{2.27}
$$

which is the same statement as in Eq. (2.21).

| $\mechanical{s}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{m}}$ | Interval | The proper line element within a cross section. |
