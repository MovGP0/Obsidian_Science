> [!TLDR]
> The sigma model links chiral symmetry breaking to nucleon masses, light pions, and low-energy pion scattering.

<!-- PDF page 95; printed page 81 -->

Before applying these lessons to non-Abelian gauge theories, it will be worthwhile to establish some insights that will serve us well when we examine the high-energy behavior of the electroweak theory and the low-energy behavior of quantum chromodynamics. Consider again the Dirac Lagrangian for free nucleons that we constructed in section 2.3. As before, we use the composite spinor

$$
\psi\equiv\begin{pmatrix}p\\n\end{pmatrix} \tag{2.3.13}
$$

to write the free-nucleon Lagrangian compactly as

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}=\bar\psi(i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu-\mechanical{m})\psi. \tag{2.3.14}
$$

The Lagrangian is invariant under global isospin rotations that take $\psi\rightarrow\exp(i\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\boldsymbol\tau/2)\psi$, where $\boldsymbol\tau$ consists of the usual $2\times2$ Pauli isospin matrices and $\boldsymbol\alpha=(\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3)$ is an arbitrary, $x$-independent (three-vector) parameter of the transformation.

<!-- PDF page 96; printed page 82 -->

Now, following Gell-Mann and Lévy [6], let us take the nucleons to be massless. The Lagrangian simplifies to

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}=\bar\psi(i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu)\psi. \tag{5.4.1}
$$

This form remains invariant under global isospin rotations, but it is also invariant under the group of *chiral symmetries*, $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L\otimes\mathrm{SU}(2)_R$. To develop this statement, we designate the left- and right-handed components of the nucleon spinor as

$$
\psi_L=\frac12(1-\gamma_5)\psi,\quad\psi_R=\frac12(1+\gamma_5)\psi, \tag{5.4.2}
$$

from which

$$
\bar\psi_L=\frac12\bar\psi(1+\gamma_5),\quad\bar\psi_R=\frac12\bar\psi(1-\gamma_5). \tag{5.4.3}
$$

Now we may decompose the Lagrangian as

$$
\begin{aligned}
\energy{\mathcal L}&=i\bar\psi\not\partial\psi
=i\bar\psi[\tfrac12(1-\gamma_5)+\tfrac12(1+\gamma_5)]\not\partial[\tfrac12(1-\gamma_5)+\tfrac12(1+\gamma_5)]\psi\\
&=i\bar\psi_L\not\partial\psi_L+i\bar\psi_R\not\partial\psi_R,
\end{aligned} \tag{5.4.4}
$$

in which left-right cross terms vanish because $\gamma_\mu\gamma_5=-\gamma_5\gamma_\mu$, according to (A.2.21). The absence of terms coupling left and right means that we have the freedom to make separate isospin rotations on the left-handed and right-handed fields:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\psi_L&\rightarrow\psi'_L=\exp\left(i\boldsymbol\alpha_L\cdot\frac{\boldsymbol\tau}2\right)\psi_L\equiv G_L\psi_L,\\
\psi_R&\rightarrow\psi'_R=\exp\left(i\boldsymbol\alpha_R\cdot\frac{\boldsymbol\tau}2\right)\psi_R\equiv G_R\psi_R.
\end{aligned} \tag{5.4.5}
$$

The nucleon mass term that we have eschewed is

$$
\mechanical{m}\bar\psi\psi=\mechanical{m}\bar\psi[\tfrac12(1-\gamma_5)+\tfrac12(1+\gamma_5)]\psi
=\mechanical{m}(\bar\psi_L\psi_R+\bar\psi_R\psi_L), \tag{5.4.6}
$$

which mixes the L and R fields. The answer falls out once we recall that $[\frac12(1\pm\gamma_5)]^2=\frac12(1\pm\gamma_5)$. It is sometimes convenient to recast the transformations $G_L$ and $G_R$ in terms of an $\mathrm{SU}(2)_V$ isospin rotation,

$$
G_I=\exp\left(i\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\frac{\boldsymbol\tau}2\right),\quad\boldsymbol\alpha=\boldsymbol\alpha_R+\boldsymbol\alpha_L, \tag{5.4.7a}
$$

and an $\mathrm{SU}(2)_A$ chiral rotation,

$$
G_5=\exp\left(i\gamma_5\boldsymbol\alpha_5\cdot\frac{\boldsymbol\tau}2\right),\quad\boldsymbol\alpha_5=\boldsymbol\alpha_R-\boldsymbol\alpha_L. \tag{5.4.7b}
$$

The nucleon mass term explicitly breaks the chiral symmetry and leaves only normal isospin invariance intact.

Gell-Mann and Lévy found that they could build a Lagrangian with chiral symmetry and a nucleon mass if the chiral symmetry were spontaneously broken.

To see how this comes about, let us introduce $\boldsymbol\Sigma$, a $2\times2$ matrix of spinless meson fields that transforms under chiral isospin rotations as

$$
\boldsymbol\Sigma\rightarrow\boldsymbol\Sigma'=G_L\boldsymbol\Sigma G_R^\dagger \tag{5.4.8}
$$

and add Yukawa couplings of the mesons and nucleons, so that

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}=\bar\psi(i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu)\psi-g\bar\psi_L\boldsymbol\Sigma\psi_R-g\bar\psi_R\boldsymbol\Sigma^\dagger\psi_L+\energy{\mathcal L_\Sigma}, \tag{5.4.9}
$$

where the final term describes meson self-interactions. If we expand $\boldsymbol\Sigma$ in terms of four real fields as

$$
\boldsymbol\Sigma=\sigma+i\boldsymbol\pi\cdot\boldsymbol\tau, \tag{5.4.10}
$$

the Lagrangian becomes

$$
\begin{aligned}
\energy{\mathcal L}&=\bar\psi(i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu)\psi-g\sigma(\bar\psi_L\psi_R+\bar\psi_R\psi_L)
-ig\boldsymbol\pi\cdot(\bar\psi_L\boldsymbol\tau\psi_R-\bar\psi_R\boldsymbol\tau\psi_L)+\energy{\mathcal L_\Sigma}\\
&=\bar\psi(i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu)\psi-g\sigma\bar\psi\psi+ig\boldsymbol\pi\cdot\bar\psi\boldsymbol\tau\gamma_5\psi+\energy{\mathcal L_\Sigma}.
\end{aligned} \tag{5.4.11}
$$

The form is suggestive: the second term has the look of a potential nucleon mass term if $\langle\sigma\rangle_0$ were nonzero, and the third has the right form to describe the $\pi N\bar N$ coupling if we identify $g^2/4\pi=13.7$ [7].

How can we build meson self-interactions that are invariant under $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L\otimes\mathrm{SU}(2)_R$? The most general invariant without derivatives will be a function of

$$
\boldsymbol\Sigma^\dagger\boldsymbol\Sigma=I\sigma^2+\boldsymbol\pi\cdot\boldsymbol\tau\,\boldsymbol\pi\cdot\boldsymbol\tau=I(\sigma^2+\boldsymbol\pi\cdot\boldsymbol\pi), \tag{5.4.12}
$$

so we write

$$
\energy{\mathcal L_\Sigma}=\frac12(\partial_\mu\sigma)(\partial^\mu\sigma)+\frac12(\partial_\mu\boldsymbol\pi)\cdot(\partial^\mu\boldsymbol\pi)-\energy{V}(\sigma^2+\boldsymbol\pi\cdot\boldsymbol\pi). \tag{5.4.13}
$$

Now consider meson self-interactions dictated by a potential

$$
\energy{V}(\sigma^2+\boldsymbol\pi\cdot\boldsymbol\pi)=\frac{\mechanical{\mu}^2}{2}(\sigma^2+\boldsymbol\pi\cdot\boldsymbol\pi)+\frac{|\lambda|}{4}(\sigma^2+\boldsymbol\pi\cdot\boldsymbol\pi)^2. \tag{5.4.14}
$$

If the parameter $\mechanical{\mu}^2>0$, there is a unique minimum-energy state at $\sigma^2+\boldsymbol\pi\cdot\boldsymbol\pi=0$ for which the full symmetry of the theory is manifest: the left-handed and right-handed components of the proton and neutron remain massless, and the mesons $\sigma$ and $\boldsymbol\pi$ have a common mass $\mechanical{\mu}$. The case $\mechanical{\mu}^2<0$ is more interesting: the minimum of the potential now occurs along a continuum defined by $\sigma^2+\boldsymbol\pi\cdot\boldsymbol\pi=-\mechanical{\mu}^2/|\lambda|\equiv v^2$. To analyze the consequences, it is convenient to rewrite the potential as

$$
\energy{V}(\sigma^2+\boldsymbol\pi\cdot\boldsymbol\pi)=\frac14|\lambda|(\sigma^2+\boldsymbol\pi\cdot\boldsymbol\pi-v^2)^2, \tag{5.4.15}
$$

<!-- PDF page 98; printed page 84 -->

and to exploit the freedom to make $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L\otimes\mathrm{SU}(2)_R$ rotations to bring the vacuum expectation value to the $\sigma$-direction,

$$
\langle\sigma\rangle_0=v,\quad\langle\boldsymbol\pi\rangle_0=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}. \tag{5.4.16}
$$

Evidently the vacuum state is unchanged by isospin rotations but breaks $\mathrm{SU}(2)_A$ symmetry (cf. problem 5.3).

To explore small perturbations about the vacuum state, we set $\sigma'=\sigma-v$, so that $\langle\sigma'\rangle_0=0$. Now the Lagrangian is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\energy{\mathcal L}&=\bar\psi(i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu)\psi-gv\bar\psi\psi-g\sigma'\bar\psi\psi+ig\boldsymbol\pi\cdot\bar\psi\boldsymbol\tau\gamma_5\psi\\
&\quad+\frac12(\partial_\mu\sigma')(\partial^\mu\sigma')+\frac12(\partial_\mu\boldsymbol\pi)\cdot(\partial^\mu\boldsymbol\pi)-\frac14|\lambda|(\sigma'^2+\boldsymbol\pi\cdot\boldsymbol\pi+2\sigma'v)^2,
\end{aligned} \tag{5.4.17}
$$

which describes nucleons with mass $\mechanical{M_N}=gv$ coupled to a scalar field, $\sigma'$, with $\text{mass}^2\,\mechanical{m_{\sigma'}}^2=2|\lambda|v^2=-2\mechanical{\mu}^2$, and to *massless* pseudoscalar pions. The physical pions are far lighter than the other hadrons: $\mechanical{m_\pi}^2/\mechanical{m_p}^2\approx1/50$. Here they emerge, thanks to spontaneous breaking of the chiral symmetry $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L\otimes\mathrm{SU}(2)_R\rightarrow\mathrm{SU}(2)_V$, as Nambu–Goldstone bosons, one for each broken generator of $\mathrm{SU}(2)_A$.

What are the dynamical implications of the model? The conserved currents associated with the Lagrangian (5.4.11) are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf V_\mu&=\bar\psi\gamma_\mu\frac{\boldsymbol\tau}{2}\psi+\boldsymbol\pi\times(\partial_\mu\boldsymbol\pi),\\
\mathbf A_\mu&=\bar\psi\gamma_\mu\frac{\boldsymbol\tau}{2}\gamma_5\psi+\boldsymbol\pi(\partial_\mu\sigma)-\sigma(\partial_\mu\boldsymbol\pi).
\end{aligned} \tag{5.4.18}
$$

In terms of the shifted field $\sigma'=\sigma-v$, the axial current has the form

$$
\mathbf A_\mu=-v(\partial_\mu\boldsymbol\pi)+\text{bilinears in }\boldsymbol\pi,\sigma',\psi. \tag{5.4.19}
$$

The first term is interesting, because it couples the pion to the vacuum through

$$
\langle0|A_\mu^i|\pi^i(\mechanical{p})\rangle=iv\mechanical{p_\mu}\delta^{ij}\equiv if_\pi\mechanical{p_\mu}\delta^{ij}, \tag{5.4.20}
$$

where the last form gives a conventional definition of the pseudoscalar decay constant $f_\pi\approx92\,\unit{MeV}$ [8]. Identifying $f_\pi=v$, we see that the spontaneous breakdown of chiral symmetry may be seen as inducing pion decay. Earlier we were led to identify $g$ as the pion–nucleon coupling. We may combine all our information and test for consistency:

$$
939\,\unit{MeV}=\mechanical{M_N}=gv\overset{?}{=}g_{\pi NN}f_\pi=1215\,\unit{MeV}. \tag{5.4.21}
$$

This is not a quantitative triumph, but the connection (5.4.21) is a simplified version of the celebrated Goldberger–Treiman relation [9],

$$
1192\,\unit{MeV}=g_A\mechanical{M_N}=g_{\pi NN}f_\pi=1215\,\unit{MeV}, \tag{5.4.22}
$$

where $g_A\approx1.2695$ is the nucleon axial charge.

Finally, let us look more closely at the meson self-interactions, which appear in the final term of (5.4.17),

$$
\begin{aligned}
\energy{\mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}}}&=-\frac14|\lambda|(\sigma^2+\boldsymbol\pi\cdot\boldsymbol\pi+2\sigma v)^2\\
&=-\frac14|\lambda|[\sigma^4+(\boldsymbol\pi\cdot\boldsymbol\pi)^2+4\sigma^2v^2+2\sigma^2\boldsymbol\pi\cdot\boldsymbol\pi+4\sigma^3v+4v\sigma\boldsymbol\pi\cdot\boldsymbol\pi],
\end{aligned} \tag{5.4.23}
$$

where we have dropped the primes to streamline notation. We have already considered the mass term, $4\sigma^2v^2$. Putting it aside and expanding in components, the interactions are given by

$$
\energy{\mathcal L'_{\mathrm{int}}}=-|\lambda|v\sigma(2\pi^+\pi^-+\pi^0\pi^0+\sigma\sigma)
-\frac14|\lambda|(2\pi^+\pi^-+\pi^0\pi^0+\sigma\sigma)^2. \tag{5.4.24}
$$

The Feynman diagrams that contribute at tree level to $\pi^+\pi^0$ scattering are shown in figure 5.3. Being attentive to combinatorial factors, we can read off the Feynman rules for the $\pi\pi\sigma$ vertex $(-2i|\lambda|v)$ and for the four-pion contact term $(-2i|\lambda|)$. Accordingly, the scattering amplitude is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal M(\pi^+\pi^0\rightarrow\pi^+\pi^0)&=(-2i|\lambda|v)^2\frac{i}{\mechanical{q}^2-\mechanical{m_\sigma}^2}-2i|\lambda|\\
&=-\frac{i\mechanical{m_\sigma}^2}{v^2}\frac{\mechanical{q}^2}{\mechanical{q}^2-\mechanical{m_\sigma}^2},
\end{aligned} \tag{5.4.25}
$$

where we have used $\mechanical{m_\sigma}^2=2|\lambda|v^2$ to simplify the result. Recalling our identification of the vacuum expectation value $v=f_\pi$, we can recast the scattering amplitude as

$$
\mathcal M=\frac{i\mechanical{q}^2}{f_\pi^2}\frac1{1-\mechanical{q}^2/\mechanical{m_\sigma}^2}
\approx\frac{i\mechanical{q}^2}{f_\pi^2}\left(1+\frac{\mechanical{q}^2}{\mechanical{m_\sigma}^2}+\cdots\right), \tag{5.4.26}
$$

where the last expression holds for $\mechanical{q}^2\ll\mechanical{m_\sigma}^2$. Two features are noteworthy: first, to leading order in $\mechanical{q}^2$, the amplitude depends only on the symmetry-breaking parameter $f_\pi$, not on $\mechanical{m_\sigma}$; second, the amplitude vanishes as $\mechanical{q}^2\rightarrow0$. The vanishing of the amplitude at zero momentum is a universal feature of theories built on chiral symmetry.

To close this excursion, we remark that the interaction Lagrangian (5.4.24) is identical in form to the interaction Lagrangian for the Higgs sector of the

electroweak theory, with the identification $(\pi^+,\pi^0,\pi^-)\rightarrow(w^+,z,w^-)$, the three massless scalars that become longitudinal components of $W^+,Z^0,W^-$, and $\sigma\rightarrow h$, the massive neutral scalar that becomes the Higgs boson [10].
