> [!TLDR]
> In the Abelian Higgs model, the gauge boson gains mass and the Goldstone field supplies its longitudinal component.

We consider locally gauge-invariant Lagrangians that give rise to spontaneously broken symmetries. An unexpected cooperation will arise between the massless gauge fields (such as made the Yang–Mills theory an unacceptable description of the strong interactions of nucleons) and the massless Nambu–Goldstone bosons

<!-- PDF page 93; printed page 79 -->

that have been seen to accompany spontaneous symmetry breaking. The simplest example of this interplay is provided by the Abelian Higgs model [3], which is nothing but the locally gauge-invariant extension of the Goldstone model discussed in the previous section: a U(1)-invariant theory that describes, in the absence of spontaneous symmetry breaking, the electrodynamics of charged scalars. The Lagrangian is simply

$$
\energy{\mathcal L}=|D^\mu\phi|^2-\mechanical{\mu}^2|\phi|^2-|\lambda|(\phi\phi^*)^2-\frac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}, \tag{5.3.1}
$$

where

$$
\phi=\frac{\phi_1+i\phi_2}{\sqrt2} \tag{5.3.2}
$$

is a complex scalar field [4] and, as usual,

$$
D_\mu\equiv\partial_\mu+i\electric{q}\magnetic{A_\mu} \tag{5.3.3}
$$

and

$$
F_{\mu\nu}\equiv\partial_\nu\magnetic{A_\mu}-\partial_\mu\magnetic{A_\nu}. \tag{5.3.4}
$$

The Lagrangian (5.3.1) is invariant under U(1) rotations

$$
\phi\rightarrow\phi'=e^{i\signal{\theta}}\phi \tag{5.3.5}
$$

and under the local gauge transformations

$$
\begin{aligned}
\phi(x)&\rightarrow\phi'(x)=e^{i\electric{q}\alpha(x)}\phi(x),\\
\magnetic{A_\mu}(x)&\rightarrow\magnetic{A'_\mu}(x)=\magnetic{A_\mu}(x)-\partial_\mu\alpha(x).
\end{aligned} \tag{5.3.6}
$$

As usual, there are two cases, depending upon the parameters of the effective potential.

For $\mechanical{\mu}^2>0$, the potential has a unique minimum at $\phi=0$, and the exact symmetry of the Lagrangian is preserved. The spectrum is simply that of ordinary QED of charged scalars, with a single massless photon $\magnetic{A_\mu}$ and two scalar particles, $\phi$ and $\phi^*$, with common mass $\mechanical{\mu}$.

The situation when $\mechanical{\mu}^2=-|\mechanical{\mu}|^2<0$ is that of a spontaneously broken symmetry and requires a closer analysis. The potential has a continuum of absolute minima, corresponding to a continuum of degenerate vacua, at

$$
\langle|\phi|^2\rangle_0=\frac{-\mechanical{\mu}^2}{2|\lambda|}\equiv\frac{v^2}{2}. \tag{5.3.7}
$$

To explore the spectrum, we shift the fields in order to rewrite the Lagrangian (5.3.1) in terms of displacements from the physical vacuum. The latter may be chosen,

<!-- PDF page 94; printed page 80 -->

without loss of generality, as

$$
\langle\phi\rangle_0=\frac v{\sqrt2}, \tag{5.3.8}
$$

where $v>0$ is a real number. We then define the shifted field

$$
\phi'=\phi-\langle\phi\rangle_0, \tag{5.3.9}
$$

which is conveniently parameterized in terms of

$$
\begin{aligned}
\phi&=\frac{e^{i\zeta/v}(v+\eta)}{\sqrt2}\\
&\approx\frac{(v+\eta+i\zeta)}{\sqrt2}.
\end{aligned} \tag{5.3.10}
$$

Then the Lagrangian appropriate for the study of small oscillations is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\energy{\mathcal L_{\mathrm{so}}}&=\frac12[(\partial_\mu\eta)(\partial^\mu\eta)+2\mechanical{\mu}^2\eta^2]+\frac12[(\partial_\mu\zeta)(\partial^\mu\zeta)]\\
&\quad-\frac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}+\electric{q}v\magnetic{A_\mu}(\partial^\mu\zeta)+\frac{\electric{q}^2v^2}{2}\magnetic{A_\mu}\magnetic{A^\mu}+\cdots.
\end{aligned} \tag{5.3.11}
$$

As we expect from out study of the Goldstone phenomenon, the $\eta$-field, which corresponds to radial oscillations, has a $(\text{mass})^2=-2\mechanical{\mu}^2>0$. The gauge field $\magnetic{A_\mu}$ appears to have acquired a mass, but it is mixed up in the penultimate term with the seemingly massless $\zeta$-field.

An astute choice of gauge will make it easier to sort out the spectrum of the spontaneously broken theory. To this end, it is convenient to rewrite the terms involving $\magnetic{A_\mu}$ and $\zeta$ as

$$
\frac{\electric{q}^2v^2}{2}\left(\magnetic{A_\mu}+\frac1{\electric{q}v}\partial_\mu\zeta\right)\left(\magnetic{A^\mu}+\frac1{\electric{q}v}\partial^\mu\zeta\right), \tag{5.3.12}
$$

a form that pleads for the gauge transformation

$$
\magnetic{A_\mu}\rightarrow\magnetic{A'_\mu}=\magnetic{A_\mu}+\frac1{\electric{q}v}\partial^\mu\zeta, \tag{5.3.13}
$$

which corresponds to the phase rotation on the scalar field

$$
\phi\rightarrow\phi'=e^{-i\zeta(x)/v}\phi(x)=\frac{v+\eta}{\sqrt2}. \tag{5.3.14}
$$

Knowing that the Lagrangian is locally gauge invariant, we may return to the original expression (5.3.1) to compute

$$
\energy{\mathcal L_{\mathrm{so}}}=\frac12[(\partial_\mu\eta)(\partial^\mu\eta)+2\mechanical{\mu}^2\eta^2]-\frac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}+\frac{\electric{q}^2v^2}{2}\magnetic{A'_\mu}\magnetic{A'^{\mu}}, \tag{5.3.15}
$$

<!-- PDF page 95; printed page 81; text before section 5.4 -->

plus an irrelevant constant. In this gauge the particle spectrum is manifest:

- an $\eta$-field, with $(\text{mass})^2=-2\mechanical{\mu}^2>0$;
- a massive vector field $\magnetic{A'_\mu}$, with mass $=\electric{q}v$;
- no $\zeta$-field.

By virtue of our choice of gauge, the $\zeta$-particle has disappeared from the Lagrangian entirely. Where has it gone? The gauge transformation (5.3.13) shows that what was formerly the $\zeta$-field has become the longitudinal component of the massive vector field $\magnetic{A'_\mu}$. Before spontaneous symmetry breaking, the theory had four particle degrees of freedom: two scalars $\phi$ and $\phi^*$ plus two helicity states of the massless gauge field $\magnetic{A_\mu}$. After spontaneous symmetry breaking, we are left with one scalar particle $\eta$ plus three helicity states of the massive gauge field $\magnetic{A'_\mu}$, for a total of four particle degrees of freedom. In common parlance it is said that the massless photon “swallowed” the massless Nambu–Goldstone boson to become a massive vector boson. The remaining massive scalar ($\eta$) is known as the Higgs boson. The special gauge in which the particle spectrum became transparent is known as the unitary gauge, or U-gauge, because only physical states appear in the Lagrangian. This is not to say that the unitarity of the S-matrix, or the complete set of Feynman rules for the theory, is obvious in this gauge.

This is a truly remarkable result, suggesting as it does the possibility of constructing spontaneously broken gauge theories in which the interactions are mediated by massive vector bosons, rather than the phenomenologically unacceptable massless vector bosons of the unbroken theories. In a sense, each of the massless-particle diseases that we have encountered—gauge bosons and Nambu–Goldstone bosons—has provided the cure for the other [5]. Understanding the workings of spontaneous symmetry breaking in gauge theories has been the work of many hands, but the general strategy has come to be called the Higgs mechanism, in honor of one of the major contributors.
