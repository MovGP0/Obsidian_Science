> [!TLDR]
> The SU(5) model combines quarks and leptons, relates gauge couplings, and uses two stages of symmetry breaking to generate particle masses.

The choice of a unifying gauge group $G$ is to be guided by requirements implicit in our discussion of the motivation for unification. It is worthwhile to formulate these requirements somewhat more precisely. It will be necessary to assume, as indicated in (9.1.6), that $G$ contains $\mathrm{SU}(3)_{\mathrm{c}}\otimes\mathrm{SU}(2)_{\mathrm{L}}\otimes\mathrm{U}(1)_Y$. Next, because of the fermion content we wish to build into the theory, $G$ must admit complex representations. To see why this is so, let us examine the $\mathrm{SU}(3)_{\mathrm{c}}\otimes\mathrm{SU}(2)_{\mathrm{L}}\otimes\mathrm{U}(1)$ content of the

<!-- PDF page 406; printed page 392 -->

“first generation” of fermions $u,d,\nu_e$, and $e$. (There is no need to introduce the complication of flavor mixing at this stage.)

It will be convenient to express all the fermions in terms of left-handed fields only. We have frequently used the chiral decomposition of a Dirac field,

$$
\psi=\frac{1}{2}(1-\gamma_5)\psi+\frac{1}{2}(1+\gamma_5)\psi
=\psi_{\mathrm{L}}+\psi_{\mathrm{R}}, \tag{9.2.1}
$$

which is particularly useful because the gauge-field couplings preserve chirality. If the field $\psi$ is understood to annihilate a particle, the charge-conjugate field

$$
\psi^c\equiv C\bar{\psi}^{\mathrm{T}}, \tag{9.2.2}
$$

where T designates transpose, annihilates an antiparticle. In the (standard) representation of Dirac matrices that we have adopted, the charge-conjugation matrix is given by

$$
C=i\gamma^2\gamma^0=-C^{-1}=-C^\dagger=-C^{\mathrm{T}}, \tag{9.2.3}
$$

for which

$$
C\gamma_\mu C^{-1}=-\gamma_\mu^{\mathrm{T}}. \tag{9.2.4}
$$

It is then easy to see that the charge conjugate of a right-handed field is left-handed, for

$$
\begin{aligned}
\psi_{\mathrm{L}}^c&=\frac{1}{2}(1-\gamma_5)\psi^c
=\frac{1}{2}(1-\gamma_5)C\bar{\psi}^{\mathrm{T}}\\
&=C\frac{1}{2}(1-\gamma_5)\bar{\psi}^{\mathrm{T}}
=C\left[\bar{\psi}\frac{1}{2}(1-\gamma_5)\right]^{\mathrm{T}}
=C(\bar{\psi}_{\mathrm{R}})^{\mathrm{T}};
\end{aligned} \tag{9.2.5}
$$

similarly,

$$
\bar{\psi}_{\mathrm{L}}^c=\psi_{\mathrm{R}}^{\mathrm{T}}C
=-\psi_{\mathrm{R}}^{\mathrm{T}}C^{-1}. \tag{9.2.6}
$$

Thus we enumerate the fermion fields of the first generation in terms of their $\mathrm{SU}(3)_{\mathrm{c}}\otimes\mathrm{SU}(2)_{\mathrm{L}}\otimes\mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ quantum numbers as

$$
\left.\begin{aligned}
u_{\mathrm{L}},d_{\mathrm{L}}&:(3,2)_{Y=1/3}\\
d_{\mathrm{L}}^c&:(3^*,1)_{2/3}\\
u_{\mathrm{L}}^c&:(3^*,1)_{-4/3}\\
\nu_{\mathrm{L}},e_{\mathrm{L}}&:(1,2)_{-1}\\
e_{\mathrm{L}}^c&:(1,1)_2
\end{aligned}\right\}. \tag{9.2.7}
$$

To incorporate a massive neutrino (cf. §6.7), we could add

$$
N_{\mathrm{L}}^c:(1,1)_0. \tag{9.2.8}
$$

Evidently this set of representations is not equal to its complex conjugate, so we must require that $G$ possess complex representations to accommodate these fermions. As a final requirement, we demand that $G$ have only a single coupling constant. For the purposes of this discussion, that will be what is meant by unification.

We may search systematically for candidate-unifying groups that meet these (or indeed other) criteria. The smallest such group is $\mathrm{SU}(5)$. Let us now see how it meets the requirements we have set out. To analyze the structure of the model, it is helpful to refer to the $\mathrm{SU}(3)_{\mathrm{c}}\otimes\mathrm{SU}(2)_{\mathrm{L}}\otimes\mathrm{U}(1)$ decomposition of some of the low-dimensioned representations of $\mathrm{SU}(5)$ given in table 9.1. The 15-dimensional representation, which might be the first hope to accommodate the 15 fermions of the first generation (putting aside a right-handed neutrino), contains color-sextet quarks and, thus, is not acceptable. Similarly, the 45-dimensional representation, which could be hoped to contain three such fermion generations, contains color-octet and color-antisextet elements. However, the first-generation fermions may be assigned to the $5^*$ and $10$ representations as

$$
5^*:\quad\psi_{j\mathrm{L}}=
\left(\begin{array}{c}
d_1^c\\
d_2^c\\
d_3^c\\
\hline
e\\
-\nu_e
\end{array}\right)_{\mathrm{L}} \tag{9.2.9}
$$

and

$$
10:\quad\psi_{\mathrm{L}}^{jk}=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
\left(\begin{array}{ccc|cc}
0&u_3^c&-u_2^c&-u_1&-d_1\\
-u_3^c&0&u_1^c&-u_2&-d_2\\
u_2^c&-u_1^c&0&-u_3&-d_3\\
\hline
u_1&u_2&u_3&0&-e^c\\
d_1&d_2&d_3&e^c&0
\end{array}\right)_{\mathrm{L}}, \tag{9.2.10}
$$

where the quark colors (red, green, blue) have been denoted (1, 2, 3). The factor $1/\sqrt{2}$ in (9.2.10) is a convenient normalization, and many of the signs are matters of convention. The identification of $(e,-\nu_e)^{\mathrm{T}}$ as a $2^*$ of weak isospin follows from the assignment of $(\nu_e,e)$ as a $2$. An additional (right-handed) neutrino state (9.2.8) could be assigned as an $\mathrm{SU}(5)$ singlet.[^9-2-sterile-neutrino] Although it would have been pleasing to assign all the particles of the first generation to a single irreducible representation, as can be done in the closely analogous group $\mathrm{SO}(10)$ (see problem 9.1), there is nothing intrinsically objectionable about this assignment.

We see that the $\mathrm{SU}(5)$ model can accommodate, if not predict, the known elementary fermions in terms of 15-member generations assigned to the (reducible) $5^*\oplus10$ representations. These assignments bring with them some agreeable features. First, because the electric charge operator $\electric{Q}$ is to be a generator of $\mathrm{SU}(5)$, the sum of electric charges over any representation must be zero. (Indeed, this requirement influenced the assignments presented earlier.) This means, in particular, that

$$
\electric{Q}(d^c)=(-1/3)\electric{Q}(e), \tag{9.2.11}
$$

which explains the quantization of electric charge. In fact, although it appears here almost as an arithmetic triviality, charge quantization is deeply related to the existence of magnetic monopoles [11] in unified theories.

We know that the $\mathrm{SU}(3)_{\mathrm{c}}\otimes\mathrm{SU}(2)_{\mathrm{L}}\otimes\mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ subgroup upon which the nonunified gauge theories of strong, weak, and electromagnetic interactions are founded is anomaly free for the representation we have chosen. This was in fact part of the motivation for identifying quark and lepton doublets as belonging to the same fermion generation. It remains to verify that the unified theory is also anomaly-free. This may be done either by direct computation or by using the fact [12] that the anomaly for a representation $R$ may be characterized by

$$
\operatorname{tr}(\{T^a,T^b\}T^c)=A(R)d^{abc}, \tag{9.2.12}
$$

where the $T^a$ are the normalized generators of the representation $R$ and the $d^{abc}$ are the symmetric structure constants of the Lie algebra. For a representation that is given by the completely antisymmetric product of $p$ fundamental representations of

<!-- PDF page 409; printed page 395 -->

$\mathrm{SU}(N)$, the coefficient $A(R)$ is given by

$$
A(R)=\frac{(N-3)!(N-2p)}{(N-p-1)!(p-1)!}. \tag{9.2.13}
$$

Thus for the $5^*$ ($p=4$) we find

$$
A(5^*)=-1, \tag{9.2.14}
$$

and for the $10$ ($p=2$) we have

$$
A(10)=1. \tag{9.2.15}
$$

Thus, remarkably, the $5^*$ and $10$ have equal and opposite anomalies and the unified theory is entirely anomaly free.

Let us now verify that the gauge-boson content is also what is required by the known phenomenology. Constructing a gauge theory by standard methods, we shall encounter 24 gauge bosons corresponding to the 24 elements of the adjoint representation of $\mathrm{SU}(5)$. Using the decomposition of table 9.1, we may identify the gauge bosons as follows:

$$
\left.\begin{aligned}
(8,1)_0&\leftrightarrow\text{gluons}\\
(1,3)_0&\leftrightarrow W^+,\ W^-,\ W_3\\
(1,1)_0&\leftrightarrow\mathcal{A}\\
(3,2)_{-5/3}&\leftrightarrow X^{-4/3},\ Y^{-1/3}\\
(3^*,2)_{5/3}&\leftrightarrow X^{4/3},\ Y^{1/3}
\end{aligned}\right\}. \tag{9.2.16}
$$

The first 12 of these are precisely the known gauge bosons of the $\mathrm{SU}(3)_{\mathrm{c}}\otimes\mathrm{SU}(2)_{\mathrm{L}}\otimes\mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ theory: the 8 gluons and 4 electroweak bosons that become, upon spontaneous symmetry breaking, the physical $W^+$, $W^-$, $Z^0$, and photon. The last 12 objects are new in the $\mathrm{SU}(5)$ theory. They are often called *leptoquark bosons* because they mediate transitions between quarks and leptons. The $\mathrm{SU}(5)$ gauge bosons may be displayed in matrix form as

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathsf{H}\sqrt{2}&=
\left(\begin{array}{ccc|cc}
&&&X_1&Y_1\\
&\text{gluons}&&X_2&Y_2\\
&&&X_3&Y_3\\
\hline
X'_1&X'_2&X'_3&W_3/\sqrt{2}&W^+\\
Y'_1&Y'_2&Y'_3&W^-&-W_3/\sqrt{2}
\end{array}\right)\\
&\quad+\frac{\mathcal{A}}{\sqrt{30}}
\left(\begin{array}{ccc|cc}
-2&&&&\\
&-2&&\multicolumn{2}{c}{\large 0}\\
&&-2&&\\
\hline
\multicolumn{3}{c|}{\large 0}&3&\\
&&&&3
\end{array}\right),
\end{aligned} \tag{9.2.17}
$$

<!-- PDF page 410; printed page 396 -->

where the matrix $\mathsf{H}$ is written in terms of the gauge bosons and the normalized generators of the adjoint representation $t^a$ as

$$
\mathsf{H}\equiv\sum_{a=1}^{24}t^aH^a. \tag{9.2.18}
$$

We have separated the $\mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ gauge field to emphasize a crucial difference between the $\mathrm{SU}(5)$ theory and the $\mathrm{SU}(2)_{\mathrm{L}}\otimes\mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ model, which is that the hypercharge coupling is no longer a free parameter. Its strength, as we shall see explicitly, is fixed by its position in the $\mathrm{SU}(5)$ gauge group.

Until now, we have constructed only theories in which the fermions lie in the fundamental representation of the gauge group. (A modest exception to this restriction occurred in problem 8.14.) The fermion assignments we have chosen for this $\mathrm{SU}(5)$ theory necessitate an extension of our methods to include fermions in the $5^*$ and $10$ representations. The transformation properties of these representations and the construction of minimal interaction terms are, however, easily worked out. For each fermion generation, the interaction term in the $\mathrm{SU}(5)$ Lagrangian may be written

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}}={}&-\frac{g_5}{2}G_\mu^a
(\bar{u}\gamma^\mu\lambda^au+\bar{d}\gamma^\mu\lambda^ad)\\
&-\frac{g_5}{2}W_\mu^i
(\bar{\mathsf{L}}_u\gamma^\mu\tau^i\mathsf{L}_u+
\bar{\mathsf{L}}_e\gamma^\mu\tau^i\mathsf{L}_e)\\
&-\frac{g_5}{2}\frac{3}{5}\mathcal{A}_\mu
\sum_{\substack{\text{fermion}\\\text{species}}}
\bar{f}\gamma^\mu Yf\\
&-\frac{g_5}{\sqrt{2}}\left[
X_{\mu;\alpha}^-\left(
\bar{d}_{\mathrm{R}}^\alpha\gamma^\mu e_{\mathrm{R}}^c+
\bar{d}_{\mathrm{L}}^\alpha\gamma^\mu e_{\mathrm{L}}^c+
\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}
\bar{u}_{\mathrm{L}}^{c\gamma}\gamma^\mu u_{\mathrm{L}}^\beta
\right)+\text{h.c.}\right]\\
&+\frac{g_5}{\sqrt{2}}\left[
Y_{\mu;\alpha}^-\left(
\bar{d}_{\mathrm{R}}^\alpha\gamma^\mu\nu_{\mathrm{R}}^c+
\bar{u}_{\mathrm{L}}^\alpha\gamma^\mu e_{\mathrm{L}}^c+
\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}
\bar{u}_{\mathrm{L}}^{c\beta}\gamma^\mu d_{\mathrm{L}}^\gamma
\right)+\text{h.c.}\right],
\end{aligned} \tag{9.2.19}
$$

where the notation $\lambda^a$ has been used for $\mathrm{SU}(3)$ matrices and gluons are noted by $G_\mu^a$. The color index $a$ runs from 1 to 8, and the color indices $\alpha,\beta$, and $\gamma$ run over 1, 2, 3 or red, green, blue. The $\mathrm{SU}(2)_{\mathrm{L}}$ doublets are

$$
\mathsf{L}_u=\begin{pmatrix}u\\d\end{pmatrix}_{\mathrm{L}},
\quad
\mathsf{L}_e=\begin{pmatrix}\nu_e\\e\end{pmatrix}_{\mathrm{L}}.
\tag{9.2.20}
$$

(Substitute $d\to d_\theta$ in (9.2.19) and (9.2.20) to incorporate quark mixing.)

The first three terms of (9.2.19) are the standard interactions of the unbroken $\mathrm{SU}(3)_{\mathrm{c}}\otimes\mathrm{SU}(2)_{\mathrm{L}}\otimes\mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ model, provided that we identify the coupling constants $g_{\mathrm{strong}}\equiv g_3$ and $g_{\mathrm{SU}(2)_{\mathrm{L}}}\equiv g_2$ with $g_5$. In the weak-hypercharge term we must identify

$$
g'=g_5\sqrt{\frac{3}{5}}, \tag{9.2.21}
$$

<!-- PDF page 411; printed page 397 -->

where $g'$ corresponds to the canonical definition in the electroweak theory, as expressed in (6.3.13). The numerical factor arose, as earlier remarked, from the requirement that the field $\mathcal{A}_\mu$ couple to the current of a normalized generator of $\mathrm{SU}(5)$. This result may be retrieved in more pedestrian fashion, as follows. Because the electric charge $\electric{Q}$ is a generator of the group, it may be written in the form

$$
\electric{Q}=T_3+\kappa T_0, \tag{9.2.22}
$$

where $T_3$ is a generator of $\mathrm{SU}(2)_{\mathrm{L}}$ and $T_0$ is a weak-isospin-singlet generator of $\mathrm{SU}(5)$. For $\mathrm{SU}(N)$, we then have that

$$
\sum_{\text{representation}}\electric{Q}^2
=(1+\kappa^2)\sum_{\text{representation}}T_3^2.
\tag{9.2.23}
$$

An explicit calculation for the $5^*$ then yields

$$
\frac{4}{3}=(1+\kappa^2)\frac{1}{2}, \tag{9.2.24}
$$

so that

$$
\kappa^2=\frac{5}{3}. \tag{9.2.25}
$$

This shows that the normalization of $T_0$ differs by a factor of $\sqrt{3/5}$ from the conventional $\mathrm{U}(1)$ hypercharge operator $Y$.

The important consequence of this information is that the weak-hypercharge coupling is no longer an independent parameter but is given by

$$
g'^2=\frac{3}{5}g_2^2. \tag{9.2.26}
$$

Recalling the definition (6.3.50) of the weak mixing angle, we find that

$$
x_W=\sin^2\theta_W=\frac{g'^2}{g_2^2+g'^2}=\frac{3}{8}
\tag{9.2.27}
$$

in the unbroken $\mathrm{SU}(5)$ theory.

The fourth and fifth terms in the interaction Lagrangian correspond to new transitions that occur in the unified theory. The new vertices, summarized in figure 9.1, mediate processes such as proton decay, which proceeds by the three elementary transitions shown in figure 9.2. Each of these changes both baryon number and lepton number by $-1$ unit and changes the number of elementary fermions by $-4$.

The intermediate bosons $W^+$, $W^-$, and $Z^0$ are expected to acquire masses according to something resembling the usual mechanism of spontaneous symmetry breaking. The necessary symmetry breaking can be achieved in two steps. First, a $24$ of real scalar fields is introduced to break

$$
\mathrm{SU}(5)\xrightarrow{24}
\mathrm{SU}(3)_{\mathrm{c}}\otimes\mathrm{SU}(2)_{\mathrm{L}}\otimes\mathrm{U}(1)_Y.
\tag{9.2.30}
$$

At this point, the $X$ and $Y$ leptoquark bosons acquire mass. Next, a $5$-dimensional representation of complex scalar fields is employed to accomplish the breaking

$$
\mathrm{SU}(3)_{\mathrm{c}}\otimes\mathrm{SU}(2)_{\mathrm{L}}\otimes\mathrm{U}(1)_Y
\xrightarrow{5}\mathrm{SU}(3)_{\mathrm{c}}\otimes\mathrm{U}(1)_{\mathrm{EM}}.
\tag{9.2.31}
$$

This is the straightforward extension of the spontaneous symmetry breaking in the electroweak theory described in section 6.3, in which a complex scalar $\mathrm{SU}(2)_{\mathrm{L}}$ doublet breaks $\mathrm{SU}(2)_{\mathrm{L}}\otimes\mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ down to $\mathrm{U}(1)_{\mathrm{EM}}$. The spontaneous symmetry breaking gives rise to many physical Higgs bosons. It is instructive to consider the logic of the breakdown in some detail.

To accomplish the symmetry breaking at the leptoquark scale, we assume that the adjoint $24$ representation of real scalar bosons acquires a vacuum expectation value

$$
\langle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{24}\rangle_0=
\left(\begin{array}{ccc|cc}
1&&&&\\
&1&&\multicolumn{2}{c}{\large 0}\\
&&1&&\\
\hline
\multicolumn{3}{c|}{\large 0}&-\frac{3}{2}&\\
&&&&-\frac{3}{2}
\end{array}\right)\cdot v_{24}, \tag{9.2.32}
$$

where $v_{24}$ is the parameter that will set the scale of masses of the $X$ and $Y$ bosons. It is obvious that this preserves separately the $\mathrm{SU}(3)$ symmetry of the first three

<!-- PDF page 414; printed page 400 -->

indices and the $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ symmetry of the last two indices. However, the symmetry under the $\mathrm{SU}(5)$ generators that couple the color indices to the $\mathrm{SU}(2)_{\mathrm{L}}$ indices will be broken, and the corresponding gauge bosons will acquire mass. Referring to the $\mathrm{SU}(3)_{\mathrm{c}}\otimes\mathrm{SU}(2)_{\mathrm{L}}\otimes\mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ decomposition of the $24$ given in table 9.1, we see that the $X$ and $Y$ bosons corresponding to the $(3,2)_{-5/3}$ and $(3^*,2)_{5/3}$ representations of the subgroup acquire masses, whereas the gluons $(8,1)_0$, incipient $W$ bosons $(1,3)_0$, and hypercharge boson $(1,1)_0$ remain massless. These are accompanied by 12 physical scalar particles, or Higgs bosons, corresponding to the $(8,1)_0$, $(1,3)_0$, and $(1,1)_0$ representations. The masses of these Higgs bosons will be comparable to those acquired by the leptoquark bosons. The $24$ will not endow the fermions with similar masses because it does not occur in the $\bar{\mathsf{L}}\mathsf{R}$ products [cf. (6.3.20) and (7.1.32)],

$$
\begin{aligned}
5^*\otimes10&=5\oplus45,\\
10\otimes10&=5^*\oplus45^*\oplus50^*,
\end{aligned} \tag{9.2.33}
$$

that generate renormalizable mass terms for the quarks and charged leptons.

The complex $5$ contains the complex $(1,2)_1$ representation used for spontaneous symmetry breaking in the standard model of the weak and electromagnetic interactions. It is, therefore, appropriate to choose the vacuum expectation value

$$
\langle\phi_5\rangle_0=
\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}v_5/\sqrt{2}, \tag{9.2.34}
$$

where $v_5=(G_{\mathrm{F}}\sqrt{2})^{-1/2}\approx246\,\unit{\mathrm{GeV}}$ [cf. (6.3.45)]. Of the 10 real fields of the complex $5$, only 3 are absorbed into the longitudinal components of the now-massive $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$. In addition to the normal Higgs scalar $H^0$, still with unknown mass, there are 6 additional physical scalar particles, the color triplets $h^{\pm1/3}$, corresponding to the

$$
(3,1)_{-2/3}\quad\text{and}\quad(3^*,1)_{2/3} \tag{9.2.35}
$$

representations. These are potentially troublesome objects because they can mediate nucleon decay through processes such as

$$
u+d\to h^{1/3}\to
\begin{cases}
e^++\bar{u},\\
\bar{\nu}_e+\bar{d}.
\end{cases} \tag{9.2.36}
$$

The Yukawa couplings are, as usual, closely tied to the fermion masses. Unless these vanish, the only way to suppress the rate for $h^{\pm1/3}$-mediated proton decay is to arrange that the color-triplet scalar particles receive masses comparable to those of the $X$ and $Y$ bosons. This may be achieved [15] by introducing locally gauge-invariant interactions among $\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{24}$ and $\phi_5$, which would, in any event, arise from

<!-- PDF page 415; printed page 401 -->

quantum corrections to the lowest-order interaction terms. However, maintaining a ratio of 13 orders of magnitude between the electroweak scale characterized by $v_5$ and the leptoquark scale characterized by $v_{24}$ requires an exceedingly delicate tuning of parameters in the Higgs potentials. This balancing act is neither natural nor likely to survive radiative corrections. Its precarious nature may well be a symptom of the incompleteness of this minimal example of a unified theory.

We noted earlier, in our discussion of the symmetry-breaking effects of the $24$, that scalars belonging to the fundamental $5$ representation could generate fermion masses in the manner familiar from the electroweak theory. The necessary Yukawa term in the Lagrangian is

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Yukawa}}=
\left[\zeta_d\bar{\psi}_{\mathrm{R}j}^c\psi_{\mathrm{L}}^{jk}\phi_k^\dagger
+\zeta_u\varepsilon_{jklmn}\bar{\psi}_{\mathrm{L}}^{cjk}
\psi_{\mathrm{L}}^{lm}\phi^n\right]+\text{h.c.} \tag{9.2.37}
$$

for a single generation. If we write the vacuum expectation value of the scalar field as

$$
\langle\phi_k\rangle_0=\frac{\delta_k^5v_5}{\sqrt{2}}, \tag{9.2.38}
$$

we see that the first of the Yukawa terms becomes, simply,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_d&=\frac{\zeta_dv_5}{\sqrt{2}}
(\bar{\psi}_{\mathrm{R}j}^c\psi_{\mathrm{L}}^{j5}+\text{h.c.})\\
&=-\frac{\zeta_dv_5}{\sqrt{2}}
(\bar{d}_{\mathrm{R}}^\alpha d_{\mathrm{L}}^\alpha+
\bar{e}_{\mathrm{R}}^ce_{\mathrm{L}}^c+\text{h.c.})\\
&=-\frac{\zeta_dv_5}{\sqrt{2}}(\bar{d}d+\bar{e}e),
\end{aligned} \tag{9.2.39}
$$

where $\alpha$ is a color index for the quarks. The masses of the electron and the down quark are, therefore, given by

$$
\mechanical{m_d}=\mechanical{m_e}=\frac{\zeta_dv_5}{\sqrt{2}}. \tag{9.2.40}
$$

That is, the $\mathrm{SU}(5)$ symmetry requires the down quark and the electron mass matrices to be the same. In similar fashion, for several generations we obtain, in addition the predictions,

$$
\mechanical{m_s}=\mechanical{m_\mu}, \tag{9.2.41}
$$

$$
\mechanical{m_b}=\mechanical{m_\tau}. \tag{9.2.42}
$$

The mass of the up quark, generated by the second term in (9.2.37), continues to be a free parameter of the theory. Although the relations (9.2.40)–(9.2.42) support in a qualitative fashion our identification of the quarks and leptons that make up each fermion generation, they are not quantitatively successful. However, as we have seen earlier for coupling constants, masses in field theory are to be interpreted as parameters that depend on the momenta at which they are measured. The equalities

<!-- PDF page 416; printed page 402 -->

(9.2.40)–(9.2.42) should then be interpreted as predictions that apply for $\mechanical{Q}^2\gtrsim\mechanical{M_X}^2$. At lower values of $\mechanical{Q}^2$, the equalities are then broken by the differing rates of evolution of the fermion mass operators, with results that are at least in schematic agreement with experiment (Ref. [15]). We shall return to this question in section 9.6 but turn first to the subject of coupling constants and their evolution.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\psi,\psi_{\mathrm{L}},\psi_{\mathrm{R}},\psi^c,\psi_{j\mathrm{L}},\psi_{\mathrm{L}}^{jk}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{GeV}}^{3/2}$ | Fermion fields | The Dirac, chiral, conjugate, and SU(5) multiplet fields. |
| $u,d,d_\theta,e,\nu_e,N,f$ | $\unit{\mathrm{GeV}}^{3/2}$ | Particle fields | The quark and lepton fields, including the mixed down-quark field and optional sterile neutrino. |
| $\mathsf{L}_u,\mathsf{L}_e$ | $\unit{\mathrm{GeV}}^{3/2}$ | Weak doublet fields | The left-handed quark and lepton field doublets. |
| $\electric{Q}$ | $1$ | Electric charge number | The particle charge in units of the elementary charge. |
| $Y$ | $1$ | Weak hypercharge | The hypercharge of each subgroup representation. |
| $\mathsf{H},H^a,G_\mu^a,W_\mu^i,\mathcal{A}_\mu,X,Y$ | $\unit{\mathrm{GeV}}$ | Gauge fields | The SU(5) field matrix and its gluon, weak, hypercharge, and leptoquark components. |
| $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}},\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Yukawa}},\mathcal{L}_d$ | $\unit{\mathrm{GeV}}^4$ | Lagrangian densities | The interaction and fermion-mass terms in natural units. |
| $g_5,g_3,g_{\mathrm{strong}},g_2,g_{\mathrm{SU}(2)_{\mathrm{L}}},g'$ | $1$ | Gauge couplings | The unified, strong, weak-isospin, and hypercharge interaction strengths. |
| $\theta_W$ | $\unit{\mathrm{rad}}$ | Weak mixing angle | The angle that relates the neutral electroweak fields. |
| $x_W$ | $1$ | Weak mixing parameter | The squared sine of the weak mixing angle. |
| $\signal{\tau}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{years}}$ | Proton lifetime | The lifetime or lower lifetime bound for the specified decay modes. |
| $\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{24},\phi_5,\phi_k,\phi^n,H^0,h^{\pm1/3}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{GeV}}$ | Scalar fields | The adjoint and fundamental Higgs fields and physical scalar components. |
| $v_{24},v_5$ | $\unit{\mathrm{GeV}}$ | Vacuum field scales | The vacuum values that set the leptoquark and electroweak mass scales. |
| $G_{\mathrm{F}}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{GeV}}^{-2}$ | Fermi constant | The weak-interaction constant that determines the electroweak vacuum scale. |
| $\zeta_d,\zeta_u$ | $1$ | Yukawa couplings | The scalar couplings that generate down-type and up-type fermion masses. |
| $\mechanical{m_d},\mechanical{m_e},\mechanical{m_s},\mechanical{m_\mu},\mechanical{m_b},\mechanical{m_\tau},\mechanical{M_X}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{GeV}}$ | Particle masses | The fermion masses and the leptoquark mass in the unification relations. |
| $\mechanical{Q}$ | $\unit{\mathrm{GeV}}$ | Momentum scale | The scale at which the fermion masses are compared. |
| — | $\unit{\mathrm{GeV}}$ | Leptoquark mass scale | The source gives a scale of approximately $10^{15}\,\unit{\mathrm{GeV}}$. |
