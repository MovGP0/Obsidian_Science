> [!TLDR]
> Fermion masses evolve with the gauge couplings, and the SU(5) model relates quark and lepton masses at the unification scale.

<!-- PDF page 428; printed page 414 -->

Unraveling the origins of electroweak symmetry breaking will not necessarily give insight into the origin and pattern of fermion masses, because they are set by the Yukawa couplings $\zeta_i$, of unknown provenance, that we first met in (6.3.20). The puzzling pattern of quark masses is depicted in figure 9.6. The fact that masses—like coupling constants—are scale dependent might encourage us to hope that what looks like an irrational pattern at low scales might reveal an underlying order at some other scale.

To illustrate the possibilities, let us adopt the specific framework of $\mathrm{SU}(5)$ unification, with the two-step spontaneously symmetry breaking introduced in section 9.2. At a high scale, a $24$ of scalars breaks $\mathrm{SU}(5)\to\mathrm{SU}(3)_{\mathrm{c}}\otimes\mathrm{SU}(2)_{\mathrm{L}}\otimes\mathrm{U}(1)_Y$, giving extremely large masses to the leptoquark gauge bosons $X^{\pm4/3}$ and $Y^{\pm1/3}$. As we have already observed, the $24$ does not occur in the $\bar{\mathsf{L}}\mathsf{R}$ products that generate fermion masses, so quarks and leptons escape large tree-level masses. At the electroweak scale, a $5$ of scalars (containing the standard-model Higgs fields) breaks $\mathrm{SU}(3)_{\mathrm{c}}\otimes\mathrm{SU}(2)_{\mathrm{L}}\otimes\mathrm{U}(1)_Y\to\mathrm{SU}(3)_{\mathrm{c}}\otimes\mathrm{U}(1)_{\mathrm{EM}}$ and endows fermions with mass. This approach relates quark and lepton masses at the unification scale,

$$
\left.\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{m_e}&=\mechanical{m_d},\\
\mechanical{m_\mu}&=\mechanical{m_s},\\
\mechanical{m_\tau}&=\mechanical{m_b},
\end{aligned}\right\}\quad\text{at }\mechanical{u};
\quad\text{plus separate parameters}\quad
\left\{\begin{aligned}
&\mechanical{m_u},\\
&\mechanical{m_c},\\
&\mechanical{m_t},
\end{aligned}\right. \tag{9.6.1}
$$

with implications for the observed masses that we will now elaborate.

The fermion masses evolve from the unification scale $\mechanical{u}$ to the experimental scale $\mechanical{\mu}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ln[\mechanical{m_{u,c,t}}(\mechanical{\mu}^2)]
&\approx\ln[\mechanical{m_{u,c,t}}(\mechanical{u}^2)]
+\frac{12}{33-2n_f}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_3(\mechanical{\mu}^2)}{\alpha_u}\right)\\
&\quad+\frac{27}{88-8n_f}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_2(\mechanical{\mu}^2)}{\alpha_u}\right)
-\frac{3}{10n_f}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_1(\mechanical{\mu}^2)}{\alpha_u}\right),
\end{aligned} \tag{9.6.2}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ln[\mechanical{m_{d,s,b}}(\mechanical{\mu}^2)]
&\approx\ln[\mechanical{m_{d,s,b}}(\mechanical{u}^2)]
+\frac{12}{33-2n_f}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_3(\mechanical{\mu}^2)}{\alpha_u}\right)\\
&\quad+\frac{27}{88-8n_f}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_2(\mechanical{\mu}^2)}{\alpha_u}\right)
+\frac{3}{20n_f}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_1(\mechanical{\mu}^2)}{\alpha_u}\right),
\end{aligned} \tag{9.6.3}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ln[\mechanical{m_{e,\mu,\tau}}(\mechanical{\mu}^2)]
&\approx\ln[\mechanical{m_{e,\mu,\tau}}(\mechanical{u}^2)]\\
&\quad+\frac{27}{88-8n_f}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_2(\mechanical{\mu}^2)}{\alpha_u}\right)
-\frac{27}{20n_f}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_1(\mechanical{\mu}^2)}{\alpha_u}\right),
\end{aligned} \tag{9.6.4}
$$

where we have omitted a small Higgs-boson contribution to keep the formulas short. The classic success of $\mathrm{SU}(5)$ unification is the predicted relation between $\mechanical{m_b}$ and $\mechanical{m_\tau}$

<!-- PDF page 430; printed page 416 -->

(Ref. [15]). Combining (9.6.3) and (9.6.4), we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ln\left[\frac{\mechanical{m_b}(\mechanical{\mu}^2)}
{\mechanical{m_\tau}(\mechanical{\mu}^2)}\right]
&\approx\ln\left[\frac{\mechanical{m_b}(\mechanical{u}^2)}
{\mechanical{m_\tau}(\mechanical{u}^2)}\right]\\
&\quad+\frac{12}{33-2n_f}
\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_3(\mechanical{\mu}^2)}{\alpha_u}\right)
-\frac{3}{2n_f}
\ln\left(\frac{\alpha_1(\mechanical{\mu}^2)}{\alpha_u}\right),
\end{aligned} \tag{9.6.5}
$$

where the first term on the right-hand side vanishes. Choosing, for illustration, $n_f=6$, $1/\alpha_u=40$, $1/\alpha_3(\mechanical{\mu}^2)=5$, and $1/\alpha_1(\mechanical{\mu}^2)=65$, we compute at a low scale

$$
\mechanical{m_b}=2.91\mechanical{m_\tau}\approx5.16\,\unit{\mathrm{GeV}}, \tag{9.6.6}
$$

in suggestive agreement with experiment. The factor-of-three ratio arises because the quark masses, influenced by QCD, evolve more rapidly than the lepton masses.

The example of $b$-$\tau$ unification raises the hope that all fermion masses arise on high scales and show simple patterns there. The other cases are not so encouraging (cf. problem 9.18).

The prospect of finding order among the fermion masses has spawned a lively theoretical industry [35]. The essential strategy comprises four steps: (1) Begin with supersymmetric $\mathrm{SU}(5)$, which has advantages for $\sin^2\theta_W$, coupling-constant unification, and the proton lifetime, or with supersymmetric $\mathrm{SO}(10)$, which accommodates a massive neutrino gracefully. (2) Find “textures”—simple patterns of Yukawa matrices—that lead to successful predictions for masses and mixing angles. (3) Interpret the textures in terms of symmetry breaking patterns or interfamily (“horizontal”) symmetries. (4) Seek a derivation—or at least a motivation—for the winning entry.
