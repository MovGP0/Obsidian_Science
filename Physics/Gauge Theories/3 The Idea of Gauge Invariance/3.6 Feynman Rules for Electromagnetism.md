> [!TLDR]
> Interaction terms give electromagnetic vertex factors, and free-field equations give momentum-space propagators.

The passage from Lagrangian to Feynman rules is documented in many places. Here, we shall merely outline the final procedure without derivation, assuming the reader to be as familiar with the methods as he or she cares to be.

The recipe for extracting vertex factors from the interaction Lagrangian $\energy{\mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}}}$ is uncomplicated. In the quantity $i\energy{\mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}}}$, replace all the field operators by free-particle wave functions and evaluate the resulting expressions in momentum space. Omit all factors that correspond to the external lines. What remains is the vertex factor of the interaction.

Consider first the interaction in spinor electrodynamics. We have, from (3.5.16),

$$
i\energy{\mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}}}=-i\electric{q}\magnetic{A_\mu}\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\psi, \tag{3.6.1}
$$

into which we insert

$$
\begin{aligned}
\psi&=u_1e^{-i\mechanical{p_1}\cdot x},\\
\bar\psi&=\bar u_2e^{i\mechanical{p_2}\cdot x},\\
\magnetic{A_\mu}&=\epsilon_\mu^*(\mechanical{k})e^{i\mechanical{k}\cdot x},
\end{aligned} \tag{3.6.2}
$$

so that

$$
i\energy{\mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}}}=-i\electric{q}\epsilon_\mu^*(\mechanical{k})\bar u_2\gamma^\mu u_1e^{i(\mechanical{p_2}+\mechanical{k}-\mechanical{p_1})\cdot x}, \tag{3.6.3}
$$

from which, after discarding the external factors, we retain

$$
-i\electric{q}\gamma^\mu. \tag{3.6.4}
$$

For scalar electrodynamics, the interaction Lagrangian of (3.5.23) contains two terms. The trilinear term in $\energy{\mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}}}$ yields

$$
i\energy{\mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}}}[3]=\electric{q}\magnetic{A_\mu}[\phi^*\partial^\mu\phi-(\partial^\mu\phi^*)\phi]. \tag{3.6.5}
$$

Upon substitution of

$$
\begin{aligned}
\phi&=S_1e^{-i\mechanical{p_1}\cdot x},\\
\phi^*&=S_2e^{i\mechanical{p_2}\cdot x}
\end{aligned} \tag{3.6.6}
$$

and the usual form for the photon, we find

$$
i\energy{\mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}}}[3]\rightarrow\epsilon_\mu^*(\mechanical{k})S_1S_2e^{i(\mechanical{p_2}+\mechanical{k}-\mechanical{p_1})\cdot x}\electric{q}(-i\mechanical{p_1}-i\mechanical{p_2})^\mu, \tag{3.6.7}
$$

which yields a vertex factor

$$
-i\electric{q}(\mechanical{p_1}+\mechanical{p_2})^\mu. \tag{3.6.8}
$$

Finally, for the interaction term

$$
i\energy{\mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}}}[4]=i\electric{q}^2\magnetic{A_\mu}\magnetic{A_\nu}g^{\mu\nu}\phi^*\phi, \tag{3.6.9}
$$

we write

$$
i\energy{\mathcal L_{\mathrm{int}}}[4]\rightarrow i\electric{q}^2g^{\mu\nu}
(\epsilon_\mu^*(\mechanical{k_1})\epsilon_\nu^*(\mechanical{k_2})+\epsilon_\mu^*(\mechanical{k_2})\epsilon_\nu^*(\mechanical{k_1}))
e^{i(\mechanical{p_2}+\mechanical{k_1}+\mechanical{k_2}-\mechanical{p_1})\cdot x}, \tag{3.6.10}
$$

from which the vertex factor is

$$
2i\electric{q}^2g^{\mu\nu}. \tag{3.6.11}
$$

The momentum-space propagators may be obtained with the aid of a similar mnemonic device. The general form of the wave functions is typified by that for the Klein–Gordon field,

$$
(\Box+\mechanical{m}^2)\phi(x)=J(x), \tag{3.6.12}
$$

where the source term $J$ may itself depend on the fields. The propagator is simply related to the inverse of the momentum-space representation of the operator on the left-hand side. Thus for the Klein–Gordon field we have

$$
G(\mechanical{p}^2)=\frac{i}{\mechanical{p}^2-\mechanical{m}^2+i\varepsilon}, \tag{3.6.13}
$$

whereas for the fermion field in the Dirac equation

$$
(\not{\mechanical{p}}-\mechanical{m})\psi(x)=J(x), \tag{3.6.14}
$$

the propagator is

$$
G(\mechanical{p})=\frac{i}{\not{\mechanical{p}}-\mechanical{m}+i\varepsilon}
=i\frac{(\not{\mechanical{p}}+\mechanical{m})}{\mechanical{p}^2-\mechanical{m}^2+i\varepsilon}. \tag{3.6.15}
$$

To obtain the photon propagator, a choice of gauge is required. This is because the equation of motion,

$$
\Box\magnetic{A^\nu}-\partial^\nu(\partial_\mu\magnetic{A^\mu})=\electric{J^\nu}, \tag{3.2.22}
$$

does not uniquely determine the potential $\magnetic{A^\nu}$ in terms of the conserved current $\electric{J^\nu}$. (A massless photon has but two degrees of freedom.) To resolve the gauge ambiguity in the most pedestrian fashion we may choose the Lorenz gauge $\partial_\mu\magnetic{A^\mu}=0$, in which case (3.2.22) collapses to a set of massless Klein–Gordon equations, which lead to the propagator

$$
G(\mechanical{k}^2)=\frac{-ig^{\mu\nu}}{\mechanical{k}^2+i\varepsilon}. \tag{3.6.16}
$$

Other gauge-fixing procedures are discussed briefly in section 6.3 and section 8.3. The gauge-independence of the theory guarantees that observable quantities will be independent of the choice of gauge. Graphical summaries of the Feynman rules for scalar and spinor electrodynamics are given in appendix B.5.

The analysis presented in this chapter has shown the theory of electromagnetism to be the gauge theory associated with the phase transformations that form the Abelian group U(1). We shall next investigate the generalization of these ideas to non-Abelian gauge theories, or Yang–Mills theories.
