> [!TLDR]
> Gauge transformations change the potentials while the electric and magnetic fields remain the same.

<!-- PDF page 54; printed page 40 -->

We have already remarked on two of the motivations for examining gauge invariance in detail: the hope of finding an explanation, or at least a deeper understanding, of the conservation of electric charge and the desire (represented in the first instance by Weyl’s attempts) to derive electrodynamics from some basic principle. As a necessary preliminary to these studies, let us review the manifestations of gauge arbitrariness in classical electrodynamics.

Maxwell’s equation for magnetic charge,

$$
\nabla\cdot\magnetic{\mathbf B}=0, \tag{3.2.1}
$$

invites us to write the magnetic field as

$$
\magnetic{\mathbf B}=\nabla\times\magnetic{\mathbf A}, \tag{3.2.2}
$$

where $\magnetic{\mathbf A}$ is called the vector potential. This identification ensures that $\magnetic{\mathbf B}$ will be divergenceless by virtue of the identity

$$
\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\magnetic{\mathbf A})=0. \tag{3.2.3}
$$

If we add an arbitrary gradient to the vector potential

$$
\magnetic{\mathbf A}\rightarrow\magnetic{\mathbf A}+\nabla\Lambda, \tag{3.2.4}
$$

the magnetic field is unchanged because

$$
\magnetic{\mathbf B}=\nabla\times(\magnetic{\mathbf A}+\nabla\Lambda)=\nabla\times\magnetic{\mathbf A}. \tag{3.2.5}
$$

In similar fashion, the curl equation (Faraday–Lenz) for the electric field,

$$
\nabla\times\electric{\mathbf E}=\frac{-\partial\magnetic{\mathbf B}}{\partial t}, \tag{3.2.6}
$$

which can be rewritten as

$$
\nabla\times\left(\electric{\mathbf E}+\frac{\partial\magnetic{\mathbf A}}{\partial t}\right)=0, \tag{3.2.7}
$$

<!-- PDF page 55; printed page 41 -->

suggests the identification

$$
\electric{\mathbf E}+\frac{\partial\magnetic{\mathbf A}}{\partial t}=-\nabla\electric{V}, \tag{3.2.8}
$$

where $\electric{V}$ is known as the scalar potential. In order that the electric field remain invariant under the shift (3.2.4), we must also require

$$
\electric{V}\rightarrow\electric{V}-\frac{\partial\Lambda}{\partial t}. \tag{3.2.9}
$$

All this can be expressed compactly in covariant notation. The electromagnetic field-strength tensor

$$
F^{\mu\nu}=-F^{\nu\mu}=\partial^\nu\magnetic{A^\mu}-\partial^\mu\magnetic{A^\nu}
=\begin{pmatrix}
0&\electric{E_1}&\electric{E_2}&\electric{E_3}\\
-\electric{E_1}&0&\magnetic{B_3}&-\magnetic{B_2}\\
-\electric{E_2}&-\magnetic{B_3}&0&\magnetic{B_1}\\
-\electric{E_3}&\magnetic{B_2}&-\magnetic{B_1}&0
\end{pmatrix} \tag{3.2.10}
$$

built up from the four-vector potential

$$
\magnetic{A^\mu}=(\electric{V};\magnetic{\mathbf A}), \tag{3.2.11}
$$

is unchanged by the “gauge transformation”

$$
\magnetic{A^\mu}\rightarrow\magnetic{A^\mu}-\partial^\mu\Lambda, \tag{3.2.12}
$$

where $\Lambda(x)$ is an arbitrary function of the coordinate. The fact that many different four-vector potentials yield the same electromagnetic fields and thus describe the same physics is a manifestation of the gauge invariance of classical electrodynamics.

The Maxwell equations (3.2.1) and (3.2.6), which motivated the introduction of a potential, may be rewritten in covariant form as

$$
\partial^\lambda F^{\mu\nu}+\partial^\mu F^{\nu\lambda}+\partial^\nu F^{\lambda\mu}=0. \tag{3.2.13}
$$

A more-compact expression follows upon introduction of the dual field-strength tensor:

$$
{}^*F^{\mu\nu}=-\frac12\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}F_{\rho\sigma}
=\begin{pmatrix}
0&\magnetic{B_1}&\magnetic{B_2}&\magnetic{B_3}\\
-\magnetic{B_1}&0&-\electric{E_3}&\electric{E_2}\\
-\magnetic{B_2}&\electric{E_3}&0&-\electric{E_1}\\
-\magnetic{B_3}&-\electric{E_2}&\electric{E_1}&0
\end{pmatrix}, \tag{3.2.14}
$$

which may be obtained formally from $F^{\mu\nu}$ by replacing $\electric{\mathbf E}\rightarrow\magnetic{\mathbf B}$ and $\magnetic{\mathbf B}\rightarrow-\electric{\mathbf E}$. Here we adopt the convention [cf. (A.2.19)] that Levi-Cività’s antisymmetric symbol $\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$ is equal to $\mp1$ for even or odd permutations of (0123) and that $\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}=-\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$. Evidently, the inverse relation is

$$
F_{\mu\nu}=\frac12\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}{}^*F^{\rho\sigma}. \tag{3.2.15}
$$

Taking the dual of (3.2.13), we find that the Maxwell equations (3.2.1) and (3.2.6) may be written as

$$
\partial_\mu{}^*F^{\mu\nu}=0. \tag{3.2.16}
$$

The remaining Maxwell equations,

$$
\nabla\cdot\electric{\mathbf E}=\electric{\rho}=-\nabla\cdot\magnetic{\dot{\mathbf A}}-\nabla^2\electric{V}, \tag{3.2.17}
$$

(where $\magnetic{\dot{\mathbf A}}=\partial\magnetic{\mathbf A}/\partial t$) and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla\times\magnetic{\mathbf B}&=\electric{\mathbf J}+\electric{\dot{\mathbf E}}
=\electric{\mathbf J}-\magnetic{\ddot{\mathbf A}}-\nabla\electric{\dot V}\\
&=\nabla\times(\nabla\times\magnetic{\mathbf A})=-\nabla^2\magnetic{\mathbf A}+\nabla(\nabla\cdot\magnetic{\mathbf A}),
\end{aligned} \tag{3.2.18}
$$

[using (3.2.8) for $\electric{\mathbf E}$ and (3.2.2) for $\magnetic{\mathbf B}$] correspond, in covariant notation, to

$$
\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=-\electric{J^\nu}, \tag{3.2.19}
$$

with the electromagnetic current given by

$$
\electric{J^\nu}=(\electric{\rho};\electric{\mathbf J}). \tag{3.2.20}
$$

Two consequences are immediately apparent. First, the electromagnetic current is conserved:

$$
\partial_\nu\electric{J^\nu}=-\partial_\nu\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=0. \tag{3.2.21}
$$

Second, the wave equation (3.2.19) may be expanded as

$$
\Box\magnetic{A^\nu}-\partial^\nu(\partial_\mu\magnetic{A^\mu})=\electric{J^\nu}, \tag{3.2.22}
$$

which becomes, in the absence of sources and in Lorenz [6] gauge $(\partial_\mu\magnetic{A^\mu}=0)$,

$$
\Box\magnetic{A^\nu}=0. \tag{3.2.23}
$$

Each component of the vector potential, to be identified with the photon field, thus satisfies a Klein–Gordon equation for a massless particle.

In these familiar results we see a relationship between gauge invariance, current conservation, and massless vector fields. Let us now attempt to understand these connections more completely.
