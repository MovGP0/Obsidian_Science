> [!TLDR]
> Triviality and vacuum stability bound the Higgs-boson mass and the energy range over which electroweak theory can remain complete.

<!-- PDF page 255; printed page 241 -->

By demanding that the electroweak theory be complete and consistent as a quantum field theory, we can establish bounds on the Higgs boson mass and uncover evidence that discoveries will not end with the Higgs boson. Scalar field theories make sense on all energy scales only if they are noninteracting, or “trivial” [98]. The vacuum of quantum field theory is a generalized dielectric medium that screens (or antiscreens) charge. Accordingly, the effective charge is a function of the distance or, equivalently, of the energy scale. This is the famous phenomenon of the running coupling constant, about which we will have more to say in sections 8.2 and 8.3.

In $\lambda\phi^4$ theory (compare the interaction term in the Higgs potential), it is easy to calculate the variation of the coupling constant $\lambda$ in perturbation theory by summing quantum corrections given by bubble graphs. The coupling constant $\lambda(\energy{\kappa})$ on a physical scale $\energy{\kappa}$ is related to the coupling constant on a higher scale $\energy{\Lambda}$ by

$$
\frac{1}{\lambda(\energy{\kappa})}
=\frac{1}{\lambda(\energy{\Lambda})}
+\frac{3}{2\pi^2}\ln\left(\frac{\energy{\Lambda}}{\energy{\kappa}}\right). \tag{7.10.1}
$$

In order for the Higgs potential to be stable (i.e., for the energy of the vacuum state not to race off to $-\infty$), $\lambda(\energy{\Lambda})$ must not be negative. Applied to (7.10.1), this condition leads to an inequality,

$$
\frac{1}{\lambda(\energy{\kappa})}
\ge\frac{3}{2\pi^2}\ln\left(\frac{\energy{\Lambda}}{\energy{\kappa}}\right), \tag{7.10.2}
$$

<!-- PDF page 256; printed page 242 -->

that implies an *upper bound*,

$$
\lambda(\energy{\kappa})\le\frac{2\pi^2}{3\ln(\energy{\Lambda}/\energy{\kappa})}, \tag{7.10.3}
$$

on the coupling strength at the physical scale $\energy{\kappa}$. If the theory is to make sense to arbitrarily high energies—or short distances—we must consider the limit $\energy{\Lambda}\to\infty$ while holding $\energy{\kappa}$ fixed at some reasonable physical scale; the bound (7.10.3) then forces $\lambda(\energy{\kappa})$ to zero. The scalar field theory has become free field theory; in theorist’s jargon, it is trivial.

Rearranging and exponentiating both sides of (7.10.3) gives the condition

$$
\energy{\Lambda}\le\energy{\kappa}\exp\left(\frac{2\pi^2}{3\lambda(\energy{\kappa})}\right), \tag{7.10.4}
$$

from which we can infer a limit on the Higgs-boson mass. Choosing the physical scale as $\energy{\kappa}=\mechanical{M_H}$, using the definition $\mechanical{M_H}^2=2\lambda(\mechanical{M_H})v^2$, we find that

$$
\energy{\Lambda}\le\energy{\Lambda^\star}
\equiv\mechanical{M_H}\exp\left(\frac{4\pi^2v^2}{3\mechanical{M_H}^2}\right). \tag{7.10.5}
$$

For any given Higgs-boson mass, we can identify a maximum energy scale $\energy{\Lambda^\star}$ at which the theory ceases to make sense, so that new physics must enter. From this perspective, the electroweak theory is at best an effective theory, valid over a finite range of energies. This perturbative analysis leading to (7.10.5) must be supplemented by lattice-field-theory analyses as the Higgs-boson mass approaches $1\,\unit{TeV}$ and the interactions become strong [99]. A two-loop analysis leads to the bounds [100]

$$
\left.\mechanical{M_H}\right|_{\energy{\Lambda^\star}=\mechanical{M_{\mathrm{Pl}}}}
\lesssim180\,\unit{GeV};\quad
\left.\mechanical{M_H}\right|_{\energy{\Lambda^\star}=1\,\unit{TeV}}
\lesssim700\,\unit{GeV}. \tag{7.10.6}
$$

The correlation between $\mechanical{M_H}$ and $\energy{\Lambda^\star}$ is indicated by the upper curve in figure 7.26. If the elementary Higgs boson takes on the largest mass allowed by perturbative unitarity arguments, the electroweak theory lives on the brink of instability.

We remarked in section 5.6 that the analysis of spontaneous symmetry breaking presented there was entirely classical in that quantum corrections to the Higgs potential were neglected and that we should verify in specific applications that these do not alter essential conclusions. Such considerations lead to an interesting lower bound on the mass of the Higgs boson in the electroweak theory. When the one-loop corrections to the Higgs potential (6.3.19) are calculated, the effective potential can be written in the form

$$
\energy{V}(\phi^\dagger\phi)
=-\mechanical{\mu}^2\phi^\dagger\phi
+B(\phi^\dagger\phi)^2\ln(\phi^\dagger\phi/\mechanical{M}^2), \tag{7.10.7}
$$

where the parameter $\mechanical{M}$ has been chosen to absorb the erstwhile $(\phi^\dagger\phi)^2$ term. The coefficient $B$ includes contributions of gauge boson, Higgs boson, and fermion loops.

The effective potential has a local minimum at a value of $\langle\phi^\dagger\phi\rangle_0$ defined by

$$
\langle\phi^\dagger\phi\rangle_0
\left[\ln\left(\frac{\langle\phi^\dagger\phi\rangle_0}{\mechanical{M}^2}\right)+\frac12\right]
=\frac{\mechanical{\mu}^2}{2B}, \tag{7.10.8}
$$

at which the value of the potential is

$$
\energy{V}(\langle\phi^\dagger\phi\rangle_0)
=-B\langle\phi^\dagger\phi\rangle_0^2
\left[\ln\left(\frac{\langle\phi^\dagger\phi\rangle_0}{\mechanical{M}^2}\right)+1\right]. \tag{7.10.9}
$$

This local minimum will be an absolute minimum provided that $\energy{V}(\langle\phi^\dagger\phi\rangle_0)<\energy{V}(0)$, which is ensured if

$$
\ln\left(\frac{\langle\phi^\dagger\phi\rangle_0}{\mechanical{M}^2}\right)>-1. \tag{7.10.10}
$$

Inserting this constraint in the expression for the mass of the physical Higgs boson,

$$
\mechanical{M_H}^2
\equiv\left.\energy{V}''\right|_{\langle\phi^\dagger\phi\rangle_0}
=4B\langle\phi^\dagger\phi\rangle_0
\left[\ln\left(\frac{\langle\phi^\dagger\phi\rangle_0}{\mechanical{M}^2}\right)+\frac32\right], \tag{7.10.11}
$$

yields a lower bound on the Higgs-boson mass [101],

$$
\mechanical{M_H}^2>2B\langle\phi^\dagger\phi\rangle_0. \tag{7.10.12}
$$

Extending this analysis to the case in which the electroweak theory is complete and consistent up to scale $\energy{\Lambda^\star}$ leads to the lower bound on the Higgs-boson mass shown as the lower curve in figure 7.26.

Evidently the theory can be self-consistent up to very high energies, provided that the Higgs boson is relatively light. For the electroweak theory to make sense all the way up to a unification scale $\energy{\Lambda^\star}=\energy{u}\approx10^{16}\,\unit{GeV}$, the Higgs-boson mass must lie in the interval $134\,\unit{GeV}\lesssim\mechanical{M_H}\lesssim177\,\unit{GeV}$. If $\mechanical{M_H}$ is not within this chimney, the electroweak theory is incomplete; it is an effective theory that will be subsumed in a more comprehensive description. The most favored mass interval cited in section 7.9 lies below the chimney. Problem 7.28 investigates the implications of the triviality and stability bounds in light of current information about a standard-model Higgs boson.

The condition of absolute vacuum stability that leads to the lower bound on $\mechanical{M_H}$ displayed in figure 7.26 is more stringent than is required by observational evidence. It is consistent with observations for the ground state of the electroweak theory to be a *false* (metastable) vacuum that has survived quantum fluctuations until now, so that the mean time to tunnel from our vacuum to a deeper vacuum exceeds the age of the universe. Renormalization-group-improved one-loop calculations of the tunneling probability at zero temperature characterize the stability, metastability, or instability of the electroweak theory in terms of the Higgs-boson mass and the top-quark mass [102]. Should $(\mechanical{M_H},\mechanical{m_t})$ settle in the metastable zone, we would have a provocative hint for new physics below the Planck scale [103].
