> [!TLDR]
> Deeply inelastic scattering tests the parton model and measures electroweak interactions through structure functions, cross sections, and polarization.

<!-- PDF page 219; printed page 205 -->

When hadron structure is examined on a very short time or distance scale, as is possible in large-momentum-transfer collisions, hadrons are found to behave as if

<!-- PDF page 220; printed page 206 -->

they were composed of essentially noninteracting pointlike structures. The pointlike constituents of hadrons have been named *partons* by Feynman. The basic idea of the parton model is analogous to the well-traveled notion that for many purposes a nucleus may be regarded as a collection of structureless and noninteracting nucleons—but with a critical difference. Nucleons are rather easily liberated from nuclei, but the division of a hadron into its constituent partons has never been observed. The parton model has thus had a paradoxical aspect, which may be summarized in the question, How could partons be quasifree within hadrons if they interact so strongly that they cannot be separated? The question became more urgent with each success of the model. Only with the development of the theory of strong interactions known as quantum chromodynamics has a resolution emerged, as we shall see in chapter 8. Much progress has been made on the general problem of hadron structure, but we cannot yet claim a complete solution.

Our principal concern in this section is not with hadron structure, but with the interactions of weak and electromagnetic currents with the fundamental constituents. Thus, we shall develop and apply the parton model in an intuitive fashion that is close to the original spirit in which the model was formulated. Loftier formulations may be found among the suggested readings at the end of the chapter. The heuristic approach has the advantage of leading immediately to a transparent physical picture of hard collisions that is readily generalized to incorporate QCD refinements. The development of this section will have three primary goals: to formulate in a general language the kinematics and observables of inclusive lepton–hadron scattering, to specialize to the parton model and verify its approximate validity, and to investigate what can be learned about the electroweak interactions in deeply inelastic lepton–hadron scattering.

We wish to consider inclusive reactions of the generic form

$$
\ell+N\to\ell'+\text{anything}, \tag{7.4.1}
$$

for which the kinematic notation is indicated in figure 7.8. From the four-momenta designated there we may form the useful invariants

$$
\energy{s}=(\mechanical{\ell}+\mechanical{P})^2, \tag{7.4.2}
$$

$$
\mechanical{Q}^2\equiv-\mechanical{q}^2=-(\mechanical{\ell}-\mechanical{\ell'})^2, \tag{7.4.3}
$$

$$
\energy{\nu}=\frac{\mechanical{q}\cdot\mechanical{P}}{\mechanical{M}}, \tag{7.4.4}
$$

where $\mechanical{M}$ is the target mass and

$$
\mechanical{W}^2=2\mechanical{M}\energy{\nu}+\mechanical{M}^2-\mechanical{Q}^2 \tag{7.4.5}
$$

is the square of the invariant mass of the produced hadronic system, “anything.” In the laboratory frame, in which (neglecting the lepton mass)

$$
\left.\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{\ell^\mu}&=(\energy{E};0,0,\energy{E})\\
\mechanical{\ell'^\mu}&=(\energy{E'};\energy{E'}\sin\theta,0,\energy{E'}\cos\theta)\\
\mechanical{P^\mu}&=(\mechanical{M};0,0,0)
\end{aligned}\right\}, \tag{7.4.6}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{Q}^2&=2\energy{E}\energy{E'}(1-\cos\theta)\\
&=4\energy{E}\energy{E'}\sin^2\frac{\theta}{2}
\end{aligned} \tag{7.4.7}
$$

and recognize as an energy-loss variable

$$
\energy{\nu}=\energy{E}-\energy{E'}. \tag{7.4.8}
$$

From the connection (7.4.5) we see that the line

$$
\mechanical{Q}^2=2\mechanical{M}\energy{\nu} \tag{7.4.9}
$$

in the $\mechanical{Q}^2$-$2\mechanical{M}\energy{\nu}$ plane corresponds to elastic scattering, for which

$$
\mechanical{W}^2=\mechanical{M}^2. \tag{7.4.10}
$$

Similarly, the excitation of a system with mass $\mechanical{M^*}$, such as a discrete resonance, occurs along a line

$$
\mechanical{Q}^2=2\mechanical{M}\energy{\nu}+\mechanical{M}^2-\mechanical{M^*}^2. \tag{7.4.11}
$$

These kinematic regimes are indicated in figure 7.9.

As a first specific example, consider the case of inelastic electron–proton scattering with an unpolarized beam and target. The square of the invariant amplitude, averaged over initial spins and summed over final spins, may be written as

$$
\overline{|\mathcal{M}|^2}=\frac{\electric{e}^4}{(\mechanical{q}^2)^2}L^{\mu\nu}W_{\mu\nu}, \tag{7.4.12}
$$

where the anticipated factors for the spin average have been absorbed into the definitions of the tensors $L^{\mu\nu}$ and $W_{\mu\nu}$, which describe the structures of the leptonic and hadronic vertices, respectively. The lepton tensor is prescribed by QED as

$$
\begin{aligned}
L^{\mu\nu}&=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}[\bar u(\mechanical{\ell'})\gamma^\mu u(\mechanical{\ell})
\bar u(\mechanical{\ell})\gamma^\nu u(\mechanical{\ell'})]\\
&=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}[\gamma^\mu(\mechanical{m}+\not{\mechanical{\ell}})
\gamma^\nu(\mechanical{m}+\not{\mechanical{\ell'}})]\\
&=2[\mechanical{\ell^\mu}\mechanical{\ell'^\nu}
+\mechanical{\ell^\nu}\mechanical{\ell'^\mu}
-g^{\mu\nu}(\mechanical{\ell}\cdot\mechanical{\ell'}-\mechanical{m}^2)].
\end{aligned} \tag{7.4.13}
$$

In the absence of any detailed information about the hadronic vertex, we may write the general form

$$
\begin{aligned}
W_{\mu\nu}={}&V_1g_{\mu\nu}+V_2\mechanical{P_\mu}\mechanical{P_\nu}
+V_3(\mechanical{P_\mu}\mechanical{q_\nu}+\mechanical{P_\nu}\mechanical{q_\mu})\\
&+V_4(\mechanical{P_\mu}\mechanical{q_\nu}-\mechanical{P_\nu}\mechanical{q_\mu})
+V_5\mechanical{q_\mu}\mechanical{q_\nu}
+V_6\epsilon_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}\mechanical{P^\alpha}\mechanical{q^\beta}.
\end{aligned} \tag{7.4.14}
$$

Because $L^{\mu\nu}$ is symmetric under interchange of the indices $\mu$ and $\nu$, the antisymmetric terms proportional to $V_4$ and $V_6$ cannot contribute to the cross section. Current conservation requires that

$$
\mechanical{q^\mu}W_{\mu\nu}=0=\mechanical{q^\nu}W_{\mu\nu}. \tag{7.4.15}
$$

This implies that

$$
V_1\mechanical{q_\nu}+V_2(\mechanical{q}\cdot\mechanical{P})\mechanical{P_\nu}
+V_3(\mechanical{q}\cdot\mechanical{P}\mechanical{q_\nu}+\mechanical{q}^2\mechanical{P_\nu})
+V_5\mechanical{q}^2\mechanical{q_\nu}=0, \tag{7.4.16}
$$

from which the coefficients of $\mechanical{P_\nu}$ and $\mechanical{q_\nu}$ must vanish separately, so that

$$
\left.\begin{aligned}
V_1+(\mechanical{q}\cdot\mechanical{P})V_3+\mechanical{q}^2V_5&=0\\
(\mechanical{q}\cdot\mechanical{P})V_2+\mechanical{q}^2V_3&=0
\end{aligned}\right\}. \tag{7.4.17}
$$

Eliminating

$$
V_3=-\frac{(\mechanical{q}\cdot\mechanical{P})V_2}{\mechanical{q}^2} \tag{7.4.18}
$$

and

$$
V_5=\frac{(\mechanical{q}\cdot\mechanical{P})^2V_2}{\mechanical{q}^4}
-\frac{V_1}{\mechanical{q}^2}, \tag{7.4.19}
$$

we find that

$$
W_{\mu\nu}=V_1\left(g_{\mu\nu}-\frac{\mechanical{q_\mu}\mechanical{q_\nu}}{\mechanical{q}^2}\right)
+V_2\left[\mechanical{P_\mu}-\frac{(\mechanical{q}\cdot\mechanical{P})\mechanical{q_\mu}}{\mechanical{q}^2}\right]
\left[\mechanical{P_\nu}-\frac{(\mechanical{q}\cdot\mechanical{P})\mechanical{q_\nu}}{\mechanical{q}^2}\right], \tag{7.4.20}
$$

<!-- PDF page 223; printed page 209 -->

which is conventionally written as

$$
W_{\mu\nu}=-W_1\left(g_{\mu\nu}-\frac{\mechanical{q_\mu}\mechanical{q_\nu}}{\mechanical{q}^2}\right)
+\frac{W_2}{\mechanical{M}^2}
\left[\mechanical{P_\mu}-\frac{(\mechanical{q}\cdot\mechanical{P})\mechanical{q_\mu}}{\mechanical{q}^2}\right]
\left[\mechanical{P_\nu}-\frac{(\mechanical{q}\cdot\mechanical{P})\mechanical{q_\nu}}{\mechanical{q}^2}\right]. \tag{7.4.21}
$$

The objects $W_{1,2}$ are known as the structure functions for (unpolarized) inelastic electron–proton scattering. All that may be learned about hadron structure from such collisions is contained in these two structure functions, which depend upon $\mechanical{P}$ and $\mechanical{q}$ or, more precisely, upon the invariants $\mechanical{Q}^2$ and $\energy{\nu}$.

To make contact with observables, we neglect lepton masses, so that

$$
\mechanical{\ell}\cdot\mechanical{\ell'}
=\mechanical{q}\cdot\mechanical{\ell'}
=-\mechanical{q}\cdot\mechanical{\ell}
=\frac{\mechanical{Q}^2}{2}, \tag{7.4.22}
$$

and form

$$
L^{\mu\nu}W_{\mu\nu}
=2W_1(\mechanical{Q}^2,\energy{\nu})\mechanical{Q}^2
+W_2(\mechanical{Q}^2,\energy{\nu})
\left[\frac{4(\mechanical{\ell}\cdot\mechanical{P})(\mechanical{\ell'}\cdot\mechanical{P})}{\mechanical{M}^2}
-\mechanical{Q}^2\right]. \tag{7.4.23}
$$

In the laboratory frame, we may express

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{\ell}\cdot\mechanical{P}&=\mechanical{M}\energy{E},\\
\mechanical{\ell'}\cdot\mechanical{P}&=\mechanical{M}\energy{E'},
\end{aligned} \tag{7.4.24}
$$

to obtain

$$
L^{\mu\nu}W_{\mu\nu}=4\energy{E}\energy{E'}
\left[2W_1(\mechanical{Q}^2,\energy{\nu})\sin^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)
+W_2(\mechanical{Q}^2,\energy{\nu})\cos^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\right]. \tag{7.4.25}
$$

The differential cross section in the laboratory frame is given by

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d^2\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\energy{E'}d\Omega'}
&=\frac{1}{16\pi^2}\frac{\energy{E'}}{\energy{E}}\overline{|\mathcal{M}|^2}
=\frac{(4\pi\alpha)^2}{16\pi^2\mechanical{Q}^4}\frac{\energy{E'}}{\energy{E}}L^{\mu\nu}W_{\mu\nu}\\
&=\frac{4\alpha^2\energy{E'}^2}{\mechanical{Q}^4}
\left[2W_1(\mechanical{Q}^2,\energy{\nu})\sin^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)
+W_2(\mechanical{Q}^2,\energy{\nu})\cos^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\right].
\end{aligned} \tag{7.4.26}
$$

It is often more convenient to express the differential cross section with respect to the invariants $\energy{\nu}$ and $\mechanical{Q}^2$ as

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d^2\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\mechanical{Q}^2d\energy{\nu}}
&=\frac{\pi}{\energy{E}\energy{E'}}\frac{d^2\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\energy{E'}d\Omega'}\\
&=\frac{4\pi\alpha^2}{\mechanical{Q}^4}\frac{\energy{E'}}{\energy{E}}
\left[2W_1(\mechanical{Q}^2,\energy{\nu})\sin^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)
+W_2(\mechanical{Q}^2,\energy{\nu})\cos^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\right].
\end{aligned} \tag{7.4.27}
$$

It is interesting to compare the cross section (7.4.26) with the more familiar results for elastic scattering. The Rosenbluth formula for elastic electron–proton

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d^2\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\energy{E'}d\Omega'}
=\frac{4\alpha^2\energy{E'}^2}{\mechanical{Q}^4}\bigg\{&
2\mechanical{Q}^2\left[\frac{\Gamma_1(\mechanical{Q}^2)}{2\mechanical{M}}+\Gamma_2(\mechanical{Q}^2)\right]^2
\sin^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\\
&+[\Gamma_1^2(\mechanical{Q}^2)+\mechanical{Q}^2\Gamma_2^2(\mechanical{q}^2)]
\cos^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\bigg\}
\delta\left(\energy{\nu}-\frac{\mechanical{Q}^2}{2\mechanical{M}}\right),
\end{aligned} \tag{7.4.28}
$$

which has precisely the same structure. For a structureless Dirac proton, also considered in problem 3.3, the form factors are simply

$$
\left.\begin{aligned}
\Gamma_1(\mechanical{Q}^2)&=1\\
\Gamma_2(\mechanical{Q}^2)&=0
\end{aligned}\right\}, \tag{7.4.29}
$$

so we may identify the structure functions for scattering from a pointlike spin-$\tfrac12$ particle as

$$
\left.\begin{aligned}
W_1^{\mathrm{pt}}(\mechanical{Q}^2,\energy{\nu})
&=(\mechanical{Q}^2/4\mechanical{M}^2)\delta(\energy{\nu}-\mechanical{Q}^2/2\mechanical{M})\\
W_2^{\mathrm{pt}}(\mechanical{Q}^2,\energy{\nu})
&=\delta(\energy{\nu}-\mechanical{Q}^2/2\mechanical{M})
\end{aligned}\right\}. \tag{7.4.30}
$$

The dimensionless combinations

$$
\left.\begin{aligned}
2\mechanical{M}W_1^{\mathrm{pt}}(\mechanical{Q}^2,\energy{\nu})
&=(\mechanical{Q}^2/2\mechanical{M}\energy{\nu})\delta(\mechanical{Q}^2/2\mechanical{M}\energy{\nu}-1)\\
\energy{\nu}W_2^{\mathrm{pt}}(\mechanical{Q}^2,\energy{\nu})
&=\delta(\mechanical{Q}^2/2\mechanical{M}\energy{\nu}-1)
\end{aligned}\right\} \tag{7.4.31}
$$

are seen to depend upon the kinematic invariants only through the dimensionless ratio

$$
x=\frac{\mechanical{Q}^2}{2\mechanical{M}\energy{\nu}}. \tag{7.4.32}
$$

Similarly, by examining the form of the differential cross section for electron–elementary-scalar scattering derived in problem 3.1 or by considering directly the structure of the photon–scalar vertex, we see at once that

$$
\left.\begin{aligned}
W_2^{(0)}(\mechanical{Q}^2,\energy{\nu})
&=\delta(\energy{\nu}-\mechanical{Q}^2/2\mechanical{M})\\
W_1^{(0)}(\mechanical{Q}^2,\energy{\nu})&=0
\end{aligned}\right\}. \tag{7.4.33}
$$

To conclude these essentially kinematic developments, let us relate the structure functions to the cross sections for the absorption of virtual photons. Writing the flux of virtual photons as $\signal{\Phi}$, we may define the cross section for absorption of photons of helicity $\lambda$ as

$$
\mechanical{\sigma}_\lambda=\frac{4\pi^2\alpha}{\signal{\Phi}}
\signal{\epsilon_\lambda^{\mu*}}W_{\mu\nu}\signal{\epsilon_\lambda^\nu}, \tag{7.4.34}
$$

<!-- PDF page 225; printed page 211 -->

following the conventions appropriate to real photons. For photons of helicity $\pm1$, for which

$$
\signal{\epsilon_{\pm1}^\mu}=(0;-1,\mp i,0)/\sqrt2, \tag{7.4.35}
$$

we compute

$$
\mechanical{\sigma}_{\mathrm{T}}\equiv\tfrac12
(\mechanical{\sigma}_{+1}+\mechanical{\sigma}_{-1})
=\frac{4\pi^2\alpha}{\signal{\Phi}}W_1, \tag{7.4.36}
$$

whereas for longitudinally polarized (scalar) photons, with

$$
\signal{\epsilon_{\mathrm{S}}^\mu}
=(\sqrt{\mechanical{Q}^2+\energy{\nu}^2};0,0,\energy{\nu})/\sqrt{\mechanical{Q}^2}, \tag{7.4.37}
$$

the cross section is

$$
\mechanical{\sigma}_{\mathrm{S}}=\frac{4\pi^2\alpha}{\signal{\Phi}}
\left[-W_1+\left(1+\frac{\energy{\nu}^2}{\mechanical{Q}^2}\right)W_2\right]. \tag{7.4.38}
$$

The requirement that $\mechanical{\sigma}_{\mathrm{T}}$ and $\mechanical{\sigma}_{\mathrm{S}}$ be nonnegative leads to the restrictions

$$
\left.\begin{aligned}
W_1&\ge0\\
W_2\left(1+\frac{\energy{\nu}^2}{\mechanical{Q}^2}\right)&\ge W_1
\end{aligned}\right\}. \tag{7.4.39}
$$

Finally, we note that the ratio

$$
\frac{\mechanical{\sigma}_{\mathrm{S}}}{\mechanical{\sigma}_{\mathrm{T}}}
=\frac{W_2(1+\energy{\nu}^2/\mechanical{Q}^2)}{W_1}-1 \tag{7.4.40}
$$

is sensitive to the spin of the target. For a structureless spin-$\tfrac12$ target, with structure functions given by (7.4.30), the ratio vanishes as $\energy{\nu}\to\infty$:

$$
\frac{\mechanical{\sigma}_{\mathrm{S}}}{\mechanical{\sigma}_{\mathrm{T}}}
=\frac{2\mechanical{M}}{\energy{\nu}}\underset{\energy{\nu}\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0,\quad
\text{spin-}\tfrac12. \tag{7.4.41}
$$

In contrast, for a spinless target with structure functions given by (7.4.33), the transverse cross section vanishes, so that

$$
\frac{\mechanical{\sigma}_{\mathrm{S}}}{\mechanical{\sigma}_{\mathrm{T}}}
\to\infty,\quad\text{spin-zero}. \tag{7.4.42}
$$

This completes our discussion of the kinematics of electron–proton scattering. We now turn to the parton model itself.

The transition to the parton model is most gracefully made in the infinite-momentum frame, in which the longitudinal momentum of the target (proton, for example) is extremely large. The target is regarded as a collection of $N$ free partons, each carrying a fraction $x_i$ $(i=1,2,\ldots,N)$ of the longitudinal momentum of the target, as shown in figure 7.10. Assuming the mass of a parton to be insignificant both before and after a collision and that the transverse momentum of an incident

<!-- PDF page 226; printed page 212 -->

![[gauge-theories-fig-7-10-infinite-momentum-partons.svg|**Fig. 7.10** Parton-model depiction of the proton in the infinite-momentum frame.]]

parton is negligible, we may write the four-momentum of an individual parton as

$$
\mechanical{p_i^\mu}=x_i\mechanical{P^\mu}. \tag{7.4.43}
$$

Then if interactions among the partons can be neglected, so that the individual current–parton interactions may be treated incoherently, we may write the contribution to $W_2$ due to scattering from a single parton of charge $\electric{e_i}$ as [19]

$$
\begin{aligned}
W_2^{(i)}(\mechanical{Q}^2,\energy{\nu};x_i)
&=x_i\electric{e_i}^2\delta\left(\frac{\mechanical{q}\cdot\mechanical{p_i}}{\mechanical{M}}
-\frac{\mechanical{Q}^2}{2\mechanical{M}}\right)\\
&=x_i\electric{e_i}^2\delta\left(\frac{x_i\mechanical{q}\cdot\mechanical{P}}{\mechanical{M}}
-\frac{\mechanical{Q}^2}{2\mechanical{M}}\right)\\
&=\electric{e_i}^2\delta\left(\energy{\nu}-\frac{\mechanical{Q}^2}{2\mechanical{M}x_i}\right),
\end{aligned} \tag{7.4.44}
$$

which reproduces, as it must, the Rutherford cross section

$$
\frac{d\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\mechanical{Q}^2}
=\frac{4\pi\alpha^2\electric{e_i}^2}{\mechanical{Q}^4} \tag{7.4.45}
$$

at high energies.

The incoherence assumption, or impulse approximation, means that the structure function for electron–proton scattering is simply the sum over the contributions of individual partons:

$$
W_2(\mechanical{Q}^2,\energy{\nu})
=\sum_i\int_0^1 dx_i\,f_i(x_i)W_2^{(i)}(\mechanical{Q}^2,\energy{\nu};x_i), \tag{7.4.46}
$$

where $f_i(x_i)$ gives the probability of finding the $i$th parton with momentum fraction $x_i$. The integration over $dx_i$ is readily carried out using the rule

$$
\int dx\,\delta(h(x))
=\left.\frac{1}{\partial h/\partial x}\right|_{h(x)=0}. \tag{7.4.47}
$$

We find at once that

$$
W_2(\mechanical{Q}^2,\energy{\nu})
=\sum_i\frac{\electric{e_i}^2f_i(x)x}{\energy{\nu}}, \tag{7.4.48}
$$

where the scaling variable $x=\mechanical{Q}^2/2\mechanical{M}\energy{\nu}$ has already been defined in (7.4.32). Consequently, the dimensionless quantity

$$
\energy{\nu}W_2(\mechanical{Q}^2,\energy{\nu})=\sum_i\electric{e_i}^2f_i(x)x, \tag{7.4.49}
$$

obtained as an incoherent sum over the contributions of individual partons, is identified as a function of a single dimensionless variable $x$. It is convenient to define the scaling form as the combination

$$
F_2(x)\equiv\energy{\nu}W_2(\mechanical{Q}^2,\energy{\nu}). \tag{7.4.50}
$$

Knowing the relation between $W_1$ and $W_2$ for structureless particles of spin-zero or spin-$\tfrac12$, we may construct from this result the general form of the cross section. It is again convenient to define a dimensionless form based on $W_1$, which will evidently depend only upon the scaling variable $x$. It is conventionally written as

$$
F_1(x)\equiv\mechanical{M}W_1(\mechanical{Q}^2,\energy{\nu}), \tag{7.4.51}
$$

which is given by

$$
F_1(x)=\begin{cases}
0,&\text{spin-zero partons},\\
\left(\dfrac{1}{2x}\right)F_2(x),&\text{spin-}\tfrac12\text{ partons}.
\end{cases} \tag{7.4.52}
$$

That $\energy{\nu}W_2(\mechanical{Q}^2,\energy{\nu})$ should become independent of $\mechanical{Q}^2$ for fixed values of $x$ as $\energy{\nu},\mechanical{Q}^2\to\infty$ was anticipated by Bjorken [20] and demonstrated in classic experiments by the SLAC–MIT Collaboration [21]. This behavior, which we have seen to be quite natural within the parton model, is in sharp contrast to the comportment of elastic scattering or resonance excitation, both characterized by rapidly falling form factors. Experiments have also shown the ratio $\mechanical{\sigma}_{\mathrm{S}}/\mechanical{\sigma}_{\mathrm{T}}$ to be small [22], suggesting that the charged partons carry spin-$\tfrac12$. The fact that deeply inelastic electron scattering is readily interpreted as scattering from structureless, spin-$\tfrac12$ constituents suggests that the charged partons be identified as quarks. What are the implications of this identification?

To fully exploit the scaling behavior of the cross section, it is convenient to introduce the inelasticity parameter

$$
y\equiv\frac{\energy{\nu}}{\energy{E}}, \tag{7.4.53}
$$

which evidently satisfies

$$
0\le y\le1. \tag{7.4.54}
$$

The cross section may be expressed as

$$
\frac{d^2\mechanical{\sigma}}{dx\,dy}
=\frac{2\mechanical{M}\energy{\nu}^2}{y}\frac{d^2\mechanical{\sigma}}{d\mechanical{Q}^2d\energy{\nu}}
=\frac{4\pi\alpha^2\energy{s}}{\mechanical{Q}^4}[F_2(x)(1-y)+F_1(x)xy^2], \tag{7.4.55}
$$

plus a term of order $\mechanical{M}/\energy{E}$, which may be safely neglected at high energies. According to (7.4.49), the structure functions are directly related to the quark–parton

distribution functions. For $ep$ scattering, we have that

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{F_2^{ep}(x)}{x}
={}&\tfrac49(u(x)+\bar u(x))+\tfrac19(d(x)+\bar d(x))\\
&+\tfrac19(s(x)+\bar s(x))+\cdots,
\end{aligned} \tag{7.4.56}
$$

with $u(x)=f_u(x)$, and so on. The neutron structure functions may be obtained by applying an isospin rotation, which amounts to $u\leftrightarrow d$, whereupon

$$
\frac{F_2^{en}(x)}{x}
=\tfrac49(d(x)+\bar d(x))+\tfrac19(u(x)+\bar u(x))+\cdots, \tag{7.4.57}
$$

where $u(x)$, $d(x)$, $\ldots$, refer to the quark content of the proton. The expressions (7.4.56) and (7.4.57) lead at once to the bounds

$$
\tfrac14\le\frac{F_2^{en}(x)}{F_2^{ep}(x)}\le4. \tag{7.4.58}
$$

Measurements respect the parton-model bounds [23], and the data have the following simple interpretation. For small values of $x$, where the sea of quark–antiquark pairs may be expected to dominate, so that

$$
u(x)\simeq\bar u(x)\simeq d(x)\simeq\bar d(x),\quad x\ll1, \tag{7.4.59}
$$

then we anticipate a ratio close to unity, as is observed. At large $x$, it is the so-called valence quarks that must dominate, which is to say that

$$
u(x),d(x)\gg\bar u(x),\bar d(x),\quad x\to1. \tag{7.4.60}
$$

If the up- and down-quark distributions had the same shape as $x\to1$ (a simple but not compelling hypothesis), which would imply that

$$
u(x)=2d(x),\quad x\to1, \tag{7.4.61}
$$

then the ratio $F_2^{en}(x)/F_2^{ep}(x)$ would be expected to approach $\tfrac23$. The prevailing interpretation that the ratio falls below $\tfrac23$ is taken to indicate that

$$
\frac{d(x)}{u(x)}\to0,\quad x\to1 \tag{7.4.62}
$$

in most modern sets of parton distribution functions. A recent measurement by the Jefferson Lab BoNuS experiment using a spectator-tagging technique to remove the nuclear uncertainties associated with previous deuterium target measurements is shown in figure 7.11.

$$
\int_0^1 dx\,[\tfrac23(u(x)-\bar u(x))-\tfrac13(d(x)-\bar d(x))]=1, \tag{7.4.63}

To proceed further in the analysis of the quark distributions without making strongly model-dependent assumptions, we must make use of information from the charged-current weak interactions. The most general form of the cross section for the inclusive reaction

$$
\nu+N\to\mu+\text{anything} \tag{7.4.69}
$$

may be derived (cf. problem 7.14) by the same methods used to derive the form (7.4.27) for deeply inelastic electron scattering. There is the important difference that the lepton tensor in this case has a V − A structure, and the cross section expression is slightly complicated by the violation of parity. The general result is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d^2\mechanical{\sigma}^\nu}{d\mechanical{Q}^2d\energy{\nu}}
=\frac{G_{\mathrm{F}}^2}{2\pi}\energy{E'}\energy{E}
\bigg[&2W_1^\nu\sin^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)

\end{aligned} \tag{7.4.70}

where the final term arises from the parity-violating $\epsilon_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}\mechanical{P^\alpha}\mechanical{q^\beta}$ term in the general expansion (7.4.14) of the hadronic vertex. The cross section is conveniently recast in terms of the scaling variables $x$ and $y$ as

$$
\frac{d^2\mechanical{\sigma}^{\nu,\bar\nu}}{dx\,dy}
=\frac{G_{\mathrm{F}}^2\mechanical{M}\energy{E}}{\pi}

\pm\mathcal{F}_3(x)xy\left(1-\frac{y}{2}\right)\right], \tag{7.4.71}

where the dimensionless structure functions $\mathcal{F}_{1,2}(x)$ are defined in analogy with $F_{1,2}(x)$ in the electromagnetic case, and

$$
\mathcal{F}_3(x)\equiv\energy{\nu}W_3^\nu(x). \tag{7.4.72}
$$

$$
\mechanical{\sigma}_{\mathrm{t}}(\nu N\to\ell+\text{anything})\propto\energy{E}. \tag{7.4.73}

At higher energies, the parity violations and charge asymmetries implied by the $\mathrm{SU}(2)_{\mathrm{L}}\otimes\mathrm{U}(1)_{\mathrm{Y}}$ electroweak theory become more dramatic. Most of our discussion of the consequences of the standard model for lepton–hadron interactions has taken place in the context of the point-coupling limit $\mechanical{Q}^2\ll\mechanical{M_{W,Z}}^2$. The possibility of studying weak interactions at extremely high energies, at electron–proton colliders or in ultrahigh-energy neutrino scattering, raises the possibility of observing the damping effect of the intermediate-boson propagator upon the total cross section and other observables. It is straightforward in the parton model to calculate the total charged-current cross sections for $e^\mp p$ scattering and $(\nu,\bar\nu)N$ scattering. If there were no intermediate bosons and if Bjorken scaling were perfect, the cross section would rise linearly with the squared c.m. energy $\energy{s}$. An intermediate boson with mass $\mechanical{M_W}\approx80.4\,\unit{GeV}$ causes a pronounced damping of the total cross section (roughly a 50% reduction) at $\energy{s}\approx10^5\,\unit{GeV}^2$, the HERA regime. The first evidence for the influence of the $W$ propagator was obtained in the earliest HERA measurements of the charged-current reaction $ep\to\nu+\text{anything}$ at an equivalent lab energy near $47\,\unit{TeV}$ [31]. Subsequent measurements led to quantitative determinations of $\mechanical{M_W}$; the most precise to date is $\mechanical{M_W}=80.786\pm0.205^{+0.30}_{-0.098}\,\unit{GeV}$ [32], to be compared with the current world average, $\mechanical{M_W}=80.399\pm0.023\,\unit{GeV}$ (Ref. [3]).

The H1 and ZEUS experiments at the $e^\pm p$ collider HERA compared the momentum-transfer dependence of neutral-current $(e^\pm p\to e^\pm+\text{anything})$ and charged-current $(e^\pm p\to(\bar\nu_e,\nu_e)+\text{anything})$ at $\sqrt{\energy{s}}=318\,\unit{GeV}$. A recent summary compiled by H1 and ZEUS is given in figure 7.14. At low values of $\mechanical{Q}^2$, the neutral-current cross section exceeds the charged-current cross section by more than two orders of magnitude, because the electromagnetic interaction is much stronger than the weak interaction at long wavelengths. There the neutral-current cross section is proportional to $1/\mechanical{Q}^4$ due to the photon propagator, whereas the charged-current cross section is proportional to $1/(\mechanical{Q}^2+\mechanical{M_W}^2)^2$. At large values of $\mechanical{Q}^2>(\mechanical{M_W}^2,\mechanical{M_Z}^2)$, the cross sections roughly track each other. This behavior supports the notion that the intrinsic strengths of the weak and electromagnetic interactions are comparable.

Within the standard model, the charged weak current is purely left-handed, and so only left-handed electrons and right-handed positrons participate in the reactions $e^\mp p\to(\nu,\bar\nu)+\text{anything}$ [cf. (7.4.86) and (7.4.87)]. The HERA experiments put this prediction to the test by measuring the total cross sections for charged-current deeply inelastic scattering as functions of the lepton-beam polarization. The results, collected in figure 7.15, display the expected linear variation with polarization [36]. The $e^-p$ cross section vanishes for right-handed electrons, whereas the $e^+p$ cross section vanishes for left-handed positrons.
