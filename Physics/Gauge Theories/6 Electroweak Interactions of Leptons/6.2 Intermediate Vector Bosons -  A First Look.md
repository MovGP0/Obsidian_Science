> [!TLDR]
> Charged vector bosons reproduce the low-energy weak interaction, but their high-energy amplitudes require further interactions to preserve unitarity.

<!-- PDF page 124; printed page 110 -->

Although Fermi’s phenomenological interaction was inspired by the theory of electromagnetism, the analogy was not complete, and we may hope to obtain a more satisfactory theory by pushing the analogy further. An obvious device is to assume that the weak interaction, like quantum electrodynamics, is mediated by vector-boson exchange. The weak intermediate boson must have the following three properties:

1. It carries charge $\pm1$, because the familiar manifestations of the weak interactions (such as $\beta$-decay) are charge-changing.
2. It must be rather massive to reproduce the short range of the weak force.
3. Its parity must be indefinite.

Furthermore, its couplings to the fermions are determined by the low-energy phenomenology. At each $\nu e W$ vertex will occur a factor

$$
-i\left(\frac{G_F\mechanical{M_W}^2}{\sqrt2}\right)^{1/2}\gamma_\mu(1-\gamma_5), \tag{6.2.1}
$$

where $\mechanical{M_W}$ is the mass of the weak intermediate boson. The intermediate vector boson propagator has the standard form for a massive vector meson, derived in problem 4.2,

$$
\frac{-i(g_{\mu\nu}-\mechanical{k_\mu}\mechanical{k_\nu})}{\mechanical{k}^2-\mechanical{M_W}^2}. \tag{6.2.2}
$$

![[gauge-theories-fig-6-6-inverse-muon-w-exchange.svg|**Fig. 6.6** Lowest-order contribution to inverse muon decay, in the intermediate-boson model.]]

According to this picture, inverse muon decay proceeds by the $t$-channel exchange diagram shown in figure 6.6. The Feynman amplitude is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal M&=\frac{iG_F\mechanical{M_W}^2}{\sqrt2}\bar u(\nu,\mechanical{q_2})\gamma^\mu(1-\gamma_5)u(e,\mechanical{p_1})\frac{g_{\mu\nu}-\mechanical{k_\mu}\mechanical{k_\nu}}{\mechanical{k}^2-\mechanical{M_W}^2}\\
&\quad\times\bar u(e,\mechanical{p_2})\gamma^\nu(1-\gamma_5)u(\nu,\mechanical{q_1}),
\end{aligned} \tag{6.2.3}
$$

with $\mechanical{k}=\mechanical{p_2}-\mechanical{q_1}=\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{q_2}$. By substituting $\mechanical{k_\mu}=(\mechanical{p_1}-\mechanical{q_2})_\mu$ and $\mechanical{k_\nu}=(\mechanical{p_2}-\mechanical{q_1})_\nu$ and using the Dirac equation, we find that the contribution of the $\mechanical{k_\mu}\mechanical{k_\nu}$ term in the propagator is of order $(\mechanical{m}^2/\mechanical{M_W}^2)$ and may be safely neglected. What remains is simply the amplitude we encountered in the point-coupling theory, times $\mechanical{M_W}^2/(\mechanical{M_W}^2-\mechanical{k}^2)$. The point-coupling result is recovered in the limit $\mechanical{M_W}\rightarrow\infty$. Without further computation, we have

$$
\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega_{\mathrm{c.m.}}}(\nu_\mu e\rightarrow\mu\nu_e)
\approx\frac{G_F^2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}[1-(\mechanical{\mu}^2-\mechanical{m}^2)/2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}]^2}
{2\pi^2[1+\mechanical{m}\energy{E}(1-\cos\theta)/\mechanical{M_W}^2]^2}, \tag{6.2.4}
$$

where $\mechanical{m}$ and $\mechanical{\mu}$ have been neglected with respect to $\mechanical{M_W}$ and $\energy{E}$. The integrated cross section

$$
\sigma(\nu_\mu e\rightarrow\mu\nu_e)\approx\frac{2G_F^2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{\pi}
\frac{[1-(\mechanical{\mu}^2-\mechanical{m}^2)/2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}]^2}{(1+2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}/\mechanical{M_W}^2)} \tag{6.2.5}
$$

reduces to the form (6.1.74) at modest energies but approaches a constant value at high energies:

$$
\lim_{\energy{E}\rightarrow\infty}\sigma(\nu_\mu e\rightarrow\mu\nu_e)=\frac{G_F^2\mechanical{M_W}^2}{\pi}. \tag{6.2.6}
$$

<!-- PDF page 126; printed page 112 -->

This is a great improvement over the point-coupling theory, but a problem remains: the $s$-wave amplitude violates partial-wave unitarity. To see this, we write

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal M_0&=\frac{\sqrt s}{2}\int_{-1}^1d(\cos\theta)f(\theta)\\
&=\frac{G_F\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{\pi\sqrt2}\int_{-1}^1dz\left[1+\frac{\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{\mechanical{M_W}^2}(1-z)\right]^{-1}\\
&=\frac{G_F\mechanical{M_W}^2}{\pi\sqrt2}\ln\left(1+\frac{2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{\mechanical{M_W}^2}\right)\\
&=\frac{G_F\mechanical{M_W}^2}{\pi\sqrt2}\ln\left(1+\frac{s}{\mechanical{M_W}^2}\right).
\end{aligned} \tag{6.2.7}
$$

In this instance the unitarity constraint (6.1.79) is respected, as long as

$$
s\le\mechanical{M_W}^2\left[\exp\left(\frac{\pi\sqrt2}{G_F\mechanical{M_W}^2}\right)-1\right]. \tag{6.2.8}
$$

Notice that in the point-coupling limit $\mechanical{M_W}\rightarrow\infty$ we recover the result (6.1.84). These violations of unitarity arise at incredibly high energies (for $\mechanical{M_W}=100\,\unit{GeV}$, for example, unitarity is respected up to beam energies of $3.5\times10^{23}\,\unit{GeV}$), and it might be hoped that the very mild violation of unitarity could be conquered in higher orders of perturbation theory. This is not the case. The $\mechanical{k_\mu}\mechanical{k_\nu}$ term in the $W$-boson propagator makes the theory nonrenormalizable by inducing new divergences in each order of perturbation theory. Equivalently, the introduction of the intermediate boson causes divergence problems in new physical processes, as we shall see presently, that make this ad hoc theory unacceptable. For the moment, however, let us continue to investigate the consequences of the $W$ in leptonic interactions.

In the crossed reaction,

$$
\bar\nu_e e\rightarrow\bar\nu_\mu\mu, \tag{6.2.9}
$$

the intermediate boson appears as a direct-channel resonance, as shown in figure 6.7. The differential cross section is

$$
\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega_{\mathrm{c.m.}}}(\bar\nu_e e\rightarrow\bar\nu_\mu\mu)
=\frac{G_F^2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{8\pi^2}\frac{(1-\cos\theta)^2}{(1-2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}/\mechanical{M_W}^2)^2}
\left[1-\frac{(\mechanical{\mu}^2-\mechanical{m}^2)}{2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}\right]^2, \tag{6.2.10}
$$

which differs from the point-coupling result only by the factor $1/(1-2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}/\mechanical{M_W}^2)^2$. The cross section becomes

$$
\sigma(\bar\nu_e e\rightarrow\bar\nu_\mu\mu)=\frac{2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}G_F^2[1-(\mechanical{\mu}^2-\mechanical{m}^2)/2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}]^2}
{3\pi(1-2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}/\mechanical{M_W}^2)^2}, \tag{6.2.11}
$$

<!-- PDF page 127; printed page 113 -->

![[gauge-theories-fig-6-7-direct-channel-w-resonance.svg|**Fig. 6.7** Lowest-order contribution to the reaction $\bar\nu_e e\rightarrow\bar\nu_\mu\mu$ in the intermediate-boson model.]]

which vanishes in the high-energy limit

$$
\sigma(\bar\nu_e e\rightarrow\bar\nu_\mu\mu)\xrightarrow{\energy{E}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{G_F^2\mechanical{M_W}^4}{6\pi\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}. \tag{6.2.12}
$$

The apparent infinity in the cross section at $s=2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}=\mechanical{M_W}^2$ arises only because we have neglected the width of the unstable intermediate boson, an oversight we shall remedy at once. That aside, the unitarity problems for leptonic processes may be regarded as solved, at least in a practical sense, by the tree diagrams involving $W$ exchange.

Nothing that we have said fixes the mass of the intermediate boson, which remains a parameter. Once the mass is specified, however, the decay characteristics of the intermediate vector boson are essentially determined by the requirement that the low-energy phenomenology of the V − A theory be reproduced. In particular, we can predict definitely the rates for leptonic decays of the $W$.

In the rest frame of the $W^-$, the outgoing momenta for the decay

$$
W^-\rightarrow e(\mechanical{p})\bar\nu_e(\mechanical{q}) \tag{6.2.13}
$$

are

$$
\left.\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{p}&=\frac{\mechanical{M_W}}2(1;\sin\theta,0,\cos\theta)\\
\mechanical{q}&=\frac{\mechanical{M_W}}2(1;-\sin\theta,0,-\cos\theta)
\end{aligned}\right\}, \tag{6.2.14}
$$

<!-- PDF page 128; printed page 114 -->

where the electron mass has been neglected. The matrix element for the decay is given by

$$
\mathcal M=-i\left(\frac{G_F\mechanical{M_W}^2}{\sqrt2}\right)^{1/2}\bar u(e,\mechanical{p})\gamma_\mu(1-\gamma_5)v(\nu,\mechanical{q})\epsilon^\mu, \tag{6.2.15}
$$

where $\epsilon^\mu\equiv(0;\hat{\boldsymbol\epsilon})$ is the polarization vector of the decaying particle. Squaring the matrix element and summing over final-particle spins, we have, neglecting the electron mass,

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\mathcal M|^2&=\frac{G_F\mechanical{M_W}^2}{\sqrt2}\operatorname{tr}[\not\epsilon(1-\gamma_5)\not{\mechanical{q}}(1+\gamma_5)\not\epsilon^*\not{\mechanical{p}}]\\
&=\frac{G_F\mechanical{M_W}^2}{\sqrt2}\,2\operatorname{tr}[(1+\gamma_5)\not\epsilon\not{\mechanical{q}}\not\epsilon^*\not{\mechanical{p}}]\\
&=\frac{8G_F\mechanical{M_W}^2}{\sqrt2}(\epsilon\cdot\mechanical{q}\,\epsilon^*\cdot\mechanical{p}-\epsilon\cdot\epsilon^*\,\mechanical{p}\cdot\mechanical{q}+\epsilon\cdot\mechanical{p}\,\epsilon^*\cdot\mechanical{q}+i\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\epsilon^\mu\mechanical{q^\nu}\epsilon^{*\rho}\mechanical{p^\sigma}).
\end{aligned} \tag{6.2.16}
$$

The decay rate must be independent of the polarization of the decaying particle, so we choose first the simplest case of longitudinal polarization, helicity zero,

$$
\epsilon^\mu=(0;0,0,1)=\epsilon^{\mu*}, \tag{6.2.17}
$$

for which the $\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$ term vanishes. We then have, at once,

$$
|\mathcal M|^2=\frac{4G_F\mechanical{M_W}^4}{\sqrt2}\sin^2\theta, \tag{6.2.18}
$$

so that, by virtue of (B.1.4), the differential decay rate is

$$
\frac{d\Gamma}{d\Omega}(W_0^-\rightarrow e^-\bar\nu_e)=\frac{|\mathcal M|^2}{64\pi^2\mechanical{M_W}}=\frac{G_F\mechanical{M_W}^3}{16\pi^2\sqrt2}\sin^2\theta. \tag{6.2.19}
$$

The leptonic decay rate is, therefore,

$$
\Gamma(W^-\rightarrow e^-\bar\nu_e)=\frac{G_F\mechanical{M_W}^3}{6\pi\sqrt2}\approx437\,\unit{MeV}\left(\frac{\mechanical{M_W}}{100\,\unit{GeV}}\right)^3. \tag{6.2.20}
$$

The difficulty of the calculation is not greatly increased for other polarizations. For helicity $=+1$,

$$
\epsilon^\mu=\frac{(0;-1,-i,0)}{\sqrt2}, \tag{6.2.21}
$$

<!-- PDF page 129; printed page 115 -->

![[gauge-theories-fig-6-8-w-decay-spin-configurations.svg|**Fig. 6.8** Spin configurations for the leptonic decay of intermediate bosons polarized along the positive z-axis: (a) $W^-\rightarrow e^-\bar\nu_e$; (b) $W^+\rightarrow e^+\nu_e$.]]

the square of the matrix element is

$$
|\mathcal M|^2=\frac{G_F\mechanical{M_W}^4}{\sqrt2}\,2[(1+\cos^2\theta)-2\cos\theta], \tag{6.2.22}
$$

where the final term is the contribution from $\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$. Consequently, the differential decay rate is given by

$$
\frac{d\Gamma}{d\Omega}(W_{+1}^-\rightarrow e^-\bar\nu_e)=\frac{G_F\mechanical{M_W}^3}{32\pi^2\sqrt2}(1-\cos\theta)^2, \tag{6.2.23}
$$

which corresponds, as it must, to the total rate given by (6.2.20). The result for helicity $=-1$ is simply obtained by replacing $\epsilon^\mu\leftrightarrow\epsilon^{\mu*}$ in the expression for $|\mathcal M|^2$. This yields

$$
\frac{d\Gamma}{d\Omega}(W_{-1}^-\rightarrow e^-\bar\nu_e)=\frac{G_F\mechanical{M_W}^3}{32\pi^2\sqrt2}(1+\cos\theta)^2. \tag{6.2.24}
$$

The angular dependences merely reflect the helicity correlations of the V − A theory. This is illustrated in figure 6.8. Because the electron emitted the $W^-$ decay is left-handed, it is forbidden to travel parallel to the direction of $W^-$ polarization. For the decay $W^+\rightarrow e^+\nu_e$, the situation is just reversed: the emitted positron, being right-handed, tends to follow the direction of $W^+$ polarization. This effect is an example of C-violation in the weak interactions; the correlation played an important role in the discovery of intermediate bosons in high-energy proton–antiproton collisions, as we shall see in the discussion surrounding figure 7.18.

We have already mentioned that, whereas the introduction of the intermediate boson softens the divergence of the $s$-wave amplitude for the process $\nu_\mu e\rightarrow\mu\nu_e$, it

gives rise to new divergences in other processes. The most celebrated occurs in the reaction [8]

$$
\nu_e(\mechanical{q_1})\bar\nu_e(\mechanical{q_2})\rightarrow W_0^+(\mechanical{k_+})+W_0^-(\mechanical{k_-}), \tag{6.2.25}
$$

where the subscript 0 again denotes longitudinal polarization. According to the picture we have developed until now, we need only evaluate the electron-exchange diagram shown in figure 6.9. We define

$$
\mechanical{P}=\mechanical{q_1}-\mechanical{k_+}=\mechanical{k_-}-\mechanical{q_2}. \tag{6.2.26}
$$

The matrix element is

$$
\mathcal M=\frac{-iG_F\mechanical{M_W}^2}{\sqrt2}\bar v(\nu,\mechanical{q_2})\not\!\epsilon_-^*(1-\gamma_5)\frac{\mechanical{\not\!P}+\mechanical{m}}{\mechanical{P}^2-\mechanical{m}^2}\not\!\epsilon_+^*(1-\gamma_5)u(\nu,\mechanical{q_1}), \tag{6.2.27}
$$

and in the c.m. frame, the momentum four-vectors are

$$
\left.\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{q_1}&\equiv(\energy{Q};0,0,\energy{Q})\\
\mechanical{q_2}&\equiv(\energy{Q};0,0,-\energy{Q})\\
\mechanical{k_+}&\equiv(\energy{Q};\mechanical{K}\sin\theta,0,\mechanical{K}\cos\theta)\\
\mechanical{k_-}&\equiv(\energy{Q};-\mechanical{K}\sin\theta,0,-\mechanical{K}\cos\theta)
\end{aligned}\right\}, \tag{6.2.28}
$$

with $\mechanical{K}=\sqrt{\energy{Q}^2-\mechanical{M_W}^2}$. If $\epsilon_\pm^\mu=(0;\hat{\boldsymbol\epsilon}_\pm)$ represents the $W^\pm$ polarization in its rest frame, then after a boost to the c.m. frame, we have

$$
\epsilon_\pm^\mu=\left[\frac{\mechanical{\mathbf k_\pm}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol\epsilon}_\pm}{\mechanical{M_W}};\hat{\boldsymbol\epsilon}_\pm+\frac{\mechanical{\mathbf k_\pm}(\mechanical{\mathbf k_\pm}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol\epsilon}_\pm)}{\mechanical{M_W}(\energy{Q}+\mechanical{M_W})}\right]. \tag{6.2.29}
$$

For the case of longitudinal polarization, the c.m. polarization vector is, thus,

$$
\epsilon_\pm^\mu=\left(\frac{\mechanical{K}}{\mechanical{M_W}};\frac{\energy{Q}\hat{\mathbf k}_\pm}{\mechanical{M_W}}\right), \tag{6.2.30}
$$

<!-- PDF page 131; printed page 117 -->

which, in the limit of high energies $\energy{Q}\approx\mechanical{K}\gg\mechanical{M_W}$, approaches the limiting form

$$
\epsilon_\pm^\mu\xrightarrow{\mechanical{K}\gg\mechanical{M_W}}\frac{\mechanical{k_\pm}}{\mechanical{M_W}}. \tag{6.2.31}
$$

The amplitude for the production of longitudinally polarized intermediate bosons at high energies thus becomes

$$
\mathcal M=\frac{-iG_F}{\sqrt2}\bar v(\nu,\mechanical{q_2})\mechanical{\not\!k_-}(1-\gamma_5)\frac{\mechanical{\not\!P}+\mechanical{m}}{\mechanical{P}^2-\mechanical{m}^2}\mechanical{\not\!k_+}(1-\gamma_5)u(\nu,\mechanical{q_1}). \tag{6.2.32}
$$

Using the Dirac equations

$$
\left.\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{\not\!q_1}u(\nu,\mechanical{q_1})&=0\\
\bar v(\nu,\mechanical{q_2})\mechanical{\not\!q_2}&=0
\end{aligned}\right\}, \tag{6.2.33}
$$

we replace

$$
\mechanical{\not\!k_+}\rightarrow\mechanical{\not\!k_+}-\mechanical{\not\!q_1}=-\mechanical{\not\!P} \tag{6.2.34}
$$

and

$$
\mechanical{\not\!k_-}\rightarrow\mechanical{\not\!k_-}-\mechanical{\not\!q_2}=\mechanical{\not\!P} \tag{6.2.35}
$$

to obtain

$$
\mathcal M=\frac{iG_F}{\sqrt2}\bar v(\nu,\mechanical{q_2})\mechanical{\not\!P}(1-\gamma_5)\frac{\mechanical{\not\!P}\mechanical{\not\!P}}{\mechanical{P}^2}(1-\gamma_5)u(\nu,\mechanical{q_1}), \tag{6.2.36}
$$

where we have everywhere discarded the electron mass $\mechanical{m}$. Because $\mechanical{\not\!P}\mechanical{\not\!P}=\mechanical{P}^2$, we now have

$$
\mathcal M=iG_F\sqrt2\bar v(\nu,\mechanical{q_2})\mechanical{\not\!P}(1-\gamma_5)u(\nu,\mechanical{q_1}). \tag{6.2.37}
$$

To display the high-energy behavior, it is simplest to evaluate

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\mathcal M|^2&=2G_F^2\operatorname{tr}[\mechanical{\not\!P}(1-\gamma_5)\mechanical{\not\!q_1}(1+\gamma_5)\mechanical{\not\!P}\mechanical{\not\!q_2}]\\
&=4G_F^2\operatorname{tr}[(1-\gamma_5)\mechanical{\not\!q_1}\mechanical{\not\!P}\mechanical{\not\!q_2}\mechanical{\not\!P}]\\
&=16G_F^2(2\mechanical{q_1}\cdot\mechanical{P}\;\mechanical{q_2}\cdot\mechanical{P}-\mechanical{q_1}\cdot\mechanical{q_2}\;\mechanical{P}^2)\\
&=32G_F^2\energy{Q}^2\mechanical{K}^2\sin^2\theta.
\end{aligned} \tag{6.2.38}
$$

The angular dependence is again readily understood in terms of angular momentum conservation. At high energies, the cross section becomes

$$
\sigma(\nu\bar\nu\rightarrow W_0^+W_0^-)\xrightarrow{s\gg\mechanical{M_W}^2}\frac{G_F^2s}{3\pi}, \tag{6.2.39}
$$

in gross violation of unitarity.

Equivalently, we may remark that the second-order contribution to the neutrino–antineutrino scattering amplitude, given by the box diagram in figure 6.10, which is the square of the amplitude in figure 6.9, grows as $s^2$. Because the $\mechanical{k_\mu}\mechanical{k_\nu}$ piece of the $W$-boson propagator gives rise to more severe divergences in each order of perturbation theory, it seems imperative to find some means of eradicating the divergence order by order. We may accomplish this by inventing new physical particles, which will appear in new Feynman diagrams, in which the couplings are astutely chosen to cause cancellations of the offending divergences.

In the case of the reaction $\nu\bar\nu\rightarrow W^+W^-$, for which we have the diagram of figure 6.9, a possible solution [9] (though not the one chosen by nature) is to postulate a new *paralepton* $E^+$ with the same lepton number as $e^-$ and $\nu_e$, which gives rise to the diagram shown in figure 6.11. If the $\nu EW$ coupling is chosen to be $-i[G_E\mechanical{M_W}^2/\sqrt2]^{1/2}\gamma_\mu(1-\gamma_5)$, a V − A form with arbitrary strength, the amplitude corresponding to figure 6.11 is simply

$$
\mathcal M=\frac{-iG_E\mechanical{M_W}^2}{\sqrt2}\bar v(\nu,\mechanical{q_2})\not\!\epsilon_+^*(1-\gamma_5)\frac{\mechanical{\not\!R}+\mechanical{m_E}}{\mechanical{R}^2-\mechanical{m_E}^2}\not\!\epsilon_-^*(1-\gamma_5)u(\nu,\mechanical{q_1}), \tag{6.2.40}
$$

<!-- PDF page 133; printed page 119 -->

where

$$
\mechanical{R}\equiv\mechanical{q_1}-\mechanical{k_-}=\mechanical{k_+}-\mechanical{q_2}=-\mechanical{P}+\mechanical{q_1}-\mechanical{q_2} \tag{6.2.41}
$$

and $\mechanical{P}$ was defined in (6.2.26). Neglecting fermion masses and taking both $W^+$ and $W^-$ to be longitudinally polarized, we have, using (6.2.31),

$$
\mathcal M=\frac{-iG_E}{\sqrt2}\bar v(\nu,\mechanical{q_2})\mechanical{\not\!k_+}(1-\gamma_5)\frac{\mechanical{\not\!R}\mechanical{\not\!k_-}}{\mechanical{R}^2}(1-\gamma_5)u(\nu,\mechanical{q_1}). \tag{6.2.42}
$$

Between spinors we may replace

$$
\mechanical{\not\!k_-}\rightarrow-\mechanical{\not\!R} \tag{6.2.43}
$$

and

$$
\mechanical{\not\!k_+}\rightarrow-\mechanical{\not\!P}, \tag{6.2.44}
$$

so the amplitude becomes

$$
\mathcal M=-iG_E\sqrt2\bar v(\nu,\mechanical{q_2})\mechanical{\not\!P}(1-\gamma_5)u(\nu,\mechanical{q_1}), \tag{6.2.45}
$$

which is precisely the negative of the original amplitude (6.2.37), provided that the new coupling constant is chosen equal to the old,

$$
G_E=G_F. \tag{6.2.46}
$$

This sort of order-by-order divergence cancellation is familiar in electrodynamics (see problems 3.5 and 6.10), where it is ensured by gauge invariance. Within the context of perturbation theory, such a cancellation is a prerequisite to renormalizability, because cancellations between different orders would be unstable against small changes in the coupling constant.

It is remarkable that a full program of divergence cancellation can be carried out systematically for the weak and electromagnetic interactions [10]. More remarkable still is that systematic pursuit of the program leads to theories with precisely the interaction structure of spontaneously broken gauge theories. This perhaps reflects the deep connection between gauge invariance, “safe” high-energy behavior, and renormalizability. It should not be left unsaid that for many physicists (the author among them), the intricate cancellations that we witness in the course of a gauge-theory calculation provide—together with the elegance of the gauge principle and the experimental support specific models have received, to be sure—an important reason for believing that gauge theories have something to do with reality. On that subjective note, let us now introduce the first elements of a gauge theory of the weak and electromagnetic interactions that has matured into a new law of nature.
