> [!TLDR]
> The Higgs boson couples to fermions and weak bosons; these couplings determine its decay widths and production rates.

The factor shown beside the source vertex is

$$
\frac{-i\zeta_f}{\sqrt2}=\frac{-i\mechanical{m_f}}v=\frac{-ig\mechanical{m_f}}{2\mechanical{M_W}}=-i\mechanical{m_f}(G_F\sqrt2)^{1/2}.
$$

The introduction of a complex doublet of self-interacting auxiliary scalar fields has given rise to spontaneous breaking of the local $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L\otimes\mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ gauge symmetry. This has led, by means of the Higgs mechanism, to several agreeable consequences: the gauge bosons associated with the weak interactions have acquired masses, as have the charged fermions [24], and a partial unification of the weak and electromagnetic interactions has been achieved. In place of three independent gauge coupling constants $\electric{e}$, $g$, and $g'$ associated with the $\mathrm{U}(1)_{\mathrm{EM}}$, $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L$, and $\mathrm{U}(1)_Y$ gauge groups, there are now only two—the electric charge $\electric{e}$ and the weak mixing parameter $\sin^2\theta_W$. (Further reduction of the number of independent parameters will be discussed in chapter 9.) The resulting theory has an improved high-energy behavior, in that amplitudes corresponding to tree diagrams (Feynman diagrams without loops) are nondivergent, and indeed the theory can be made renormalizable (cf. §6.8). As we have begun to see, the low-energy phenomenology of the model is in excellent accord with experimental findings. An uninvited guest at these proceedings is the Higgs boson, the massive physical scalar particle that remains after spontaneous symmetry breaking. This section is devoted to a first look at the properties of this particle, which is an essential element of the standard electroweak theory. We shall discuss the interactions of the Higgs boson with fermions and gauge bosons, examine its role in divergence cancellation, consider prospects for detection of the Higgs boson, and seek constraints on the Higgs-boson mass. We defer a discussion of dynamical-symmetry-breaking alternatives to the standard Higgs scenario to section 8.9.

The interaction of the Higgs boson with fermions has already been given in the Yukawa Lagrangian (6.3.41), which leads to the Feynman rule shown in figure 6.24. We may at once calculate the rate for the decay

$$
H\rightarrow f\bar f. \tag{6.5.1}
$$

The invariant amplitude is given by

$$
\mathcal M=-i\mechanical{m_f}(G_F\sqrt2)^{1/2}\bar u(f,\mechanical{p_1})v(f,\mechanical{p_2}), \tag{6.5.2}
$$

<!-- PDF page 161; printed page 147 -->

where the c.m. momenta of the emitted fermions may be written as

$$
\left.\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{p_1}&=(\tfrac12\mechanical{M_H};\mechanical{q}\sin\theta,0,\mechanical{q}\cos\theta)\\
\mechanical{p_2}&=(\tfrac12\mechanical{M_H};-\mechanical{q}\sin\theta,0,-\mechanical{q}\cos\theta)
\end{aligned}\right\}, \tag{6.5.3}
$$

where

$$
\mechanical{q}=\sqrt{\tfrac14\mechanical{M_H}^2-\mechanical{m_f}^2}. \tag{6.5.4}
$$

Consequently, we compute

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\mathcal M|^2&=G_F\mechanical{m_f}^2\sqrt2\operatorname{tr}[\mechanical{\not\!p_2}\mechanical{\not\!p_1}-\mechanical{m_f}^2]\\
&=4G_F\mechanical{m_f}^2\sqrt2(\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2}-\mechanical{m_f}^2)\\
&=2G_F\mechanical{M_H}^2\mechanical{m_f}^2\sqrt2(1-4\mechanical{m_f}^2/\mechanical{M_H}^2).
\end{aligned} \tag{6.5.5}
$$

The differential decay rate is, according to (B.1.4),

$$
\frac{d\Gamma}{d\Omega}=\frac{|\mathcal M|^2}{64\pi^2\mechanical{M_H}}=\frac{G_F\mechanical{m_f}^2\mechanical{M_H}}{16\pi^2\sqrt2}\cdot N_c\cdot\left(1-\frac{4\mechanical{m_f}^2}{\mechanical{M_H}^2}\right)^{3/2}, \tag{6.5.6}
$$

which is isotropic as it must be for the decay of a spinless particle. Here we have inserted the color factor $N_c=(1,3)$ for (leptons, quarks), anticipating that the Higgs mechanism gives mass to quarks as well as leptons (cf. §7.1). The $f\bar f$ partial width is

$$
\Gamma(H\rightarrow f\bar f)=\frac{G_F\mechanical{m_f}^2\mechanical{M_H}}{4\pi\sqrt2}\cdot N_c\cdot\left(1-\frac{4\mechanical{m_f}^2}{\mechanical{M_H}^2}\right)^{3/2}, \tag{6.5.7}
$$

which is proportional to $N_c\mechanical{m_f}^2\mechanical{M_H}$ as the Higgs-boson mass becomes large compared to the fermion mass. The dominant decay mode of a light $(\mechanical{M_H}\lesssim2\mechanical{M_W})$ Higgs boson is, therefore, into pairs of the most massive fermion that is kinematically accessible.

The Feynman rules for the interactions of Higgs bosons with gauge bosons and for Higgs-boson self-interactions may be obtained by expanding $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{scalar}}$ (6.3.17) in terms of the U-gauge fields, as we did partially in (6.3.33). The Feynman rules are shown in figure 6.25. As a first application, the rates for decay of a heavy Higgs boson into a pair of intermediate vector bosons are given by (cf. problem 6.8)

$$
\Gamma(H\rightarrow W^+W^-)=\frac{G_F\mechanical{M_H}^3}{32\pi\sqrt2}\frac{(1-x)^{1/2}}x(3x^2-4x+4), \tag{6.5.8}
$$

$$
\Gamma(H\rightarrow Z^0Z^0)=\frac{G_F\mechanical{M_H}^3}{64\pi\sqrt2}\frac{(1-x')^{1/2}}{x'}(3x'^2-4x'+4), \tag{6.5.9}
$$

where $x=4\mechanical{M_W}^2/\mechanical{M_H}^2$ and $x'=4\mechanical{M_Z}^2/\mechanical{M_H}^2=x/\cos^2\theta_W$. The rates for decays into weak-boson pairs are asymptotically proportional to $\mechanical{M_H}^3$ and $\tfrac12\mechanical{M_H}^3$, respectively.

The factors shown beside the six source vertices are:

(a) $W_\nu W_\mu H$:

$$
ig\mechanical{M_W}g_{\mu\nu}=2i\mechanical{M_W}^2(G_F\sqrt2)^{1/2}g_{\mu\nu}.
$$

(b) $Z_\nu Z_\mu H$:

$$
\frac{ig\mechanical{M_Z}}{\cos\theta_W}g_{\mu\nu}=2i\mechanical{M_Z}^2(G_F\sqrt2)^{1/2}g_{\mu\nu}.
$$

(c) $HHH$:

$$
-6i|\lambda|v=-i\frac{3g\mechanical{M_H}^2}{2\mechanical{M_W}}=-3i\mechanical{M_H}^2(G_F\sqrt2)^{1/2}.
$$

(d) $HHHH$:

$$
-6i|\lambda|=\frac{-3ig^2\mechanical{M_H}^2}{4\mechanical{M_W}^2}=-3i\mechanical{M_H}^2G_F\sqrt2.
$$

(e) $W_\mu^+W_\nu^-HH$:

$$
\frac{ig^2}2=2i\mechanical{M_W}^2G_F\sqrt2\,g_{\mu\nu}.
$$

(f) $Z_\mu Z_\nu HH$:

$$
\frac{ig^2}{2\cos^2\theta_W}=2i\mechanical{M_Z}^2G_F\sqrt2\,g_{\mu\nu}.
$$

In the final factors of (6.5.8) and (6.5.9), $2x^2$ and $2x'^2$, respectively, arise from decays into transversely polarized gauge bosons. The dominant decays for large $\mechanical{M_H}$ are into pairs of longitudinally polarized weak bosons.

Branching fractions for decay modes that may hold promise for the detection of a Higgs boson are displayed in figure 6.26. In addition to the $f\bar f$ and $VV$ modes that arise at tree level, the plot includes the $\gamma\gamma$, $Z\gamma$, and two-gluon modes that proceed through loop diagrams. The rare $\gamma\gamma$ channel offers an important target for LHC experiments, if the Higgs boson is light, because the relatively benign backgrounds may be overcome by fine resolution.

Below the $W^+W^-$ threshold, the standard-model Higgs boson is rather narrow, with $\Gamma(H\rightarrow\mathrm{all})\lesssim1\,\unit{GeV}$. Far above the threshold for decay into gauge-boson pairs, the total width is proportional to $\mechanical{M_H}^3$. As its mass increases toward $1\,\unit{TeV}$, the Higgs boson becomes highly unstable, with a perturbative width approaching its mass. It would, therefore, be observed as an enhanced rate rather than a distinct resonance. The Higgs-boson total width is plotted as a function of $\mechanical{M_H}$ in figure 6.27.

Because a light Higgs boson would appear as a narrow resonance, it is worth asking whether it might be discovered or studied in detail at a “Higgs factory” in the formation reaction

$$
e^+e^-\rightarrow H\rightarrow\mathrm{all}, \tag{6.5.10}
$$

shown in figure 6.28(a). The cross section is (cf. problem 6.7)

$$
\sigma(e^+e^-\rightarrow H)=\frac{4\pi\Gamma(H\rightarrow e^+e^-)\Gamma(H\rightarrow\mathrm{all})}{(s-\mechanical{M_H}^2)^2+\mechanical{M_H}^2\Gamma(H\rightarrow\mathrm{all})^2} \tag{6.5.11}
$$

![[gauge-theories-fig-6-28-higgs-production.svg|**Fig. 6.28** (a) Feynman diagram for direct-channel Higgs-boson formation in $e^+e^-$ annihilations. (b) “Higgs-strahlung” diagram for associated production of a Higgs boson and $Z^0$-boson in $e^+e^-$ annihilations.]]

where $\sqrt s$ is the $e^+e^-$ c.m. energy. The peak cross section (at $s=\mechanical{M_H}^2$) is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_{\mathrm{peak}}(e^+e^-\rightarrow H)&=\frac{4\pi}{\mechanical{M_H}^2}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(H\rightarrow e^+e^-)}{\Gamma(H\rightarrow\mathrm{all})}\\
&=4.89\times10^{-31}\,\unit{cm^2}\left[\frac{100\,\unit{GeV}}{\mechanical{M_H}}\right]^2\cdot\frac{\Gamma(H\rightarrow e^+e^-)}{\Gamma(H\rightarrow\mathrm{all})}.
\end{aligned} \tag{6.5.12}
$$

<!-- PDF page 165; printed page 151 -->

The tiny branching fraction $\Gamma(H\rightarrow e^+e^-)/\Gamma(H\rightarrow\mathrm{all})\lesssim10^{-8}$, which is owed to the small electron mass, makes this a dismal prospect, even for a relatively light Higgs boson. It is worth noting that cross sections for the reaction $\mu^+\mu^-\rightarrow H$ are larger by a factor $(\mechanical{m_\mu}/\mechanical{m_e})^2=42,750$.

Because the Higgs-boson couplings with gauge bosons are not suppressed by small fermion masses, reactions involving gauge bosons provide favorable means for Higgs-boson production. The reaction

$$
e^+e^-\rightarrow HZ^0, \tag{6.5.13}
$$

which proceeds via the $s$-channel formation of a virtual $Z^0$, as shown in figure 6.28(b), is a popular example. The cross section is

$$
\sigma(e^+e^-\rightarrow HZ)=\frac{\pi\alpha^2}{24}\left(\frac{2\mechanical{K}}{\sqrt s}\right)\frac{(\mechanical{K}^2+3\mechanical{M_Z}^2)}{(s-\mechanical{M_Z}^2)^2+\mechanical{M_Z}^2\Gamma_Z^2}\frac{(1-4x_W+8x_W^2)}{x_W^2(1-x_W)^2}, \tag{6.5.14}
$$

where, as usual, $x_W=\sin^2\theta_W$ and $\mechanical{K}$ is the c.m. momentum of the emerging particles. At very high energies, for which $\mechanical{K}\rightarrow\sqrt s/2$, the ratio

$$
\frac{\sigma(e^+e^-\rightarrow HZ)}{\sigma(e^+e^-\rightarrow\mu^+\mu^-)}\rightarrow\frac{(1-4x_W+8x_W^2)}{128x_W^2(1-x_W)^2} \tag{6.5.15}
$$

approaches from below an asymptotic value of $0.142$ for $x_W=0.2122$. Other mechanisms for Higgs-boson production can be found in the resources cited at the end of this chapter, and we will revisit the search for the Higgs boson in section 7.9.
