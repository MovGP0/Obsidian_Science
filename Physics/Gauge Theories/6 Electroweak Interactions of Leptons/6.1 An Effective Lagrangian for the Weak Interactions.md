> [!TLDR]
> The four-fermion weak theory predicts lepton scattering and inverse muon decay, but violates unitarity at high energy.

<!-- PDF page 110; printed page 96 -->

Our contemporary view of the weak interactions is the result of a long evolution of the theoretical picture of radioactive $\beta$-decay introduced by Fermi [2] in 1933. The history of the subject up to the invention of spontaneously broken gauge theories and the low-energy phenomenology of nuclear $\beta$-decay, muon decay, and so on, are well documented in a number of excellent textbooks and monographs cited at the end of this chapter. We shall not reproduce this material but shall instead emphasize some aspects of the phenomenological theory that are not given prominence in the traditional textbook treatments. Our aims are to recall some calculational techniques, to point the way toward high-energy phenomena, and to expose the limitations of the historical approach.

We begin our exploration of weak-interaction phenomena with an investigation of neutrino–electron scattering. We shall briefly consider the implications of various possible spacetime forms for the charged-current interaction and then examine in greater detail the properties of the vector-minus-axial-vector structure favored by experiment.

The most general matrix element for the charged-current $\nu_e e$ interaction, free of derivative couplings, is

$$
\mathcal M=\sum_i\mathcal M_i=\sum_i C_i\bar\nu\mathcal O_i e\,\bar e\mathcal O_i(1-\gamma_5)\nu, \tag{6.1.1}
$$

where the operators $\mathcal O_i$ are

$$
\left.\begin{array}{lll}
\mathcal O_S=1&\quad&\text{scalar}\\
\mathcal O_P=\gamma_5&&\text{pseudoscalar}\\
\mathcal O_T=\sigma_{\mu\nu}&&\text{tensor}\\
\mathcal O_V=\gamma_\mu&&\text{vector}\\
\mathcal O_A=\gamma_\mu\gamma_5&&\text{axial vector}
\end{array}\right\}, \tag{6.1.2}
$$

For calculations, it will be convenient to employ the combinations $V\pm A$, which contribute incoherently to the absolute squares of matrix elements. Let us now compute the cross section that arises from each interaction in turn. Although much of this exercise is only of academic interest for the well-studied charged-current interactions, it will serve as an indication of what may be learned from scattering experiments. It will also be a useful reference when we turn to the study of neutral-current interactions or for applications to nonstandard neutrino interactions.

Denote the incoming and outgoing four-momenta as shown:

$$
\left.\begin{array}{ll}
\nu_{\mathrm{in}}:&\mechanical{q_1}\\
\nu_{\mathrm{out}}:&\mechanical{q_2}\\
e_{\mathrm{in}}:&\mechanical{p_1}\\
e_{\mathrm{out}}:&\mechanical{p_2}
\end{array}\right\}. \tag{6.1.3}
$$

In the laboratory frame, depicted in figure 6.1(a), the four-momenta are explicitly given by

$$
\left.\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{q_1^\mu}&=(\energy{E};0,0,\energy{E})\\
\mechanical{p_1^\mu}&=(\mechanical{m};0,0,0)\\
\mechanical{p_2^\mu}&=(\energy{E'};\mechanical{P'}\sin\theta_L,0,\mechanical{P'}\cos\theta_L)\\
\mechanical{q_2^\mu}&=(\energy{E}+\mechanical{m}-\energy{E'};-\mechanical{P'}\sin\theta_L,0,\energy{E}-\mechanical{P'}\cos\theta_L)
\end{aligned}\right\}, \tag{6.1.4}
$$

where the energy of the recoiling electron is

$$
\energy{E'}=\sqrt{\mechanical{P'}^2+\mechanical{m}^2}\equiv y\energy{E}, \tag{6.1.5}
$$

<!-- PDF page 112; printed page 98 -->

with $\mechanical{m}$ the electron mass. With these definitions, the invariants of interest may be expressed as

$$
\left.\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{q_1}&=\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{q_2}=\mechanical{m}\energy{E}\\
\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{q_2}&=\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{q_1}=\mechanical{m}(\energy{E}+\mechanical{m}-\energy{E'})\approx\mechanical{m}\energy{E}(1-y)\\
\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2}&=\mechanical{m}\energy{E'}=\mechanical{m}\energy{E}y\\
\mechanical{q_1}\cdot\mechanical{q_2}&=\mechanical{m}(\energy{E'}-\mechanical{m})\approx\mechanical{m}\energy{E}y
\end{aligned}\right\}. \tag{6.1.6}
$$

In the c.m. frame, indicated in figure 6.1(b), the four-momenta are conveniently written in terms of

$$
\mechanical{p^*}=\frac{s-\mechanical{m}^2}{2\sqrt s}=\frac{\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{\sqrt s}\approx\frac{\sqrt s}2 \tag{6.1.7}
$$

and

$$
\energy{\omega^*}=\sqrt{\mechanical{p^*}^2+\mechanical{m}^2}=\frac{s+\mechanical{m}^2}{2\sqrt s} \tag{6.1.8}
$$

as

$$
\left.\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{q_1^\mu}&=(\mechanical{p^*};0,0,\mechanical{p^*})\\
\mechanical{p_1^\mu}&=(\energy{\omega^*};0,0,-\mechanical{p^*})\\
\mechanical{p_2^\mu}&=(\energy{\omega^*};\mechanical{p^*}\sin\theta,0,\mechanical{p^*}\cos\theta)\\
\mechanical{q_2^\mu}&=(\mechanical{p^*};-\mechanical{p^*}\sin\theta,0,-\mechanical{p^*}\cos\theta)
\end{aligned}\right\}. \tag{6.1.9}
$$

The invariants are then given by

$$
\left.\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{q_1}&=\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{q_2}=\mechanical{p^*}(\mechanical{p^*}+\energy{\omega^*})\approx2\mechanical{p^*}^2\\
\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{q_2}&=\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{q_1}=\mechanical{p^*}(\energy{\omega^*}-\mechanical{p^*}\cos\theta)\approx\mechanical{p^*}^2(1-\cos\theta)\\
\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2}&=\energy{\omega^*}^2+\mechanical{p^*}^2\cos\theta\approx\mechanical{p^*}^2(1+\cos\theta)\\
\mechanical{q_1}\cdot\mechanical{q_2}&=\mechanical{p^*}^2(1+\cos\theta)
\end{aligned}\right\}. \tag{6.1.10}
$$

We now compute the differential and integral cross sections for the various interaction terms contained in (6.1.1).

1. Scalar interaction: $\nu_e e\rightarrow\nu_e e$. The matrix element is

$$
\mathcal M=\bar u(\nu,\mechanical{q_2})u(e,\mechanical{p_1})\bar u(e,\mechanical{p_2})(1-\gamma_5)u(\nu,\mechanical{q_1}). \tag{6.1.11}
$$

Squaring the matrix element and summing over the spins of the outgoing particles, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\mathcal M|^2&=\operatorname{tr}[u(e,\mechanical{p_1})\bar u(e,\mechanical{p_1})u(\nu,\mechanical{q_2})\bar u(\nu,\mechanical{q_2})]\\
&\quad\times\operatorname{tr}[(1-\gamma_5)u(\nu,\mechanical{q_1})\bar u(\nu,\mechanical{q_1})(1+\gamma_5)u(e,\mechanical{p_2})\bar u(e,\mechanical{p_2})]\\
&=\operatorname{tr}[(\mechanical{m}+\not{\mechanical{p_1}})\not{\mechanical{q_2}}]\operatorname{tr}[(1-\gamma_5)\not{\mechanical{q_1}}(1+\gamma_5)(\mechanical{m}+\not{\mechanical{p_2}})].
\end{aligned} \tag{6.1.12}
$$

<!-- PDF page 113; printed page 99 -->

The first trace is simply

$$
\operatorname{tr}[(\mechanical{m}+\not{\mechanical{p_1}})\not{\mechanical{q_2}}]=4\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{q_2}, \tag{6.1.13}
$$

by virtue of (A.3.3) and (A.3.5b). The second is conveniently rearranged as

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{tr}[(1-\gamma_5)\not{\mechanical{q_1}}(1+\gamma_5)(\mechanical{m}+\not{\mechanical{p_2}})]
&=2\operatorname{tr}[(1-\gamma_5)\not{\mechanical{q_1}}(\mechanical{m}+\not{\mechanical{p_2}})]\\
&=8\mechanical{q_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2},
\end{aligned} \tag{6.1.14}
$$

where (A.3.3), (A.3.5b), (A.3.8), and (A.3.9) have been used. We therefore have $|\mathcal M|^2=32\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{q_2}\,\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{q_1}$, or, averaging over the spin states of the incident electron,

$$
\overline{|\mathcal M|^2}=16\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{q_2}\,\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{q_1}=16\mechanical{p^*}^4(1-\cos\theta)^2. \tag{6.1.15}
$$

The differential cross section in the c.m. frame is easily computed with the aid of (B.1.7) as

$$
\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega_{\mathrm{c.m.}}}=\frac{\overline{|\mathcal M|^2}}{64\pi^2s}=\frac{\mechanical{m}\energy{E}(1-\cos\theta)^2}{32\pi^2}, \tag{6.1.16}
$$

so that

$$
\sigma=\int d\Omega_{\mathrm{c.m.}}\left(\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega_{\mathrm{c.m.}}}\right)
=\frac{\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{16\pi}\int_{-1}^1dz(1-z)^2=\frac{\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{6\pi}. \tag{6.1.17}
$$

Comparing (6.1.6) and (6.1.10), we find that

$$
(1-\cos\theta)=2(1-y). \tag{6.1.18}
$$

Consequently we may write the differential cross section in the useful form

$$
\frac{d\sigma}{dy}=4\pi\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega_{\mathrm{c.m.}}}=\frac{\mechanical{m}\energy{E}(1-y)^2}{2\pi}, \tag{6.1.19}
$$

which leads, of course, to the same integrated cross section,

$$
\sigma=\int_0^1dy\frac{d\sigma}{dy}=\frac{\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{6\pi}. \tag{6.1.20}
$$

2. Scalar interaction: $\bar\nu_e e\rightarrow\bar\nu_e e$. The matrix element is

$$
\mathcal M=\bar v(\nu,\mechanical{q_1})u(e,\mechanical{p_1})\bar u(e,\mechanical{p_2})(1-\gamma_5)v(\nu,\mechanical{q_2}), \tag{6.1.21}
$$

<!-- PDF page 114; printed page 100 -->

so that

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\mathcal M|^2&=\operatorname{tr}[u(e,\mechanical{p_1})\bar u(e,\mechanical{p_1})v(\nu,\mechanical{q_1})\bar v(\nu,\mechanical{q_1})]\\
&\quad\times\operatorname{tr}[(1-\gamma_5)v(\nu,\mechanical{q_2})\bar v(\nu,\mechanical{q_2})(1+\gamma_5)u(e,\mechanical{p_2})\bar u(e,\mechanical{p_2})]\\
&=\operatorname{tr}[(\mechanical{m}+\not{\mechanical{p_1}})\not{\mechanical{q_1}}]\operatorname{tr}[(1-\gamma_5)\not{\mechanical{q_2}}(1+\gamma_5)(\mechanical{m}+\not{\mechanical{p_2}})],
\end{aligned} \tag{6.1.22}
$$

which may be obtained from the matrix element squared for $\nu_e e$ scattering by interchanging $\mechanical{q_1}$ and $\mechanical{q_2}$. As a result, we find

$$
\overline{|\mathcal M|^2}=16\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{q_1}\,\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{q_2}, \tag{6.1.23}
$$

so that

$$
\frac{d\sigma}{dy}=\frac{\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{2\pi}, \tag{6.1.24}
$$

and

$$
\sigma=\frac{\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{2\pi}. \tag{6.1.25}
$$

It is straightforward to verify that the pseudoscalar interaction yields the same cross sections.

3. Tensor coupling: $\nu_e e\rightarrow\nu_e e$. The matrix element is

$$
\mathcal M=\bar u(\nu,\mechanical{q_2})\sigma_{\mu\nu}u(e,\mechanical{p_1})\bar u(e,\mechanical{p_2})\sigma^{\mu\nu}(1-\gamma_5)u(\nu,\mechanical{q_1}), \tag{6.1.26}
$$

so we are required to evaluate

$$
|\mathcal M|^2=\operatorname{tr}[\sigma_{\mu\nu}(\mechanical{m}+\not{\mechanical{p_1}})\sigma_{\kappa\lambda}\not{\mechanical{q_2}}]
\operatorname{tr}[\sigma^{\mu\nu}(1-\gamma_5)\not{\mechanical{q_1}}(1+\gamma_5)\sigma^{\kappa\lambda}(\mechanical{m}+\not{\mechanical{p_2}})]. \tag{6.1.27}
$$

To evaluate these traces, it is convenient to exploit the identity

$$
\sigma^{\mu\nu}=i(\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu-g^{\mu\nu}) \tag{A.2.14}
$$

and to note that, by virtue of the anticommutation relations (A.2.1), a product of six $\gamma$-matrices may be simplified as

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{tr}(\not a\not b\not c\not d\not e\not f)
&=a\cdot b\,\operatorname{tr}(\not c\not d\not e\not f)-a\cdot c\,\operatorname{tr}(\not b\not d\not e\not f)+a\cdot d\,\operatorname{tr}(\not b\not c\not e\not f)\\
&\quad-a\cdot e\,\operatorname{tr}(\not b\not c\not d\not f)+a\cdot f\,\operatorname{tr}(\not b\not c\not d\not e).
\end{aligned} \tag{6.1.28}
$$

A slightly tedious calculation then yields

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\mathcal M|^2&=256(2\mechanical{q_1}\cdot\mechanical{q_2}\,\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2}
+2\mechanical{q_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_1}\,\mechanical{q_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_2}
-\mechanical{q_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2}\,\mechanical{q_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_1})\\
&=256(\mechanical{m}\energy{E})^2(1+y)^2,
\end{aligned} \tag{6.1.29}
$$

so that

$$
\frac{d\sigma}{dy}=\frac{4\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{\pi}(1+y)^2 \tag{6.1.30}
$$

and

$$
\sigma=\frac{28\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{3\pi}. \tag{6.1.31}
$$

4. Tensor coupling: $\bar\nu_e e\rightarrow\bar\nu_e e$. Again, the result may be obtained by interchanging $\mechanical{q_1}$ and $\mechanical{q_2}$. We have

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\mathcal M|^2&=256(2\mechanical{q_1}\cdot\mechanical{q_2}\,\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2}
+2\mechanical{q_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_1}\,\mechanical{q_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2}
-\mechanical{q_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_2}\,\mechanical{q_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_1})\\
&=256(\mechanical{m}\energy{E})^2(1-2y)^2,
\end{aligned} \tag{6.1.32}
$$

from which

$$
\frac{d\sigma}{dy}=\frac{4\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{\pi}(1-2y)^2 \tag{6.1.33}
$$

and

$$
\sigma=\frac{4\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{3\pi}. \tag{6.1.34}
$$

5. S − T interference: $\nu_e e\rightarrow\nu_e e$. The matrix element is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal M&=C_S\bar u(\nu,\mechanical{q_2})u(e,\mechanical{p_1})\bar u(e,\mechanical{p_2})(1-\gamma_5)u(\nu,\mechanical{q_1})\\
&\quad+C_T\bar u(\nu,\mechanical{q_2})\sigma_{\mu\nu}u(e,\mechanical{p_1})\bar u(e,\mechanical{p_2})\sigma^{\mu\nu}(1-\gamma_5)u(\nu,\mechanical{q_1}),
\end{aligned} \tag{6.1.35}
$$

so the form of the interference terms is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal I&=C_SC_T^*\operatorname{tr}[\bar u(\nu,\mechanical{q_2})u(e,\mechanical{p_1})\bar u(e,\mechanical{p_1})\sigma_{\mu\nu}u(\nu,\mechanical{q_2})]\\
&\quad\times\operatorname{tr}[\bar u(e,\mechanical{p_2})(1-\gamma_5)u(\nu,\mechanical{q_1})\bar u(\nu,\mechanical{q_1})(1+\gamma_5)\sigma^{\mu\nu}u(\nu,\mechanical{q_2})]\\
&\quad+C_S^*C_T\operatorname{tr}[\bar u(\nu,\mechanical{q_2})\sigma_{\mu\nu}u(e,\mechanical{p_1})\bar u(e,\mechanical{p_1})u(\nu,\mechanical{q_2})]\\
&\quad\times\operatorname{tr}[\bar u(e,\mechanical{p_2})\sigma^{\mu\nu}(1-\gamma_5)u(\nu,\mechanical{q_1})\bar u(\nu,\mechanical{q_1})(1+\gamma_5)u(e,\mechanical{p_2})]\\
&=C_SC_T^*\operatorname{tr}[(\mechanical{m}+\not{\mechanical{p_1}})\sigma_{\mu\nu}\not{\mechanical{q_2}}]\operatorname{tr}[(1-\gamma_5)\not{\mechanical{q_1}}(1+\gamma_5)\sigma^{\mu\nu}(\mechanical{m}+\not{\mechanical{p_2}})]\\
&\quad+C_S^*C_T\operatorname{tr}[\sigma_{\mu\nu}(\mechanical{m}+\not{\mechanical{p_1}})\not{\mechanical{q_2}}]\operatorname{tr}[\sigma^{\mu\nu}(1-\gamma_5)\not{\mechanical{q_1}}(1+\gamma_5)(\mechanical{m}+\not{\mechanical{p_2}})]\\
&=2C_SC_T^*\operatorname{tr}[\sigma_{\mu\nu}\not{\mechanical{q_2}}(\mechanical{m}+\not{\mechanical{p_1}})]\operatorname{tr}[(1+\gamma_5)\sigma^{\mu\nu}(\mechanical{m}+\not{\mechanical{p_2}})\not{\mechanical{q_1}}]\\
&\quad+2C_S^*C_T\operatorname{tr}[\sigma_{\mu\nu}(\mechanical{m}+\not{\mechanical{p_1}})\not{\mechanical{q_2}}]\operatorname{tr}[(1-\gamma_5)\sigma^{\mu\nu}\not{\mechanical{q_1}}(\mechanical{m}+\not{\mechanical{p_2}})].
\end{aligned} \tag{6.1.36}
$$

<!-- PDF page 116; printed page 102 -->

The commutation relations show the two products of traces to be equal. Again using the identity (A.2.14), we easily compute

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal I&=-128\operatorname{Re}(C_S^*C_T)(\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{q_2}\,\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{q_1}-\mechanical{q_1}\cdot\mechanical{q_2}\,\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2})\\
&=-128\operatorname{Re}(C_S^*C_T)(\mechanical{m}\energy{E})^2(1-y^2),
\end{aligned} \tag{6.1.37}
$$

so that

$$
\frac{d\sigma_{ST}}{dy}=-\frac{2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{\pi}(1-y^2)\operatorname{Re}(C_S^*C_T), \tag{6.1.38}
$$

and

$$
\sigma_{ST}=-\frac{4\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{3\pi}\operatorname{Re}(C_S^*C_T). \tag{6.1.39}
$$

The cross section corresponding to an interference term need not, of course, be positive definite. A parallel calculation shows that the same result is obtained for P − T interference.

6. S − T interference: $\bar\nu_e e\rightarrow\bar\nu_e e$. On interchanging $\mechanical{q_1}\leftrightarrow\mechanical{q_2}$ in (6.1.37), we find

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal I&=-128\operatorname{Re}(C_S^*C_T)(\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{q_1}\,\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{q_2}-\mechanical{q_1}\cdot\mechanical{q_2}\,\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2})\\
&=-128\operatorname{Re}(C_S^*C_T)(\mechanical{m}\energy{E})^2(1-2y),
\end{aligned} \tag{6.1.40}
$$

from which

$$
\frac{d\sigma_{ST}}{dy}=\frac{2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{\pi}(1-2y)\operatorname{Re}(C_S^*C_T) \tag{6.1.41}
$$

and

$$
\sigma_{ST}=0. \tag{6.1.42}
$$

7. V − A interaction: $\nu_e e\rightarrow\nu_e e$. The matrix element is

$$
\mathcal M=\bar u(\nu,\mechanical{q_2})\gamma_\mu(1-\gamma_5)u(e,\mechanical{p_1})\bar u(e,\mechanical{p_2})\gamma^\mu(1-\gamma_5)u(\nu,\mechanical{q_1}). \tag{6.1.43}
$$

Squaring the matrix element and summing over the spins of the outgoing particles, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\mathcal M|^2&=\operatorname{tr}[\gamma_\mu(1-\gamma_5)(\mechanical{m}+\not{\mechanical{p_1}})(1+\gamma_5)\gamma_\nu\not{\mechanical{q_2}}]\\
&\quad\times\operatorname{tr}[\gamma^\mu(1-\gamma_5)\not{\mechanical{q_1}}(1+\gamma_5)\gamma^\nu(\mechanical{m}+\not{\mechanical{p_2}})]\\
&\equiv A_{\mu\nu}B^{\mu\nu}.
\end{aligned} \tag{6.1.44}
$$

The first factor is then

$$
\begin{aligned}
A_{\mu\nu}&=2\operatorname{tr}[(1+\gamma_5)\gamma_\nu\not{\mechanical{q_2}}\gamma_\mu(\mechanical{m}+\not{\mechanical{p_1}})]\\
&=8(\mechanical{q_{2\nu}}\mechanical{p_{1\mu}}-g_{\mu\nu}\mechanical{q_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_1}+\mechanical{q_{2\mu}}\mechanical{p_{1\nu}})-8i\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\mechanical{q_2^\rho}\mechanical{p_1^\sigma},
\end{aligned} \tag{6.1.45}
$$

<!-- PDF page 117; printed page 103 -->

and the second is

$$
\begin{aligned}
B^{\mu\nu}&=2\operatorname{tr}[(1-\gamma_5)\not{\mechanical{q_1}}\gamma^\nu(\mechanical{m}+\not{\mechanical{p_2}})\gamma^\mu]\\
&=8(\mechanical{q_1^\nu}\mechanical{p_2^\mu}-g^{\mu\nu}\mechanical{q_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2}+\mechanical{q_1^\mu}\mechanical{p_2^\nu})+8i\epsilon^{\mu\nu\kappa\lambda}\mechanical{q_{1\kappa}}\mechanical{p_{2\lambda}}.
\end{aligned} \tag{6.1.46}
$$

As a consequence,

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\mathcal M|^2&=A_{\mu\nu}B^{\mu\nu}=128(\mechanical{q_1}\cdot\mechanical{q_2}\,\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2}+\mechanical{q_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_1}\,\mechanical{q_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_2})\\
&\quad-64i\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\mechanical{q_2^\rho}\mechanical{p_1^\sigma}(\mechanical{q_1^\nu}\mechanical{p_2^\mu}+\mechanical{q_1^\mu}\mechanical{p_2^\nu})\\
&\quad+64i\epsilon^{\mu\nu\kappa\lambda}\mechanical{q_{1\kappa}}\mechanical{p_{2\lambda}}(\mechanical{q_{2\nu}}\mechanical{p_{1\mu}}+\mechanical{q_{2\mu}}\mechanical{p_{1\nu}})\\
&\quad+64\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\kappa\lambda}\mechanical{q_2^\rho}\mechanical{p_1^\sigma}\mechanical{q_{1\kappa}}\mechanical{p_{2\lambda}}.
\end{aligned} \tag{6.1.47}
$$

The second and third terms vanish because there are only three independent momenta in the problem. The last term may be evaluated with the aid of (A.3.14); it is simply

$$
128(\mechanical{q_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_1}\,\mechanical{q_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_2}-\mechanical{q_1}\cdot\mechanical{q_2}\,\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2}), \tag{6.1.48}
$$

so that

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\mathcal M|^2&=256\mechanical{q_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_1}\,\mechanical{q_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_2}\\
&=256(\mechanical{m}\energy{E})^2,
\end{aligned} \tag{6.1.49}
$$

whereupon

$$
\frac{d\sigma}{dy}=\frac{4\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{\pi} \tag{6.1.50}
$$

and

$$
\sigma=\frac{4\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{\pi}. \tag{6.1.51}
$$

8. V − A interaction: $\bar\nu_e e\rightarrow\bar\nu_e e$. The replacement $\mechanical{q_1}\leftrightarrow\mechanical{q_2}$ yields

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\mathcal M|^2&=256\mechanical{q_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_1}\,\mechanical{q_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2}\\
&=256(\mechanical{m}\energy{E})^2(1-y)^2,
\end{aligned} \tag{6.1.52}
$$

from which

$$
\frac{d\sigma}{dy}=\frac{4\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{\pi}(1-y)^2 \tag{6.1.53}
$$

and

$$
\sigma=\frac{4\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{3\pi}. \tag{6.1.54}
$$

9. V + A interaction: $\nu_e e\rightarrow\nu_e e$. We write the matrix element as

$$
\mathcal M=\bar u(\nu,\mechanical{q_2})\gamma_\mu(1+\gamma_5)u(e,\mechanical{p_1})\bar u(e,\mechanical{p_2})\gamma^\mu(1-\gamma_5)u(\nu,\mechanical{q_1}). \tag{6.1.55}
$$

The calculation differs from the V − A calculation only by a change in the sign of the $\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$ term. Thus, we find that

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\mathcal M|^2&=256\mechanical{q_1}\cdot\mechanical{q_2}\,\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2}\\
&=256(\mechanical{m}\energy{E})^2y^2,
\end{aligned} \tag{6.1.56}
$$

which implies

$$
\frac{d\sigma}{dy}=\frac{4\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{\pi}y^2 \tag{6.1.57}
$$

and

$$
\sigma=\frac{4\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{3\pi}. \tag{6.1.58}
$$

10. V + A interaction: $\bar\nu_e e\rightarrow\nu_e e$. The preceding results are unchanged by the interchange $\mechanical{q_1}\leftrightarrow\mechanical{q_2}$, so we have at once

$$
\frac{d\sigma}{dy}=\frac{4\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{\pi}y^2 \tag{6.1.59}
$$

and

$$
\sigma=\frac{4\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{3\pi}. \tag{6.1.60}
$$

The foregoing calculations of

$$
\frac{d\sigma}{dy}=\frac{d\sigma}{dy}(STP)+\frac{d\sigma}{dy}(V-A)+\frac{d\sigma}{dy}(V+A) \tag{6.1.61}
$$

are summarized in table 6.1. We see that energy-loss distributions (or, equivalently, angular distributions) provide a means of probing the spacetime structure of the interaction. It is, however, easy to verify the following “confusion theorem” [3]: for the pair of spin-averaged $(\nu,\bar\nu)$ cross sections, any combination of V − A and V + A interactions can be reproduced by a suitably chosen combination of S, T, and P interactions. Spin dependences are, in general, different for the two sets of interactions.

It is interesting to analyze the reason for the factor-of-three difference between neutrino and antineutrino cross sections or, equivalently, the difference in angular distributions plotted in figure 6.2. It is simply this: the operators $(1\mp\gamma_5)$ are spin-projection operators in the limit of vanishing fermion mass. Thus, for a V − A interaction, we may view all light fermions as left-handed (with helicity $-\frac12$) and all antifermions as right-handed (with helicity $+\frac12$). Thus, as shown in figure 6.3(a), the initial state is a state of $\mechanical{J_z}=0$ for $\nu_e e$ scattering and $\mechanical{J_z}=1$ for $\bar\nu_e e$ scattering. The same holds for forward scattering (which we have labeled by $\cos\theta=-1$, $y=0$).

**Fig. 6.2** Angular and energy-loss distributions of $\nu_e e$ and $\bar\nu_e e$ scattering in the V − A model.

![[gauge-theories-fig-6-3-scattering-spins.svg|**Fig. 6.3** Spin configurations for $\nu_e e$ and $\bar\nu_e e$ scattering in the c.m. frame: (a) initial state; (b) final state for backward scattering.]]

For backward scattering (which we have described by $\cos\theta=+1$, $y=1$), the situation is different, as shown in figure 6.3(b). The final state for $\nu_e e$ scattering is a state with total spin $\mechanical{J_z}=0$, as was the initial state, but the final state for $\bar\nu_e e$ scattering would correspond to $\mechanical{J_z}=-1$, which would violate angular momentum conservation. Backward $\bar\nu_e e$ scattering is, therefore, forbidden, and the cross section must vanish at $y=1$. This effect will occur many times in our study of weak interactions.

One of the important observations of early experiments, which has been repeatedly confirmed and made more precise, is that the charged-current weak interactions are universal in strength: the same coupling constant applies to all leptonic and semileptonic interactions. As a consequence, the calculations we have just completed may be extended in straightforward fashion to the experimentally interesting case of inverse muon decay, the reaction

$$
\nu_\mu(\mechanical{q_1})e(\mechanical{p_1})\rightarrow\mu(\mechanical{p_2})\nu_e(\mechanical{q_2}). \tag{6.1.69}
$$

<!-- PDF page 121; printed page 107 -->

Kinematic definitions in the laboratory frame are as in the case of $\nu_e e$ elastic scattering (6.1.4), except that the final muon energy is

$$
\energy{E'}=\sqrt{\mechanical{P'}^2+\mechanical{\mu}^2}, \tag{6.1.70}
$$

where $\mechanical{\mu}$ is the muon mass. The invariants (6.1.6) become

$$
\left.\begin{aligned}
\mechanical{q_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_1}&=\mechanical{m}e\\
\mechanical{q_2}\cdot\mechanical{p_2}&=\mechanical{m}\energy{E}-\frac{\mechanical{\mu}^2-\mechanical{m}^2}{2}\\
\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{p_2}&=\mechanical{m}\energy{E}\\
\mechanical{p_1}\cdot\mechanical{q_2}&=\mechanical{m}(\energy{E}+\mechanical{m}-\energy{E'})\\
\mechanical{q_1}\cdot\mechanical{q_2}&=\mechanical{m}\energy{E'}-\frac{\mechanical{\mu}^2+\mechanical{m}^2}{2}\\
\mechanical{p_2}\cdot\mechanical{q_1}&=\mechanical{m}(\energy{E}-\energy{E'})+\frac{\mechanical{\mu}^2+\mechanical{m}^2}{2}
\end{aligned}\right\}. \tag{6.1.71}
$$

The differential cross section is now

$$
\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega_{\mathrm{c.m.}}}=\frac{\overline{|\mathcal M|^2}}{64\pi^2s}\frac{\mechanical{p'_{\mathrm{c.m.}}}}{\mechanical{p_{\mathrm{c.m.}}}}
=\frac{\overline{|\mathcal M|^2}}{64\pi^2s}\left[1-\frac{(\mechanical{\mu}^2-\mechanical{m}^2)}{2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}\right]. \tag{6.1.72}
$$

Substitution of the matrix element [cf. (6.1.49)] yields

$$
\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega_{\mathrm{c.m.}}}=\frac{G_F^2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{2\pi^2}\left[1-\frac{(\mechanical{\mu}^2-\mechanical{m}^2)}{2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}\right]^2 \tag{6.1.73}
$$

and, therefore,

$$
\sigma(\nu_\mu e\rightarrow\mu\nu_e)=\frac{2G_F^2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}{\pi}\left[1-\frac{(\mechanical{\mu}^2-\mechanical{m}^2)}{2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}\right]^2 \tag{6.1.74}
$$

$$
=\sigma_{V-A}(\nu_e e\rightarrow\nu_e e)\left[1-\frac{(\mechanical{\mu}^2-\mechanical{m}^2)}{2\mechanical{m}\energy{E}}\right]^2. \tag{6.1.75}
$$

The expected total cross section is plotted in figure 6.4. Because of its high threshold energy $(\energy{E}\approx10.9\,\unit{GeV})$, the reaction has been difficult to observe and has been studied only recently. From a signal of $15,758\pm324$ events, the CHARM-II collaboration [5] at CERN determined the asymptotic slope,

$$
\sigma_\infty(\nu_\mu e\rightarrow\mu\nu_e)/\energy{E}=(1.651\pm0.093)\times10^{-41}\,\unit{cm^2\,GeV^{-1}}, \tag{6.1.76}
$$

Inverse muon decay is a particularly interesting process for experimental study because it is purely leptonic and, thus, free from some of the ambiguities that will present themselves in the case of semileptonic interactions. The small cross section was an early impediment to detailed study. However, the reaction is also well suited to exposing the limitations of the effective Lagrangian approach. The angular distribution is, according to (6.1.73), isotropic in the c.m. system, which is to say that the scattering is purely $s$-wave. Partial-wave unitarity constrains the modulus of an inelastic partial-wave amplitude to be

$$
|\mathcal M_J|\le1, \tag{6.1.79}
$$

where the partial-wave expansion for the scattering amplitude (here written for spinless external particles) is

$$
f(\theta)=\left(2\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\right)^{1/2}=\frac1{\sqrt s}\sum_{J=0}^\infty(2J+1)P_J(\cos\theta)\mathcal M_J, \tag{6.1.80}
$$

with $P_J(x)$ a Legendre polynomial. The explicit factor of two in (6.1.80) serves to undo the average over initial electron spins. The constraint (6.1.79) is equivalent to

<!-- PDF page 123; printed page 109 -->

the familiar restriction

$$
\sigma\le\frac\pi{\mechanical{p_{\mathrm{c.m.}}}^2} \tag{6.1.81}
$$

for inelastic $s$-wave scattering. For inverse muon decay, we easily compute

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal M_0&=\frac{G_Fs}{\pi\sqrt2}\left[1-\frac{(\mechanical{\mu}^2-\mechanical{m}^2)}s\right]\\
&\xrightarrow{s\gg\mechanical{\mu}^2}\frac{G_Fs}{\pi\sqrt2},
\end{aligned} \tag{6.1.82}
$$

which implies the unitarity constraint,

$$
\frac{G_Fs}{\pi\sqrt2}\le1. \tag{6.1.83}
$$

This means that the four-fermion theory can make sense only if [7]

$$
s\le\frac{\pi\sqrt2}{G_F}, \tag{6.1.84}
$$

which is to say that

$$
\sqrt s\le617\,\unit{GeV} \tag{6.1.85}
$$

or

$$
\mechanical{p_{\mathrm{c.m.}}}\le309\,\unit{GeV}. \tag{6.1.86}
$$

Although the energy at which unitarity is violated by the point-coupling theory is elevated, it is not astronomical. We must, therefore, take seriously the objection that the effective point-coupling theory is unacceptable, as we have formulated it. Suppose now that (6.1.62) is taken as a true Lagrangian and not merely an effective one. Could the violation of unitarity that we have just exhibited be remedied in higher orders of perturbation theory? In second order we must compute the diagram shown in figure 6.5, for which the matrix element is readily seen to be

$$
\mathcal M\propto\int\frac{d^4\mechanical{k}}{\mechanical{k}^2}, \tag{6.1.87}
$$

which is quadratically divergent. In fact, the divergence difficulty of the point-coupling theory grows more severe in each order of perturbation theory. In order to be saved—and this is evidently desirable because of its successes for low-energy phenomenology, the theory must be modified in a fundamental way. The simplest attempts to salvage the theory are the subject of the next section.
