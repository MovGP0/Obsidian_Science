> Stationary action gives the Euler–Lagrange equations for particle coordinates and continuous fields.

The Lagrangian function $\energy{L}$ may be regarded as the fundamental object in classical mechanics. [2] From it may be constructed the classical action

$$
\energy{S}\equiv\int_{t_1}^{t_2}dt\,\energy{L}(\mechanical{q},\mechanical{\dot q}), \tag{2.1.1}
$$

where $\mechanical{q}(t)$ is the generalized coordinate and $\mechanical{\dot q}(t)$, the generalized velocity. The equations of motion follow from Hamilton’s principle of least action, according to which the variation

$$
\delta\energy{S}=\delta\int_{t_1}^{t_2}dt\,\energy{L}(\mechanical{q},\mechanical{\dot q})=0, \tag{2.1.2}
$$

subject to the condition that variations of the generalized coordinates vanish at the endpoints $t_1$ and $t_2$. Thus the physical path is the particular trajectory joining $\mechanical{q_1}\equiv\mechanical{q_1}(t)$ and $\mechanical{q_2}\equiv\mechanical{q_2}(t)$, along which the action is stationary. An important generalization to quantum mechanics as a weighted sum over paths has been developed by Feynman [3].

In a large number of problems of physical interest, the Lagrangian depends only upon the generalized coordinates and their first derivatives. In this case, satisfaction of Hamilton’s principle is guaranteed by the Euler–Lagrange equations in the form

$$
\frac{\partial\energy{L}}{\partial\mechanical{q}}=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial\energy{L}}{\partial\mechanical{\dot q}}\right). \tag{2.1.3}
$$

To show this, let us note that the variation in the action is given by

$$
\delta\energy{S}=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}dt\left[
\frac{\partial\energy{L}}{\partial\mechanical{q}(t)}\delta\mechanical{q}(t)+
\frac{\partial\energy{L}}{\partial\mechanical{\dot q}(t)}\delta\mechanical{\dot q}(t)\right]. \tag{2.1.4}
$$

Because $\delta\mechanical{\dot q}(t)=(d/dt)\delta\mechanical{q}(t)$, the second term may be integrated by parts:

$$
\int_{t_1}^{t_2}dt\,\frac{\partial\energy{L}}{\partial\mechanical{\dot q}}\cdot\frac{d}{dt}\delta\mechanical{q}
=\left.\frac{\partial\energy{L}}{\partial\mechanical{\dot q}}\delta\mechanical{q}\right|_{t_1}^{t_2}
-\int_{t_1}^{t_2}dt\left(\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\energy{L}}{\partial\mechanical{\dot q}}\right)\delta\mechanical{q}. \tag{2.1.5}
$$

Because the endpoints are constrained,

$$
\delta\mechanical{q}(t_1)=0=\delta\mechanical{q}(t_2), \tag{2.1.6}
$$

<!-- PDF page 41; printed page 27 -->

the first term in (2.1.5) is identically zero, and the variation of the action becomes

$$
\delta\energy{S}=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}dt\left(\frac{\partial\energy{L}}{\partial\mechanical{q}}-
\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\energy{L}}{\partial\mechanical{\dot q}}\right)\delta\mechanical{q}, \tag{2.1.7}
$$

which vanishes provided that (2.1.3) holds. For the case of several (separately varied) generalized coordinates the same arithmetic leads to a set of Euler–Lagrange equations in the form (2.1.3), one for each coordinate.

The most familiar cases in classical mechanics are those for which the Lagrangian is simply the difference of kinetic and potential energies. Indeed, if we write in one dimension

$$
\energy{L}=\frac12\mechanical{m}\mechanical{\dot x}^2-\energy{V}(\mechanical{x}), \tag{2.1.8}
$$

the Euler–Lagrange equation gives

$$
-\frac{d\energy{V}}{d\mechanical{x}}=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{\ddot x}. \tag{2.1.9}
$$

Upon identifying the applied force as

$$
\mechanical{F}\equiv-\frac{d\energy{V}}{d\mechanical{x}}, \tag{2.1.10}
$$

we simply recover Newton’s equation of motion (his second law).

The formalism can easily be generalized to include a Lagrangian that depends explicitly on the time $t$ or some other parameter. This is appropriate for an “open” system, which exchanges energy and momentum with its surroundings. A frequently encountered case is that of an external driving force $\mechanical{F}(t)$, represented in the Lagrangian by a term $\mechanical{q}\mechanical{F}(t)$. The mathematical formalism can also be extended to treat Lagrangians that contain higher than first-order derivatives of the generalized coordinates. The ensuing Euler–Lagrange equations of motion are then higher than second order, but a “local” description in terms of local differential equations remains possible as long as the Lagrangian contains only derivatives of finite order. These generalizations will not be required for the study of gauge theories.

With the Lagrangian description of classical mechanics in hand, the transition to Lagrangian field theory, guided by the requirement of relativistic invariance, is straightforward. Construction of a field theory begins from the Lagrangian density $\energy{\mathcal L}(\phi(x),\partial_\mu\phi(x))$, a functional of the field $\phi(x)$ and its four-gradient $\partial_\mu\phi(x)=\partial\phi(x)/\partial x^\mu$. The field may be regarded as a separate, generalized coordinate at each value of its argument, the spacetime coordinate $x$. Thus arises the description of field theory as a closed system with an infinite number of degrees of freedom.

The classical action is now defined as

$$
\energy{S}\equiv\int_{t_1}^{t_2}dt\int d^3x\,\energy{\mathcal L}(\phi(x),\partial_\mu\phi(x)), \tag{2.1.11}
$$

where the spatial integral of the Lagrangian density takes the part of the Lagrangian in classical mechanics:

$$
\energy{L}\equiv\int d^3x\,\energy{\mathcal L}(\phi(x),\partial_\mu\phi(x)). \tag{2.1.12}
$$

By the same reasoning followed in the classical problem, the action is required to be stationary,

$$
\delta\int_{t_1}^{t_2}dt\int d^3x\,\energy{\mathcal L}(\phi(x),\partial_\mu\phi(x))=0, \tag{2.1.13}
$$

subject as always to the constraint that the variations in the fields vanish at the endpoints characterized by $t_1$ and $t_2$. The requirement of least action is now ensured by the Euler–Lagrange equations for the Lagrangian density,

$$
\frac{\partial\energy{\mathcal L}}{\partial\phi(x)}=
\partial_\mu\frac{\partial\energy{\mathcal L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi(x))}, \tag{2.1.14}
$$

which in turn lead to explicit equations of motion for the fields. Evidently these equations will be covariant if the Lagrangian density itself transforms as a Lorentz scalar. Moreover, the equations of motion are unchanged if a total divergence is added to the Lagrangian density. For problems involving several fields $\phi_i$, the variational principle applied separately to each field leads to a set of equations (2.1.14), precisely as for mechanical problems with several generalized coordinates.

In what follows, it will frequently be convenient to refer in context to the Lagrangian density $\energy{\mathcal L}$ by the shorter term Lagrangian.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\energy{L}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Lagrangian | Kinetic energy minus potential energy in classical mechanics. |
| $\energy{S}$ | $\unit{J\,s}$ | Action | The time integral of the Lagrangian. |
| $t,t_1,t_2$ | $\unit{s}$ | Time | The time variable and the fixed endpoints. |
| $\mechanical{q},\mechanical{q_1},\mechanical{q_2}$ | — | Generalized coordinates | Coordinate units depend on the mechanical system. |
| $\mechanical{\dot q}$ | — | Generalized velocity | The time derivative of a generalized coordinate. |

| $\mechanical{\dot x},\mechanical{\ddot x}$ | $\unit{m\,s^{-1}}$ and $\unit{m\,s^{-2}}$ | Velocity and acceleration | The first and second time derivatives of position. |
| $\mechanical{m}$ | $\unit{kg}$ | Mass | The particle mass in the classical example. |
| $\energy{V}$ | $\unit{J}$ | Potential energy | The position-dependent potential energy. |
| $\mechanical{F}$ | $\unit{N}$ | Force | The applied force or external driving force. |
| $\phi$ | — | Field | The field whose value is varied at each spacetime point. |
| $\energy{\mathcal L}$ | — | Lagrangian density | Energy per spatial volume; units depend on the field convention. |
