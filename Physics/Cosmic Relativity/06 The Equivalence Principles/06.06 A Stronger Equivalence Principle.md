> [!TLDR]
> The strong equivalence principle extends universal free fall to gravitational binding energy and asserts local equivalence for gravitational phenomena as well.

## Physical question and model

What changes when the falling body's own gravity matters? The strong equivalence principle extends the question beyond negligible-self-gravity test particles. Compare bodies whose gravitational binding energies are different fractions of their total mass-energy.

## Lecture account

The formulation of the General Theory of Relativity as a geometrical theory presupposes that the EEP includes gravitational phenomena as well. Then one must insist on the *UFF of even the gravitational binding energy of the falling bodies*, leading to the Strong Equivalence Principle (SEP). Simply stated, the SEP asserts the validity of the UFF and the WEP even for bodies with an arbitrary amount of gravitational self-energy [4, 5].

Generalising from the equivalence of gravitational and inertial mass, the SEP asserts universal coupling of gravity to all matter and its interaction energy, including that of the gravitational interaction, highlighting the nonlinear nature of gravity. Hence, *at the fundamental microscopic level, the SEP affirms universal gravitational coupling to the hypothetical gravitons themselves*. This completes Einstein’s Equivalence Principle as the generalisation that asserts the local equivalence of all physical phenomena, including gravitational phenomena, in a uniform gravitational field and in a uniformly accelerated frame without gravity. The SEP is the final frontier of explicit tests of the principle of equivalence.

## Interpretation and checks

A theory can satisfy weak universality for ordinary materials and still predict a self-energy-dependent departure for planets or stars. This is why a laboratory composition test does not exhaust the strong principle. The relevant small parameter is gravitational binding energy relative to rest energy. Predict which is generally more sensitive to such a coupling, a small metal sphere or a compact star, while also considering modelling uncertainty.
