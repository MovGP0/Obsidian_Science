> [!TLDR]
> The author examines the circular definitions of inertial frames and inertial forces, and asks what physical reference determines a straight trajectory.

## Physical question and model

How can an inertial frame be identified operationally? Use sufficiently isolated test bodies, nonrotating gyroscopes, and accelerometers to establish a local freely moving frame. In curved spacetime this construction is local rather than one global Cartesian grid.

## Lecture account

Every architect of mechanics has asked what determines inertial frames and has commented on the difficulty of answering this question. We have already seen the significant contributions of Galileo, Newton, Mach, and Einstein towards an answer, which nevertheless remained inconclusive. The definition and operational meaning of an inertial frame remain important and are at the core of the theories of relativity. Operationally, inertial frames are those in which ‘inertial forces’ do not appear. An inertial force is a ‘fictitious’ force proportional to inertia without any identifiable source of interactions. From Galileo to Einstein, the existence of such inertial frames was simply assumed to state the law of motion of a free particle as $d^2\mechanical{x}/dt^2=0$, commonly stated as ‘a free particle continues to move along a straight line’. However, this calls for a definition and understanding of inertial forces, and we enter a circular loop of definitions and discussions. How do we identify and verify that a trajectory is straight without specifying reference markers? We are then back to Newton’s difficulty about relative motion. In any case, what is interesting for modern fundamental physics is that an inertial force is proportional to the inertial mass, which in turn seems identical to the gravitational mass (or at least equivalent). Thus, one may expect that gravity plays the decisive role in determining what an inertial frame is. E. Mach, despite his detailed discussion of inertial frames, did not spell this out and left matters at speculation on an unspecified interaction with external bodies. The Special Theory of Relativity, a theory of inertial dynamics in empty space, cannot take support from gravity to define its inertial frames. So is the situation in the General Theory of Relativity because its basis is the STR. The GTR also treats gravity as an inertial fictitious force by relying on the postulated equivalence of a gravitational field to an acceleration.

> [!note]
> A gravitational basis of inertial frames can be achieved only in a theory of relativity and gravity that incorporates the factual gravity of all matter in the Universe. Such a theory necessarily has the absolute frame of the Universe.

When we reviewed the primary results of Cosmic Relativity in the first chapter, the relation between gradients in the cosmic gravitational potentials and inertial forces was mentioned explicitly and quantitatively. It is appropriate to state the conceptual account of the solution to the centuries-old puzzle of the origin of inertia, as it emerged in my theory of Cosmic Relativity. Briefly, *inertial frames are those in which the dominant cosmic gravitational potentials do not have spatial and temporal variations*. A ‘force’ in physics is another name for a variation in an interaction potential. Hence, there are *no inertial forces* in such frames, and they are actually realised in a Universe in which matter and energy are distributed approximately isotropically and homogeneously on the large scale. The implied conclusion is important: inertial frames are not frames without gravity. They are in large gravitational potentials, amid a large-scale matter distribution. But those potentials are spatially uniform. Uniform motion preserves this homogeneity, although the isotropy is broken. Since the gravitational potentials of a homogeneous matter distribution remain homogeneous, the frames remain force-free and inertial. The simplicity and physical transparency of this gravitational solution to the problem of inertial frames are evident. We will see numerous demonstrations of this connection, in full quantitative detail, in subsequent chapters.

## Interpretation and checks

A useful distinction is between the operational rule and an explanation of why that rule works. An accelerometer detects proper acceleration; distant stars provide an approximate orientation reference but are not the definition of every local inertial chart. Check whether relative acceleration persists between neighbouring free bodies. If it does, tidal curvature prevents extending the local inertial frame over the whole region.
