> [!TLDR]
> Einstein used the equivalence principle to connect acceleration with gravitational redshift, gravitational time dilation, and the bending of light.

## Physical question and model

How can an accelerating laboratory predict a gravitational clock comparison? Work in a small approximately uniform field, keep potential differences small compared with light speed squared, and compare identical clocks by a specified signal path.

## Lecture account

The equivalence principle was Einstein’s “happiest thought” for good reasons. By 1911, after he realised that it opened a clear path towards a gravitation theory and well before formulating the mathematical theory, he derived important results predicting ‘post-Newtonian’ effects in a gravitational field. These are gravitational redshift of radiation, time dilation of clocks in a gravitational field, and bending of light in a gravitational field (a partial result). Since gravitational time dilation is central to this book, we review its derivation briefly.

In the STR, time dilation happens to a clock moved in space. The ‘proper time’ $\mechanical{\tau}$ in a clock moving with a frame is related to the time $t$ in a stationary reference frame by

$$
d\mechanical{\tau}^2=dt^2-\frac{d\mechanical{x}^2}{\mechanical{c}^2}=dt^2(1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2). \tag{6.6}
$$

Thus, a moving clock records less time than a stationary clock.

Given this result alone, there is no obvious path to deduce that a clock in a gravitational field runs at a different rate from a clock without the field. Two reasons matter: (a) no physical reason has been identified for why a moving clock runs at a lower rate; (b) there is no a priori fundamental connection of dynamics with gravity. The deduction of gravitational time dilation required a further insight, which Einstein supplied. He considered light of a particular frequency $\signal{f}$ (energy $\energy{E}=\energy{h}\signal{f}$) propagating vertically in a gravitational field. With the equivalence principle, the problem can be shifted to a uniformly accelerating frame. To an observer inside it, the frame is at rest in a force field indistinguishable from a gravitational field. To an observer outside, it moves with acceleration $\mechanical{a}$. Let radiation start at point $S_1$ in the frame and propagate up to a nearby point $S_2$, where it is measured (Fig. 6.6). Relative to the outside observer, the frame’s velocity change during propagation is $\delta\mechanical{v}=\mechanical{a}\delta t\simeq\mechanical{a}\mechanical{d}/\mechanical{c}$. We ignore a next-order correction from the additional distance moved by the accelerating frame, $\delta\mechanical{l}=\mechanical{a}(\delta t)^2/2$.

![[cosmic-relativity-06-06-redshift-clocks.svg|**Fig. 6.6** Einstein’s derivation of gravitational redshift and time dilation with the equivalence principle. (a) Radiation emitted at S₁ and received at S₂ in an accelerated frame has a Doppler shift for a stationary observer. For a comoving observer, the frame is at rest in a force field akin to gravity. (b) The equivalence principle implies a frequency shift in a gravitational field and different rates for clocks at different positions.]]

Since the frame’s velocity at emission differs from its velocity at reception, there is a Doppler shift,

$$
\signal{f}'=\signal{f}(1-\delta\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c})\simeq\signal{f}(1-\mechanical{a}\mechanical{d}/\mechanical{c}^2). \tag{6.7}
$$

Again, this ignores the second-order effect $\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2$, which is justified when the velocity is small compared with $\mechanical{c}$. Radiation is therefore received with frequency shift $\delta\signal{f}/\signal{f}=-\mechanical{a}\mechanical{d}/\mechanical{c}^2$. Through the two-way equivalence principle, we return to the real gravitational field. Radiation travelling up a distance $\mechanical{d}$ in a uniform gravitational field $\mechanical{g}$ has the gravitational shift

$$
\frac{\delta\signal{f}}{\signal{f}}=-\frac{\mechanical{g}\mechanical{d}}{\mechanical{c}^2}=\frac{\mechanical{\phi}_1-\mechanical{\phi}_2}{\mechanical{c}^2}, \tag{6.8}
$$

where $\mechanical{\phi}$ is the gravitational potential. This redshift result has numerous astrophysical applications. Einstein went further and extracted a more important result from the same physical situation.

The physical quantity *frequency* is, by definition, the number of occurrences of a periodic phenomenon in unit time. A number $n$ cannot change from emission to detection *in a static situation in a gravitational field*. Thus, the *unit time* in the clocks must have changed in the gravitational field. Following Einstein [22],

> At first glance, equations seem to assert something absurd. If the transmission of light from $S_1$ to $S_2$ is continuous, then how can the number of periods arriving per second at $S_2$ be different from that emitted at $S_1$? But the answer is simple. We cannot simply consider $\signal{f}_1$ and $\signal{f}_2$ as frequencies (numbers of periods per second) because we have not yet defined a time in the system $K$. $\signal{f}_2$ denotes the number of periods referred to the time unit of the clock U at $S_2$, and $\signal{f}_1$ the number of periods referred to the time unit of the identically constructed clock U at $S_1$. There is nothing that compels us to assume that the clocks U, which are situated in different gravitational potentials, must be conceived as going at the same rate. ... Thus, the clocks in $S_1$ and $S_2$ do not both give the “time” correctly.

A clock where the magnitude of the gravitational potential is larger (closer to the massive source) runs at a smaller rate $\mechanical{\phi}/\mechanical{c}^2$ relative to a stationary clock in gravity-free space. The gravitational potential is what matters. Even where the gravitational field is uniform ($\mechanical{g}$ constant), the potential varies with distance as $d\mechanical{\phi}/d\mechanical{x}=-\mechanical{g}$. Therefore, two clocks separated by $\mechanical{d}$ in a constant gravitational field run at different rates:

$$
\signal{T}_1-\signal{T}_2/\bar{\signal{T}}=-\frac{\mechanical{g}\mechanical{d}}{\mechanical{c}^2}=\frac{\mechanical{\phi}_1-\mechanical{\phi}_2}{\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{6.9}
$$

> [!note]
> Equation (6.9) reproduces the source’s grouping. Its left side appears to require parentheses, $(\signal{T}_1-\signal{T}_2)/\bar{\signal{T}}$, to express a dimensionless fractional clock-period difference consistent with the right side.

When the gravitational field itself varies, as in most cases including GPS, use the correct potential difference at the two locations rather than the approximation $\Delta\signal{T}/\signal{T}=-\mechanical{g}\mechanical{d}/\mechanical{c}^2$.

## Interpretation and checks

A photon climbing to higher Newtonian potential is received at lower frequency, while an identical stationary upper clock accumulates proper time faster relative to the lower reference. These are related but differently worded comparisons. The fractional scale is acceleration times height divided by light speed squared. Check that reversing the height difference reverses the sign. The printed period-difference equation needs a grouped numerator to be dimensionless. Large heights or strong curvature require the full metric.
