> [!TLDR]
> Satellite experiments extend the free-fall duration and search for a periodic relative displacement between test bodies of different materials.

## Physical question and model

Why does a drag-free satellite improve a differential-acceleration test? Orbit provides a long common free-fall history, while electrostatic control compares co-located test masses of different materials. The measurement is the differential control force needed to maintain their relative configuration.

## Lecture account

The advantage of Earth’s large gravitational field is not reflected in a typical free-fall experiment because of the very short duration available in the laboratory. Such experiments need chambers with a high vacuum. Even in specially constructed ‘drop towers’, the free-fall duration is only a few seconds.

A free-fall experiment can become a modulated long-term experiment with a very large observation time if conducted in a satellite in near-Earth orbit. It takes full advantage of Earth’s large gravity and the indefinite free-fall duration in orbit (Fig. 6.5). There have been many excellent proposals to extend the sensitivity of the UFF–WEP test to $10^{-17}$ in space experiments [15, 16]. Due to instrumentation complexity, funding constraints, and the long preparation required, only one experiment has been possible in recent times, called ‘Microscope’ [17]. The experiment monitored the differential acceleration of two masses for several months in orbit, in a drag-free satellite, and affirmed the UFF and the WEP to better than $2\times10^{-14}$. A further significant improvement in these constraints is not expected soon.

## Interpretation and checks

The longer observation helps only if thermal, electrostatic, magnetic, and gravity-gradient errors are modelled. As a dated comparison, the [2022 MICROSCOPE final analysis](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.129.121102) reported no weak-equivalence violation for titanium and platinum at the 10⁻¹⁵ scale, with separate statistical and systematic errors. This is a constraint on differential free fall, not a direct measurement of a proposed cosmic inertial potential.
