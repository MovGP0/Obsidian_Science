> [!TLDR]
> Einstein's light postulate links space and time measurements between moving frames and gives physical meaning to local time.

## Physical question and model

Which interval remains the same when inertial observers disagree about distance and duration? Assume flat spacetime, inertial coordinates, and Einstein synchronisation. The light relation is restricted to null paths; the spacetime interval applies to arbitrary event separations.

## Lecture account

The discussion by Einstein on space and time in the context of the propagation of light marked a revolutionary change in these concepts in physics. In effect, he replaced the concepts on space and time with spatial and temporal intervals connected by the propagation of light. We will examine the consequences—the good and the bad—of this drastic change of the representation of the physical space and time in detail, later in the book. In this chapter, I mention the reasons that necessitated this radical change.

While exploring the universal Principle of Relativity, or the impossibility to detect the state of uniform motion, Einstein stressed that there was no phenomenon in mechanics or electrodynamics that necessitated the concept of absolute rest. In other words, Einstein argued that the notion of real motion, which required an absolute reference grid of space and time, was physically meaningless. He stated his conviction that there was no empirical reason to consider a privileged reference frame to define true motion and the state of rest. Therefore, the theory should be based on physical effects that depend only on the velocity of the relative motion between frames. Then he reasoned that the empirical physical fact of the independence of the velocity of light on the velocity of the source should be made consistent with the Principle of Relativity (PoR), by *postulating the independence and invariance of the relative velocity of light on the motion of the observer* as well, and thereby on the inertial motion of any frame. This is the ‘light hypothesis’. The Lorentz transformations resulted from this postulate, as it is well known. The most important conceptual element in these developments was the ‘physicalisation’ of what was known as Lorentz's ‘local time’. Though the Lorentz transformations (LT) relating the spatial coordinates and time in different reference frames in motion were already known, from the writings of Lorentz and Poincaré, the ‘local time’ in the LT was thought to be just a mathematical device to ensure the first order ($\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$) invariance of Maxwell's equations. But Einstein realised that the postulated physical invariance of the relative velocity of light could be implemented only by requiring that the time $t'$ shown by a clock at the distance $\mechanical{x}$, in a frame O′ in relative motion at the velocity $\mechanical{v}$, should be different from the time $t$ in the rest frame O. In particular, there should be a physical counterpart to the term that is linear in the relative velocity, $t'=t-\mechanical{v}\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{c}^2$, which was Lorentz's local time. This term defines the clocks that were ‘simultaneous and synchronised’ in the O′ frame, but nevertheless running at *rates that depend on both the relative velocity and the distance, relative to a clock in the rest frame O*. We will have much to discuss about this term, both about its physical basis and about its empirical status, as revealed through many experiments.

Though Einstein was concerned about the distance measured by material measuring rods and the time measured by clocks, while discussing the theories of relativity, the mathematical reformulation of the theory in terms of the features of abstract space and time, by H. Minkowski, changed the scenario significantly [15]. While making the whole theoretical edifice more abstract and geometrical, it also provided the foundational mathematical structure for further developments of the theory, as well as for the formulation of the theory of gravity as the geometrical theory of General Relativity.

Minkowski's decisive advance was the realisation that Einstein's theory, with the invariance of the relative velocity of light and the resulting Lorentz transformations, could be formulated in terms of an invariance relation in a new four-dimensional geometrical entity, called “space-time”, by combining the three coordinates of space with time as a fourth coordinate. This was a free creation of the mathematical mind.

The most important physical quantity in the geometrical formulation of physical theories is the ‘distance’ between two points in the space under consideration. The distance is given by a quadratic sum of coordinate intervals, resembling the Pythagoras formula $a^2+b^2=c^2$ relating the lengths of the sides of a right triangle. In three dimensions, for example, the distance between two nearby points separated by the orthogonal coordinates $\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z}$ and $\mechanical{x}+d\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y}+d\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z}+d\mechanical{z}$, is $d\mechanical{l}=\sqrt{d\mechanical{x}^2+d\mechanical{y}^2+d\mechanical{z}^2}$. Minkowski's idea was to incorporate a ‘dimension’ of time into this geometrical scheme and create a 4-dimensional ‘space-time’.

The concept of space-time as a homogeneous physical entity has become an integral part of physics, but the real difference between space and time is hidden in their mathematical representation. Space-time is made up of points called ‘events’, which require the specification of a spatial location (3 coordinates) and the time of occurrence, which is the 4th coordinate; the direction of time is ‘orthogonal’ to the spatial directions. A rotation of the coordinate axes is equivalent to the transfer to a uniformly moving frame.

But, there is no natural way of treating the four numbers as describing a single geometrical point in a 4-dimensional physical world. In any geometrical scheme, the distance between two points remains invariant when the coordinate system is rotated. But, how does one rotate in space-time? In Minkowski's space-time, a ‘rotation’ involving the time-axis corresponds to a ‘boost’, or a movement at constant velocity (actually, the angle of rotation is in different senses for a spatial axis and the axis of time; we need not worry about that strange detail in this discussion). But, the conventional distance between two events in space-time, $d\mechanical{l}^2=d\mechanical{x}^2+dt^2$, does not remain invariant when the axes $(\mechanical{x},t)$ are rotated. Instead, *what remains invariant is the quadratic difference between space and time*, and not the quadratic sum. The ‘invariant distance’ $ds$ between two nearby points in space-time is $ds=\sqrt{d\mechanical{x}^2+d\mechanical{y}^2+d\mechanical{z}^2-\mechanical{c}^2dt^2}$, *with a minus sign* for $dt^2$. Consequently, the time coordinate has a hidden imaginary content, $-\mechanical{c}^2dt^2=(i\mechanical{c}dt)^2$. Therefore, Minkowski's space-time is created artificially. However, this can be made to look like the conventional ‘sum of squares’ expression for the geometrical distance by writing the distance as a product of a ‘metric’ and the square of the coordinate interval, as $ds^2=\eta_xd\mechanical{x}^2+\eta_tdt^2$, where $\eta_x=1$, and $\eta_t=-1$. The ‘η-metric’ carries the aberration of sign and (mathematically) the imaginary nature of time, hidden from any explicit view. But, it should be realised that space and time do not have equal status in the constructed space-time.

> [!note] Source coordinate convention
> The source writes both $d\mechanical{x}^2+dt^2$ and expressions with $\mechanical{c}^2dt^2$, without specifying a change of units. Its later metric expression likewise uses $\eta_t=-1$ with $dt^2$. These forms are retained. In the Pythagoras formula above, $c$ is a triangle side, not the physical speed $\mechanical{c}$.

There is a deep conceptual problem in taking ‘time’ as a physical dimension and coordinate with arbitrary extension, similar to the extended ‘space’. This has to do with the real physical nature of time that is inseparably synonymous with the changes in the physical states of matter [14]. All representations and manifestations of time are such changes, and a convenient one is chosen as a clock and a standard of time. There is no time separate from this, and there is no way of abstracting a notion of time independent and separate from changes in matter.

> [!note]
> When we talk about changes in a physical configuration that happen in time, we are actually comparing a change in one physical system with a change in another system. All such comparisons are in the present, done locally. There is no physical entity called time, separate from the reference physical systems. Physical time does not exist in empty space devoid of matter and energy. The inseparable relation between matter, gravity, and time is extensively discussed in my monograph *Gravity's Time* [14].

Then it is clear that the dimension called time does not have the arbitrary extension from the past to a future—time exists as a real physical entity only in the present. There is only a ‘now-time’; all the past exists only as an accumulated local record in space, in the present. This is even more obvious when we consider space with matter. An instant of time corresponds to the existence of matter everywhere in space. Now, another next instant is the *same matter in space* and there cannot be two copies of matter in space, existing in two instants, one in the past and another at present. One may say figuratively that a fresh duration of time *assimilates as well as annihilates* the previous one. Thus, the extended space-time is a non-existent metaphysical notion. It is convenient in the mathematical representation of evolving phenomena, but should not be confused with a real physical entity. Any statement about space-time that assumes an extended existence for the time-dimension cannot be defended. With this point clarified, we can examine the mathematical description.

We will use the common term ‘interval’ for the unconventional ‘distance’ between two points (events) in space-time. As mentioned already, a rotation of axes in space-time is equivalent to a relative motion between inertial frames. While the invariance of a physical distance between two points in space when examined from different angles is obvious, the invariance of an interval in space-time is not automatic—it is based on a postulate, of the invariance of the relative velocity of light for any inertially moving observer. With this light hypothesis, light travels the distance $d\mechanical{l}=\mechanical{c}dt$ in the time interval $dt$, in any inertial frame. The mathematical statement of this propagation between two points $\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z}$ and $\mechanical{x}+d\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y}+d\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z}+d\mechanical{z}$ is

$$
d\mechanical{l}=\sqrt{d\mechanical{x}^2+d\mechanical{y}^2+d\mechanical{z}^2}=\mechanical{c}dt. \tag{2.1}
$$

The more is the distance, the more is the time required. It is this physical statement that is written as the square of the interval between two events in space-time,

$$
ds^2=d\mechanical{x}^2+d\mechanical{y}^2+d\mechanical{z}^2-\mechanical{c}^2dt^2
\equiv\eta_{xx}d\mechanical{x}^2+\eta_{yy}d\mechanical{y}^2+\eta_{zz}d\mechanical{z}^2+\eta_{tt}\mechanical{c}^2dt^2. \tag{2.2}
$$

with the metric coefficients $\eta_{xx}=\eta_{yy}=\eta_{zz}=1$, $\eta_{tt}=-1$. Actually, this discussion shows that the negative sign for $dt^2$ is because we prefer to keep the spatial distance as real positive. Formally, we can choose time to have the positive character and then the spatial distance will pick up the negative sign. The quantity $ds$ is also called a ‘line element’. The light hypothesis implies that the relation $d\mechanical{x}^2+d\mechanical{y}^2+d\mechanical{z}^2-\mechanical{c}^2dt^2=0$ is preserved in any inertial frame. This is exactly the statement that the 4-dimensional distance between two events, the space-time interval, remains invariant under rotations in space-time.

For two arbitrary physical events at $\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z}$ and $\mechanical{x}+d\mechanical{x},\mechanical{y}+d\mechanical{y},\mechanical{z}+d\mechanical{z}$ at time $t$ and $t+dt$, the difference $d\mechanical{l}^2-\mechanical{c}^2dt^2$ is not zero; $ds^2$ can be positive or negative. However, *since any two points in space-time can be connected by light, it follows that their interval remains invariant irrespective of how one chooses to measure it*. Whether the events are connected by light or by throwing a stone is immaterial for the invariance of the interval for inertial observers. Hence, the quantity $ds^2\equiv d\mechanical{x}^2+d\mechanical{y}^2+d\mechanical{z}^2-\mechanical{c}^2dt^2$ between any two events is an invariant in inertial motion at any velocity $\mechanical{v}$.

A coordinate choice in ordinary space need not use orthonormal coordinates. Then, one can have cross terms like $d\mechanical{x}d\mechanical{y}$ in the quadratic expression for the distance, with a corresponding term $\eta_{xy}$ in the metric. However, since $d\mechanical{x}d\mechanical{y}=d\mechanical{y}d\mechanical{x}$, the geometrical metric is symmetric, $\eta_{xy}=\eta_{yx}$. Therefore, the usual general metric in n-dimensional space has $n(n+1)/2$ independent terms. Since empty space remains the same everywhere and always, the metric has no dependence on the location or the epoch of time—it does not depend on the coordinates. If we move in empty space, nothing changes, and the motion cannot even be detected, because there are no references for motion. *Empty space remains homogeneous and isotropic even for a moving observer, and this is the entire basis of the STR and its interpretation of the Lorentz transformations. If this single fact is violated, the logic and the mathematical structure of the STR, based on the Minkowski space and the Lorentz transformations, would be invalidated*. This is why it is such a fragile theory in the light of what turned out to be the factual situation regarding the space around us. Even the early observations in modern cosmology revealed that our spatial environment in the Universe is very different from the restricted space demanded by the STR.

In any case, let us continue with the description of space and time in Einstein's STR. Since space and time are treated in a similar manner, with equal status, all intervals elapsed in all directions in space-time are considered as ‘motion’. Thus, the elapsed time, while remaining at the same spatial point, is to be considered as the ‘motion in the time-dimension’; one travels a distance $\mechanical{c}dt$ *along the direction of time*, during the duration $dt$. The ‘speed’ of travel is the invariant $\mechanical{c}$. Light always travels the spatial distance $\mechanical{c}dt$ in the duration $dt$; again the speed is $\mechanical{c}$. An observer O′ in inertial motion is at rest (in space) in his frame. Since the invariant interval $ds'^2/\mechanical{c}^2=dt'^2$ when $d\mechanical{x}'$ etc. are zero, $d\tau=ds/\mechanical{c}$ can be identified with the time interval marked by a clock carried with an observer in motion. The observer travels the distance $\mechanical{c}d\tau$ at the speed $\mechanical{c}$ in the direction of time. The quantity $d\tau$ is called the proper time.

This picture of movement through space-time reveals one of the most important consequence of motion—that of the time dilation of clocks in motion. The corollary to the statement that an observer (with his clock) moves only in time while remaining stationary in space is that the clock will move less in time if it moves in space! It is the distance moved in space-time that remains as an invariant. Since it is the proper time in the comoving frame,

$$
d\tau^2=dt^2-\frac{d\mechanical{x}^2}{\mechanical{c}^2}
=dt^2\left(1-\frac{1}{\mechanical{c}^2}\left(\frac{d\mechanical{x}}{dt}\right)^2\right)
=dt^2(1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2). \tag{2.3}
$$

This means that when a duration of one second ($\delta t$) elapses in a clock that is at relative rest, a shorter duration $\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}$ elapses in a clock that moves (with a reference frame) at the velocity $\mechanical{v}$.

It is not only the length of position vectors (or distance between points) that remains invariant under rotations in space. The length of any vector that resembles the position vector also remains invariant under spatial rotations. Similarly, all suitably constructed 4-dimensional vectors, or ‘4-vectors’, share the invariance property in space-time rotations, which are the Lorentz transformations. The theoretical strength of the STR comes from this geometrical invariance of the 4-vectors that are constructed from physical quantities. For example, the 4-velocity is an important quantity; it has the spatial velocity $(\mechanical{\mathbf v})=d\mechanical{\mathbf x}/dt$ as the spatial component and velocity along the time axis, $d\tau/dt$, as the fourth component. The invariant length of the 4-velocity is $-\mechanical{c}^2$. Another important quantity is the 4-momentum, with the momentum $(\mechanical{p}_x,\mechanical{p}_y,\mechanical{p}_z)$ as the spatial components, and the energy $\energy{E}/\mechanical{c}$ as the time-component. Its invariant length is just $\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}$, where $\mechanical{m}$ is the (rest) mass. Then its squared length is

$$
-\mechanical{m}^2\mechanical{c}^2=\mechanical{p}_x^2+\mechanical{p}_y^2+\mechanical{p}_z^2-\energy{E}^2/\mechanical{c}^2 \tag{2.4}
$$

consistent with the relation $\energy{E}=\mechanical{m}\mechanical{c}^2$ in the comoving rest frame.

> [!note] Source sign and four-velocity conventions
> Equation (2.2) uses the spatial-positive signature, for which a timelike interval is negative. The subsequent source text writes $ds'^2/\mechanical{c}^2=dt'^2$ without the minus sign. Its description of four-velocity also uses derivatives with respect to coordinate time and a dimensionless fourth component, rather than a common proper-time parameter. These statements are retained as printed.

The empty space and time of the STR are altered by motion, with only the relative velocity as the physical parameter. But the properties of the STR's space and time do not vary from point to point. Particles move along straight lines in such a space-time in the absence of forces. They are the ‘geodesics’ in that space-time. One may say that the ‘geometry’ of the space-time in the STR is ‘flat’, without any curvature, similar to the Euclidean plane where parallel lines remain parallel when extended, and the sum of the angles in a triangle is $180^{\circ}$. The interval between two events is given by the expression in Eq. (2.2), with the coefficient (weight) of each coordinate intervals (like $\eta_{xx}=1$ for $d\mechanical{x}$) remaining as constants. But, when matter and its gravity are present, this simple description is no more possible. It is the modification of the Minkowskian empty space and time by the presence of matter and its motion (energy and momentum) that is the subject of the General Theory of Relativity (GTR). But the GTR inherits its space-time, and its basic mathematical structure, from the space-time of the STR. That is how the GTR becomes a geometrical theory of gravity. In abstract terms, the space and time of General Relativity are seen as ‘deformable’ by the presence of matter. However, as per the cardinal statement about dynamics of particles in the GTR, particles continue to move along the straightest possible paths in the deformed space and time. These are the geodesics or the ‘extremal’ trajectories in a space-time that is ‘curved’ by the presence of matter. Thus, any apparent curvature in the trajectories of particles, in space or in space-time, is because of the curvature of the underlying space and time, which of course remains unobservable directly.

The modification of the space-time in the GTR is represented through the modification of the metric coefficients $\eta_{ij}$. They now depend on the distribution of matter and energy. Therefore, *the components of the metric become functions of the spatial location and time*. The space-time interval varies with the position in space in the presence of matter-energy as

$$
ds^2=\sum_{i,j}g_{ij}(\mechanical{x},t)\,d\mechanical{x}^i\,d\mechanical{x}^j \tag{2.5}
$$

where $g_{ij}(\mechanical{x},t)$ is the modified metric (here, ‘$\mechanical{x}$’ stands for any coordinate, including time, and the superscript $i,j=0,1,2,3$ distinguishes $t$ and the three spatial coordinates). The constant $\mechanical{c}$ is included with the coordinate $t$, such that the units of both time and space are the same (that of the length). The metric specifies how the spatial and temporal intervals behave in the presence of matter, at each point in the space-time. These quantities represent the relativistic gravitational potentials due to the matter. What is remarkable is that the whole theory of gravitational physics is described by just this structure, with the only requirement that the metric of a physical situation involving gravity obeys Einstein's equation of the GTR. This is a set of relations connecting the metric and its spatial and temporal derivatives (up to second order) with the distribution of matter and its currents. We will study some aspects of this elegant theory in [[07.04 The GTR as the Geometric Theory of Gravity|Chapter 7]]. As a matter of fact, Einstein's equation specifies how the separation between material particles (spatial distances) and the time marked by physical clocks distributed in space are affected by the presence of matter and its motion. However, the singular feature of the gravitational interaction, that the acceleration of particles in a gravitational field does not depend on any physical property of the particles, allows one to describe the theory purely in terms of the geometric features of the underlying space and time.

There are physicists who are so obsessed with the geometrical description of gravity that they claim an undisputable physical reality to the space-time amalgam that is deformable by matter, or even without matter, by its own nature. For example, while it is well known that the Hubble recession of distant galaxies can either be described in terms of a real physical motion, or as an expansion of space itself while the galaxies remain embedded in space, motionless, there are empirically unfounded claims that the expansion of space is the only possible description. The exclusive geometric character of gravity is a widespread opinion and that is why gravity has remained excluded from the theoretical formulation of other interactions. But, there are strong opposing opinions as well. S. Weinberg wrote in his widely used text book on ‘Gravitation and Cosmology’ [16],

> However, I believe that the geometrical approach has driven a wedge between general relativity and the theory of elementary particles. As long as it could be hoped, as Einstein did hope, that matter would eventually be understood in geometrical terms, it made sense to give Riemannian geometry a primary role in describing the theory of gravitation. But now the passage of time has taught us not to expect that the strong, weak and electromagnetic interactions can be understood in geometrical terms, and too great an emphasis on geometry can only obscure the deep connections between gravitation and the rest of physics.

Einstein himself, who advocated and emphasised the geometrical features of the GTR, wrote in a letter (to L. Barnett in 1948 [17]),

> I do not agree with the idea that the general theory of relativity is geometrising Physics or the gravitational field. The concepts of Physics have always been geometrical concepts and I cannot see why the $g_{ij}$ field should be called more geometrical than, for instance, the electromagnetic field or the distance of bodies in Newtonian Mechanics. The notion comes probably from the fact that the mathematical origin of the $g_{ij}$ field is the Gauss-Riemann theory of the metrical continuum which we wont to look at as a part of geometry. I am convinced, however, that the distinction between geometrical and other kinds of fields is not logically found.

He had expressed the same opinion consistently in other occasions, for example, during his academic visit to Paris in 1922 [18]. The reasonable view to take in these matters is clear from Einstein's expressed opinion (somewhat surprising, and contrary to the general impression).

We will discuss later in more detail how gravity is described in a metrical space, as the curvature of space and time, with several space and time components. The salient feature is that instead of a separate field of gravity permeating space, the description of gravity in the GTR is as a modified and curved space-time. *There is no gravitational field akin to the electromagnetic field, according to the GTR*. Thus, the space-time of the GTR is dynamical, like matter, and it has new physical properties like ‘elasticity’ and the ability to expand or contract. Clearly, we see that the space-time of the GTR is even more feature-laden than the ether of the 19th century, but it remains as unobservable as ever, except as manifestations through the motion of matter.

## Interpretation and checks

The proper-time expression describes a clock on a timelike worldline. At rest it gives coordinate time; at low speed its correction is second order in speed divided by light speed. A photon path has zero proper-time interval, not a usable photon rest frame. The mass–energy relation is an invariant of energy and momentum, not a statement that each observer measures the same energy. In gravity, curvature can prevent one global inertial chart while preserving these relations locally.
