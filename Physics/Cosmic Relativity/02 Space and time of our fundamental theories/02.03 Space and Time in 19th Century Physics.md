> [!TLDR]
> Nineteenth-century physics used fields to describe local interactions and proposed an ether as the medium for electromagnetic waves.

## Physical question and model

Why did nineteenth-century electromagnetism change the picture of physical space? Separate the electromagnetic field, which predicts forces and wave propagation, from a mechanical ether imagined to support it. Successful field equations do not require every proposed microscopic picture of their support.

## Lecture account

The physical space of the 19th century physics was not really empty, and it was very different from Newton's space. The rapid sequence of discoveries in electricity and magnetism brought new considerations in the conception of physical space. One was the postulated physical medium of the ‘ether’, filling the whole space, permeating everything [11]. Though *the ether was as unobservable as the absolute space*, it was a firm part of physics for a whole century or more. Then, there was the notion of a continuum of ‘fields’, sourced by the charges and their currents. These were postulated to avoid the difficulty to accept an ‘action at a distance’ between spatially separated bodies. The intermediary of fields helped in reconciling interactions of matter as a local and causal mechanism; one body sources a field that varies (decreases) as the distance increases, which interacts with other bodies in its reach, ‘locally’. The concept was used very effectively, both in the physical picture and in the mathematical theory, to describe electrodynamics and gravity. The sources were charges (electric charge, mass etc.) and their currents, and the corresponding fields were accurately represented by mathematical entities, like scalars, vectors etc. This greatly helped in the rapid completion of the mathematical theory from which accurate applications followed.

It was Michael Faraday who introduced the notion of a real field that acted between material bodies across an extended space. The graphic demonstrations of the magnetic field helped to ingrain the strong image of a physical reality to fields (Fig. 2.3). Since gravitation and electromagnetism were the only long range interactions known then, and also now, they are the fields that built our intuition about the space-filling fields that obey certain mathematical constraints. When light was identified as the propagating oscillations of the electromagnetic fields, their postulated physical reality was reinforced. The empty space was still the holder of everything physical, both matter and fields. However, the propagation of light in space required space to be treated as a ‘medium’ endowed with electromagnetic properties; but, space itself does not have any electromagnetic properties and a separate entity called the ‘ether’ was postulated as the medium of electromagnetic action. *Since nobody talked about gravitational waves then, there was the need for only the electromagnetic ether, and not an additional gravitational ether*.

With the stationary ether filling space, a privileged absolute reference for the motion of electrically charged particles and the propagation of light entered physics naturally and legitimately. Unlike space, the ether had properties of a ‘material’ medium that supported the electromagnetic phenomena, especially the oscillations and the propagation of electromagnetic waves. It had a ‘permittivity’ and ‘permeability’ that defined its electrical and magnetic response. The stationary ether was the new absolute in physics.

As stated in Fig. 2.3, the radial electric field obeys $\electric{E}\propto\electric{Q}/\mechanical{r}^2$, and $\electric{q}$ is the test charge. The symbol $\magnetic{B}$ denotes the magnetic field.

As we saw in the previous chapter, the first decades of the 20th century witnessed the quick waning of the ether from the space of physics. After the acceptance of the Special Theory of Relativity, which rejected the undetectable ether, physics explicitly discarded such a hypothetical medium, though it was being discussed in the context of many experiments till much later. In fact, when Michelson published the positive result of the Michelson-Gale interferometer experiment in 1925, the interpretation he used was the retardation of the relative velocity of light in the ether, due to the rotation of the Earth [12]. Even Einstein talked about a ‘new ether’ in 1920 (Leyden lecture), to mean a space filled with the fields representing the interactions. Much later, Dirac tried to argue, unconvincingly, that ‘one is forced to have an ether’ in the quantum theory of electrodynamics [13].

Space continued to be considered in the physicists' intuition as an infinite container that can hold matter and its motion, without being affected by the presence of matter. However, the notion of fields changed the fundamental character of space. The evolution of the conception of space through the 19th and the early 20th century is described well by Einstein. He wrote (in 1953) in the forward to Max Jammer's book [5],

> ...two concepts of space may be contrasted as follows: (a) space as positional quantity of the world of material objects; (b) space as container of all material objects. In case (a), space without a material object is inconceivable. In case (b), a material object can only be conceived as existing in space; space then appears as a reality which in a certain sense is superior to the material world. Both space concepts are free creations of the human imagination, means devised for easier comprehension of our sense experience.
>
> These schematic considerations concern the nature of space from the geometric and from the kinematical point of view, respectively. They are in a sense reconciled with each other by Descartes' introduction of the coordinate system, although this presupposes the logically more daring space concept (b). The concept of space was enriched and complicated by Galileo and Newton, in that space must be introduced as the independent cause of the inertial behaviour of bodies if one wishes to give the classical principle of inertia (and therewith the classical law of motion) an exact meaning. To have realised this fully and clearly is in my opinion one of Newton's greatest achievements. In contrast with Leibniz and Huygens, it was clear to Newton that the space concept (a) was not sufficient to serve as the foundation for the inertia principle and the law of motion. He came to this decisive event though he actively shared the uneasiness which was the cause of the opposition of the other two: space is not only introduced as an independent thing apart from material objects, but also assigned an absolute role in the whole causal structure of the theory. This role is absolute in the sense that space (as an inertial system) acts on all material objects, while these do not in turn exert any reaction on space.

> ...subsequent development of the problems, proceeding in a roundabout way which no one then could possibly foresee, has shown that the resistance of Leibniz and Huygens, intuitively well founded but supported by inadequate arguments, was actually justified. It required severe struggle to arrive at the concept of independent and absolute space, indispensable for the development of theory. It has required no less strenuous exertions subsequently to overcome this concept—a process which is probably by no means as yet completed.
>
> The victory over the concept of absolute space or over that of the inertial system became possible only because the concept of material object was gradually replaced as the fundamental concept of physics by that of the field. If the laws of physics are generally covariant, that is, are not dependent on a particular choice of coordinate system, then the introduction of an independent (absolute) space is no longer necessary. That which constitutes the spatial character of the reality is then simply the four-dimensionality of the field. There is no “empty” space, that is, there is no space without a field.

From the subsequent developments in physics, it seems that the most important addition to the concept of empty space, as a potential holder for matter and its dynamics, is perhaps the introduction of physical fields that fill the whole ‘available’ space. Fields were also as unobservable as space because one could sense them only as a delayed action of a material body on other bodies, and nothing more tangible. Yet, fields were ascribed a physical reality that is more concrete than what was possible for space, or for the ether. One valid reason for this is that the same fields could propagate as waves; therefore one had to accept that if light was a real entity, the same level of reality should be ascribed to the underlying fields as well. Thus, their reality in physics is at a different level compared to the physical reality of empty space, which remains as a passive background for the dynamics of matter. One may say that fields are physical, and space is more of a metaphysical nature, in comparison.

Time, on the other hand, evolved from the absolute time of the 19th century physics to the multitude of times that depend on the relative velocity of a clock in the theory of relativity [14]. This was a more drastic change than what was being discussed in the context of the unobservable space. *Time is an accumulated physical quantity*, measured by a physical clock, and it preserves its own past records. A change in the rate of a clock in motion manifests as the secular accumulated change in the total duration, readily measurable even after the relative motion has stopped. Therefore, the measurement of the physical changes in time is a precision tool for the tests of theories of relativity. We will discuss the modifications of time by motion and gravity in great detail in this book, because of this very reason. At this stage, we merely note that the advent of the theories of relativity introduced a multitude of times, as many as the observers in different states of motion. However, it was Einstein's STR that required the rejection of the absolute universal time from physics, because the *physical effects in the STR depend only on the relative velocity between two frames, with any of the frames assuming the status of a rest frame*. Then, the central role of an absolute (preferred) spatial reference for motion disappears and with that, any notion of an absolute time (privileged clock) also disappears.

## Interpretation and checks

Use energy flow and finite propagation as the operational content of a field model. A mechanical ether becomes a distinct physical hypothesis only when it predicts an additional observable, such as motion-dependent propagation. Compare two wave media: air supplies a measurable rest frame for sound, while a proposed electromagnetic medium must be tested rather than inferred from that analogy. A useful check is to ask which constitutive properties would need independent measurement.
