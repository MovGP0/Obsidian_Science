> [!TLDR]
> Newton distinguishes absolute space and time from relative measures, then uses rotating water to discuss absolute motion.

## Physical question and model

What does Newton's rotating bucket establish about motion? The system is water in a rotating container under approximately uniform gravity. Surface shape measures rotation relative to a local inertial frame. The thought experiment does not directly measure an otherwise invisible substance called absolute space.

## Lecture account

Physics is primarily the science of the dynamics of matter—of the motion and evolution of matter, in relation to the fundamental interactions between material bodies. Two cardinal factors, implicit and taken for granted, are the notions of space and time. However, both are ‘unobservables’—physical notions that are not tangible to any form of sensing, except through the configurations of the sensible matter [3]. In the absence of all matter, the notion of time certainly ceases to exist, but the notion of space might still be definable, albeit with considerable conceptual difficulty. However, without positing space and time, it seems difficult, if not impossible, to embark on any discussion of dynamics. These are the primary postulates for physics. Therefore, they should be carefully defined and dealt with.

A detailed discussion on the concept of space used by the creators of the theories of motion—Aristotle, Kepler, Copernicus, Galileo, Newton, Huygens, Leibniz, Descartes, Mach, Einstein, and many others—is contained in the book ‘Discovery of dynamics’ by Julian Barbour, for example [4]. Also, there are detailed discussions by Max Jammer, in his book ‘Concepts of Space’ [5]. Essentially most scholars meant relative motion when they discussed motion, relative to other material objects, with the sense of time defined by changes in the material objects. Both Copernicus and Kepler stated explicitly that the motion they mentioned was the motion relative to the distant stars that were stationary, the motion with respect to their absolute immobility. It is only with Newton's attempt to formulate a theory of motion in a mathematically complete manner, that we face the distinction between an absolute space and relative spaces.

Though the all-important concept of ‘space’ always had an independent and primary physical status, separate from matter and its dynamics, it is with Newton's treatise that one sees a careful attempt to define and establish its necessary active role in dynamics. We begin the discussion on the space in physics by examining its treatment by two pioneers, Isaac Newton and Ernst Mach, separated in time by two centuries. The reason to quote their views in detail is to ensure precision and completeness. Besides, the central issue of the origin of inertia and its relation (or non-relation) to the notion of space emerges in sharp focus and contrast in these discussions.

Newton's characterisation of space and time is described in detail in the first ‘Scholium’ of Book 1 of his Principia Mathematica, after the definitions and ‘axioms’ on the laws of motion [1]. (The italics are mine.)

> **SCHOLIUM**
>
> Hitherto I have laid down the definitions of such words as are less known, and explained the sense in which I would have them to be understood in the following discourse. *I do not define time, space, place and motion, as being well known to all*. Only I must observe, that the vulgar conceive those quantities under no other notions but from the relation they bear to sensible objects. And thence arise certain prejudices, for the removing of which, it will be convenient to distinguish them into *absolute and relative, true and apparent, mathematical and common*.
>
> I. Absolute, true, and mathematical time, of itself, and from its own nature flows equably without regard to anything external, and by another name is called duration: relative, apparent, and common time, is some sensible and external (whether accurate or unequable) measure of duration by the means of motion, which is commonly used instead of true time; such as an hour, a day, a month, a year
>
> II. Absolute space, in its own nature, without regard to anything external, remains always similar and immoveable. Relative space is some movable dimension or measure of the absolute spaces; which our senses determine by its position to bodies; and which is vulgarly taken for immovable space; such is the dimension of a subterraneous, an aereal, or celestial space, determined by its position in respect of the earth...
>
> *But we may distinguish rest and motion, absolute and relative, one from the other by their properties, causes and effects*. It is a property of rest, that bodies really at rest do rest in respect to one another. And therefore as it is possible, that in the remote regions of the fixed stars, or perhaps far beyond them, there may be some body absolutely at rest; but impossible to know, from the position of bodies to one another in our regions whether any of these do keep the same position to that remote body; it follows that absolute rest cannot be determined from the position of bodies in our regions.

Newton then prescribed the empirical distinction between the real motion relative to the absolute space and a motion relative to other bodies. We see at once that the central conceptual issues in relativity and the desire for their empirical resolution can be traced to the very starting point of Newtonian mechanics. His ‘pail and water’ experiment has been discussed and debated by physicists and philosophers for the past three centuries, *without consensus* (Fig. 2.1).

(a) The rotating bucket has a large velocity relative to the water, but the surface remains flat. (b) As the water picks up the bucket's angular velocity, the surface becomes curved because of the outward centrifugal force. It remains curved when water and bucket have no relative motion. Continuing with Newton's description,

> *The effects which distinguish absolute from relative motion, are, the forces of receding from the axis of circular motion*. For there are no such forces in a circular motion purely relative, but in a true and absolute circular motion, they are greater or less, according to the quantity of the motion. If a vessel, hung by a long cord, is so often turned about that the cord is strongly twisted, then filled with water, and held at rest together with the water; after, by the sudden action of any other force, it is whirled about the contrary way, and while the cord is untwisting itself, the vessel continues for some time in this motion; the surface of the water will at first be plain, as before the vessel began to move: but the vessel, by gradually communicating its motion to the water, will make it begin sensibly to revolve, and recede by little and little from the middle, and ascend to the sides of the vessel, forming itself into a concave figure (as I have experienced), and the swifter the motion becomes, the higher will the water rise, till at last, performing its revolutions in the same times with the vessel, it becomes relatively at rest in it.

> This ascent of the water shows its endeavour to recede from the axis of its motion; and the true and absolute circular motion of the water, which is here directly contrary to the relative discovers itself, and may be measured by this endeavour. *At first, when the relative motion of the water in the vessel was greatest, it produced no endeavour to recede from the axis*; the water showed no tendency to the circumference, nor any ascent towards the sides of the vessel, but remained of a plain surface, and therefore its true circular motion had not yet begun. But afterwards, when the relative motion of the water had decreased, the ascent thereof towards the sides of the vessel proved its endeavour to recede from the axis; and this endeavour showed *the real circular motion of the water perpetually increasing, till it had acquired its greatest quantity, when the water rested relatively in the vessel. And therefore this endeavour does not depend upon any translation of the water in respect of the ambient bodies*, nor can true circular motion be defined by such translation. There is only one real circular motion of any one revolving body, corresponding to only one power of endeavouring to recede from its axis of motion, as its proper and adequate effect; but relative motions, in one and the same body, are innumerable, according to the various relations it bears to external bodies, and like other relations, are altogether destitute of any real effect, any otherwise than they may perhaps partake of that one only true motion...

For Newton, true absolute motion was related to the immutable absolute space, and different from the many measures of relative motion that depend on also the motion of the reference bodies. True physical effects, like the centrifugal force in circular motion, are manifest only during such true motion in the absolute space. For Newton, the absence of the curving of the water surface when the relative motion between the bucket and water was greatest, and the maximum curvature when the relative motion remained zero, were a sufficient proof for the absolute (true) motion, and by implication, for the reality of an absolute space. Herein contains the seed of the debate on the perennial question on the origin of inertia.

The same argument applies to the larger structures, like the Earth itself; its equatorial bulge is the result of the real centrifugal forces due to its rotation while it was solidifying. But, for us inhabitants of the Earth, there is no relative motion. What is the source of the enormous centrifugal force? Newton would reason that the bulge was the result of the Earth's rotation relative to an absolute space. But, attributing a physical effect to an unobservable entity, without a rational physical reason, was abhorrent for Mach.

## Interpretation and checks

At steady rotation, the horizontal pressure gradient supplies centripetal acceleration while vertical pressure supports the water's weight. A free surface is therefore higher farther from the rotation axis. The surface can stay curved after the bucket's relative motion to the water has changed. This rejects a simple bucket-relative criterion for rotation. Ask whether the same result uniquely chooses Newton's explanation over a theory with inertial frames defined by gravitational geometry.
