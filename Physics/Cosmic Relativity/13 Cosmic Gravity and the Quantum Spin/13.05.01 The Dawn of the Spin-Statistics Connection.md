> [!TLDR]
> Bose counting and Pauli exclusion established the two particle classes before their connection to spin was proved in field theory.

## Physical question and model

Why do Bose and Fermi occupation factors differ? Their state counting treats identical particles differently from distinguishable classical particles. The counting rule is an assumption at this stage, even when it successfully predicts macroscopic behaviour.

## Lecture account

Bose derived the radiation-state factor $8\pi\signal{\nu}^2/\mechanical{c}^3$ without classical radiation assumptions. His counting implicitly used indistinguishable quanta. After Bose contacted Einstein, Einstein recognised that the method extended to a monatomic gas. It predicted condensation of many noninteracting particles into one quantum state at finite temperature: Bose–Einstein condensation [31].

In 1925 Pauli introduced a prohibitive rule drawn from atomic spectra: no two equivalent electrons in an atom can have all quantum numbers equal [27]. His two-valued fourth quantum number was later identified with the two projections of half-integer spin.

By 1926, Bose–Einstein and Fermi–Dirac statistics described the two classes. Fermi treated an electron gas using Pauli exclusion as a constraint, deriving its equation of state and internal energy from that rule [32]. Dirac named the integer-spin particles bosons and set out the two behaviours in his 1926 paper [33].

Their distribution functions differ by a sign:

$$
\frac{1}{\exp(\energy{E}/(\thermal{k}_B\thermal{T}))-1}
\quad\text{for bosons},\qquad
\frac{1}{\exp(\energy{E}/(\thermal{k}_B\thermal{T}))+1}
\quad\text{for fermions}.
$$

Associated field theories use tensor fields for bosons and spinor fields for fermions. This connected spin, statistics, and rotation laws.

Electron, proton, and neutron exclusion helps explain the variety of elements. For bosons, sharing a state alone is not the distinctive quantum effect: classical Maxwell–Boltzmann particles can also share a state. The quantum signature is enhanced occupation of an already occupied state, or bunching. The minus sign becomes especially important at low temperature, as in condensation [31, 34].

Exclusion is visible from conduction in metals to white dwarfs and neutron stars. R. H. Fowler used it to explain dense-dwarf stability [35]. S. Chandrasekhar applied relativistic Fermi statistics to obtain the white-dwarf critical mass beyond which gravity overcomes this support [36].

F. London identified liquid-helium superfluidity with Bose–Einstein condensation [37]. A helium atom contains six fermions but behaves as a boson. The relevant spin is the total spin of the assembled entity, rather than the spin of each constituent. Superconductivity, interpreted through condensed electron Cooper pairs, further showed that even weakly bound fermion pairs can behave collectively as bosons.

Their net integer spin gives paired fermions bosonic behaviour. Later examples included masers and lasers for bosons, and neutron stars for fermions.

## Interpretation and checks

In a dilute gas, both occupation laws approach the same Boltzmann limit when their full exponent, including chemical potential, is large and positive. The displayed expressions suppress that chemical potential; state when this is appropriate. Bose condensation also depends on dimension, density of states, and confinement. Its occurrence is evidence for a statistical model, not a derivation of spin–statistics from counting alone.
