> [!TLDR]
> The source traces dynamics from Galileo's empirical principles through Newton's mathematical framework to later relativity theories.

## Physical question and model

What can the history of dynamics teach about model change? Follow changes in measurable questions, mathematical structure, and experimental reach. A sequence of influential theories is not itself an argument that the next proposed theory is correct.

## Lecture account

Galileo shifted natural philosophy from unverified Aristotelian principles towards experimentally testable descriptions. The source associates his main conflict with support for Copernican planetary motion and rejection of a uniquely privileged Earth.

Universality of free fall and the relativity principle for inertial motion were major Galilean results. Both were initially counterintuitive but supported by observation, and the author regards them as lasting foundations extending into general relativity.

Newton then systematised dynamics through axioms and mathematical deduction, while retaining continuity with Galileo and empirical results such as Kepler's laws. The source calls this a major advance in rigour and a possible paradigm shift towards the requirement to express physical statements mathematically. Newtonian mechanics becomes the starting point for the later development of dynamics and relativity.

Differential calculus enabled new formulations of the same laws. Lagrange described generalised coordinates through an abstract function; Hamilton expressed dynamics through stationary action. These principles covered systems as different as light and planets, extending physical scope beyond Newton's mechanical wording.

Newtonian mechanics combined absolute space and time, inverse-square gravity, and empirical motion laws. Galilean relativity related uniform observers, but accelerated frames required pseudo-forces. Their physical source and the origin of inertia remained unexplained.

Mach challenged Newton's absolute space. Pseudo-forces arise in nonuniform motion relative to surrounding matter, suggesting that inertia comes from interaction with that matter. The source notes that Mach did not specifically identify gravity, perhaps because known celestial matter appeared too weak. Even apart from a constructive mechanism, rejecting the necessity of absolute empty space enabled later relativity developments.

Nineteenth-century experiments studied light, colour, heat, electricity, and magnetism. Interferometry improved precision, while Faraday, Ampère, Weber, and others exposed electromagnetic relations. Maxwell consolidated this work and identified light as electromagnetic radiation.

Atoms and the ether were major added concepts. Chemistry supported discrete atoms; electrical phenomena suggested discrete charge. Transverse light waves appeared to require a rigid yet transparent medium that did not resist particle motion. This created tension between absolute empty space in mechanics and the stationary ether as a second spatial reference.

Mechanics and electromagnetism were therefore not fully integrated. Uniform motion through empty space was undetectable under Galilean relativity, but motion through a stationary ether seemed detectable through light propagation. Ether experiments gave negative results.

The author describes two interpretations: one retained ether, Galilean light propagation, and absolute space; the other rejected ether and physical absolute motion. Both used Lorentz transformations and were described as empirically supported, though the second put them in a broader framework. The source calls this a major foundational shift and criticises its reliance on indirectly tested postulates.

The author attributes the eventual preference for the STR partly to historical consensus rather than decisive evidence between those interpretations. Ether questions receded, but inertia remained unexplained. He also disputes the treatment of first-order light effects and simultaneity, arguing that attention centred on second-order time-dilation results shared by the competing accounts.

General relativity then extended the relativity programme using equivalence of accelerated frames and gravitational fields. The source frames Einstein's choice as one between arbitrary-coordinate covariance and a Machian cosmic reference for motion. It argues that the former left inertia without a physical source and treated gravity as removable inertial structure rather than identifying inertial forces as gravity.

The successful light-bending prediction helped establish the GTR before modern cosmology revealed the full cosmic matter distribution. The author argues that cosmic gravity should then have prompted a foundational revision. Instead, the accuracy of local-source predictions supported continued use of the existing theory. He regards it as incomplete concerning inertia and the physical cause of motional time dilation [4].

## Interpretation and checks

Distinguish a historical motivation from a physical derivation. Newtonian mechanics remains a controlled limit of relativistic dynamics in its domain, rather than a discarded set of useless equations. Apply the same standard to the cosmic proposal: name the limit in which it recovers existing results and the observable on which it differs. A narrative of increasing unification should end in a testable calculation.
