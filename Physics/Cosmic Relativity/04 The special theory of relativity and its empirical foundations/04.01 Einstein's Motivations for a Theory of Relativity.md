> The source examines the magnet–conductor example and other empirical motivations in Einstein's 1905 paper.

## Physical question and model

Why can a magnet–conductor experiment motivate a change in theory? One frame can describe the emf through a magnetic force and another through an electric field. The observation is the same current or voltage; the split into field components depends on the frame.

## Lecture account

Einstein's 1905 paper ‘On the electrodynamics of moving bodies’ mentioned two empirical facts as his motivations and another empirical fact as the starting point for introducing a new postulate [1]. Einstein's paper opens with the paragraph,

> It is known that Maxwell's electrodynamics—as usually understood at the present time—when applied to moving bodies, leads to asymmetries which do not appear to be inherent in the phenomena. Take, for example, the reciprocal electrodynamical action of a magnet and a conductor. The observable phenomenon here depends only on the relative motion of the conductor and the magnet, whereas the customary view draws a sharp distinction between the two cases in which either the one or the other of these bodies is in motion. For if the magnet is in motion and the conductor at rest, there arises in the neighbourhood of the magnet an electric field with a certain definite energy, producing a current at the places where parts of the conductor are situated. But if the magnet is stationary and the conductor in motion, no electric field arises in the neighbourhood of the magnet. In the conductor, however, we find an electromotive force, to which in itself there is no corresponding energy, but which gives rise, assuming equality of relative motion in the two cases discussed, to electric currents of the same path and intensity as those produced by the electric forces in the former case.

So, the primary motivation hinged on the phenomenon of the unipolar induction, which was mentioned in the previous chapter. The quantity in question is the electromotive force generated when there is relative motion between a conductor and a magnet. When the motion is linear, there is exact reciprocity—identical phenomena are observed when either the conducting wire or the magnet is moved. Only their *relative velocity* matters for the observed physical phenomenon of a current in the conductor. Pre-Einstein electrodynamics made a distinction, based on whether the field that drove the current was generated in the magnet or in the conductor, depending on which of the two ‘really’ moved. This of course goes well with the hypothesis of an ether medium; a motion is considered real only when it is relative to the all-pervading stationary ether. Einstein rejected this idea and insisted that there was no reason to invoke the notion of the real motion and only the relative motion was physically relevant, *because the observed phenomenon was the same irrespective whether the magnet moved or the conductor moved*. In other words, the concept of ‘absolute rest’ did not make physical sense in the STR. However, we already know that the symmetry of relative motion in the induction that motivated Einstein does not exist in general. The fact that Einstein was unaware of the full scope of the experimental results on the unipolar induction [2] was a crucial factor in the choice of the conceptual structure of the Special Theory of Relativity and in its subsequent developments.

Einstein's second motivation was the failure to observe the motion of the Earth relative to any master frame, like the ether. He did not explicitly refer to the Michelson-Morley experiment or any other, but mentioned this important empirical result clearly. It is possible that he was mostly referring to the first order effects like aberration. In any case, he called this persistent ‘failure’ to physically sense inertial motion as the Principle of Relativity (PoR), essentially identical to the statement of the PoR by Poincaré. Then he asserted that there was no reason to retain the concept of absolute rest and went ahead building a theory in which all physical effects depended only on relative velocities. Since the relative velocity between two inertial frames is symmetrical and reciprocal, there is complete equivalence and reciprocity between all inertial observers. Any of the observers in inertial motion can claim a state of rest and ascribe the entire motion, and any physical change due to the motion, to the other *relatively moving frames*. Thus, Einstein started his theory by rejecting the notion of any privileged (absolute) reference frame.

This posed a problem immediately about the propagation of light because, as Einstein stated, there was already sufficient empirical evidence to show that the velocity of light did not depend on the *velocity of the source*. Einstein was careful to state this precisely, as the independence of the velocity of light from the motion of the source. Einstein mentioned the astronomical evidence of light received from double stars for this fact (he wrote in a popular monograph that every school child knew this fact) [3, 4]. This is of course true for sound as well (many might not be aware of this, though) or for other familiar waves in a medium—the velocity of waves is determined by the properties of the relevant medium, and it does not depend on the velocity of the source. But, the relative velocity of sound does depend on the velocity of the observer. However, *having rejected any absolute frame, there was the requirement to make the propagation of light consistent with the Principle of Relativity*. Einstein thought that the PoR should entail that the relative velocity of light should be independent of the velocity of the observer as well, because he had asserted that only the relative motion between the source and the observer made any physical sense. In his scheme, there was no medium or a master frame that determined the velocity of motion uniquely, and distinguished between the motion of a source and the motion of an observer. *This is perhaps the most important turning point in the history of modern physics, when he went ahead and postulated that the velocity of light relative to any inertial observer was an invariant constant*.

The relative velocity of light in a particular reference frame is the distance of propagation divided by the duration of propagation, as measured in that frame. Thus, it is a hybrid physical quantity involving the fundamental intervals of both space and time. From the Galilean transformation of the spatial coordinate, which is the common empirical experience, one already knows that a point $\mechanical{x}'$ in the frame moves through the distance $\mechanical{v}t$ relative to a stationary frame. Then the coordinate $\mechanical{x}$ of the moving point in the stationary frame is $\mechanical{x}=\mechanical{x}'+\mechanical{v}t$. Clearly, the spatial distances ($\mechanical{\Delta x}$ and $\mechanical{\Delta x}'$) are different in the two frames. Therefore, the only way the relative velocity of light remains the same in all frames, independent of their motion, is when a similar velocity-dependent transformation applies for time intervals as well. Thus, *the key to an invariant relative velocity of light is the new behaviour of clocks and time in frames that are relatively moving*. A true time that depends on the velocity of motion was an entirely new concept in physics. From the light hypothesis, Einstein derived the Lorentz transformations (LT), which were already familiar in the mathematical structure of Lorentz's theory [5].

## Interpretation and checks

A successful relativistic model transforms fields, charges, and conductor velocities together. Test it by calculating the same circuit readout in both frames. The equality of outcomes motivates covariance; it does not demand that each intermediate force component be identical. Historical motivation also differs from a logically sufficient derivation: state every additional postulate needed to reach the transformation law.
