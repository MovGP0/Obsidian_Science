## Physical question and model

Why can a transverse Doppler shift appear with either sign in different formulas? The physical angle must be assigned to a specified frame. A transverse emission direction in the source frame is generally not transverse reception in the observer frame because of aberration.

## Lecture account

The expression derived by Einstein for the Doppler shift deserves a special mention. The familiar nonrelativistic expression is modified by the time dilation factor. Einstein derived the formula for the frequency of radiation perceived by “an observer moving with the velocity $\mechanical{v}$ relative to an infinitely distant source”, at the angle $\mechanical{\theta}$ relative to a source, as

$$
\signal{\nu}'=\signal{\nu}\frac{1-(\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c})\cos\mechanical{\theta}}{\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}}. \tag{4.17}
$$

Then Einstein focussed attention to the case when $\mechanical{\phi}=0$. The equation becomes

$$
\signal{\nu}'=\signal{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{1-\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}}{1+\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}}}. \tag{4.18}
$$

Einstein commented that the perceived frequency became infinite for the velocity $\mechanical{v}=-\mechanical{c}$, “contrary to the usual conception.” There is a serious ambiguity in the fact that Einstein *distinguishes the observer's real motion from the real motion of the source in writing the formula* Eq. (4.17) above. But his theory respects only the relative velocity between two frames, and rejects any absolute reference or real motion! The fact is that the Eq. (4.17) is valid only when the source is stationary and the observer is in real motion, whereas *another distinct formula is required when the observer is stationary and the source is in motion*. If the observer is moving at a velocity approaching the velocity of light, the time dilation factor diverges. As the derivation in the previous chapter shows, the formula in the latter case (when the source is moving towards the observer) is different,

$$
\signal{\nu}'=\signal{\nu}\frac{\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}}{1-(\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c})\cos\mechanical{\theta}}. \tag{4.19}
$$

When the angle $\mechanical{\phi}=0$, this formula simplifies to

$$
\signal{\nu}'=\signal{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{1+\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}}{1-\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}}}. \tag{4.20}
$$

For the special case $\mechanical{\phi}=0$, the important difference between the two expressions (source moving towards the observer or the observer moving towards the source) cannot be discerned. However, when $\mechanical{\phi}\approx\pi/2$, Einstein formula for the perceived frequency is

$$
\signal{\nu}'=\signal{\nu}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}}\approx\signal{\nu}(1+\mechanical{v}^2/2\mechanical{c}^2). \tag{4.21}
$$

Thus, according to Einstein, the frequency perceived by the observer is more than the frequency at relative rest. But the spectral shift that is confirmed in experiments, like the Ives-Stilwell experiment, directly contradicts this. The spectral shift in experiments agrees with a very different formula

$$
\signal{\nu}'=\signal{\nu}\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}\approx\signal{\nu}(1-\mechanical{v}^2/2\mechanical{c}^2). \tag{4.22}
$$

*This is a reduction in the perceived frequency*, as expected only when the source is in real motion, which is how the experiment is implemented practically. Einstein's formula for the frequency perceived by the observer (detector) in relative motion in the STR has a serious ambiguity because *the velocity $\mechanical{v}$ in the STR is always the relative velocity between two inertial frames*. Thus, one does not distinguish between the motion of the source and the motion of the observer, who is always the one who detects and perceives. An agreement between the theory of relativity and the experimental result is possible only by recognising the physical difference between the real motion of the source and the motion of the observer. All the experiments use a moving source and a stationary detector. The STR does not differentiate between this situation and the reciprocal one where the observer is in motion and the source is stationary. But, the experimental results respect the distinction between the motion of the source and the receiver. Thus, these results seem to refute Einstein's STR formula with the relative velocity.

In fact, when Einstein noticed the observations of the Doppler shift of the radiation from canal rays (consisting of accelerated Hydrogen ions) by Stark (1905–06), Einstein wrote a short paper in 1907, suggesting that the quadratic dependence predicted by his theory could be observed in the radiation from the canal rays [8]. Stark had already reported *a reduction in the detected frequency, proportional to the square of the velocity*, when the radiation is observed in a direction perpendicular to the motion of the ions. In his paper, *Einstein employed the argument of the time dilation of the moving source to correctly represent the reduction in the perceived frequency*. Einstein used the same argument in his ‘Jahrbuch’ article as well, in 1907 [9]. Thus, inadvertently, Einstein was distinguishing the real motion of the source and the real motion of the observer, a distinction that is absent in the STR, but characteristic of a theory of relativity in a preferred absolute frame. Without specifying that distinction, Einstein's expression (Eq. (4.17)) in the STR is contradicted by the results of experiments. (I note here that Laue had derived a different expression for the frequency shift in his textbook on relativity, including an additional effect of aberration when the observer is moving transversely. His formula matches what is observed in experiments. This might resolve the discrepancy.)

In the Jahrbuch review article, Einstein included two separate equations for the Doppler shift of the frequency as perceived by the observer, one for the observer moving and another for the source moving. The first was identical to the Eq. (4.17) above and the equation when the source is in motion was the ‘inverse’ expression Eq. (4.19). Thus, Einstein was in effect distinguishing the absolute motion of the source and the observer, even though the article was on his STR in which such a distinction did not exist. While he wrote the correct formulae applicable in the two situations, his explanation reveals an inconsistency. He wrote,

> The last two equations express Doppler's principle in its general form; the last equation shows how the observable frequency of the light emitted (or absorbed) by canal rays depends on the velocity of motion of the ions that form the rays and on the direction of sighting.

Here, Einstein ignores that the atom is the moving source when it emits the radiation, but the atom assumes the role of the moving observer when it absorbs the radiation. The clocks that undergo the time dilation are in different moving frames in the two cases. The case in which the atoms are the absorbing observers of radiation should show an increase in the perceived frequency, and not a decrease indicated in the formula Eq. (4.19). However, as mentioned above, the effect of aberration might mask the distinction.

## Interpretation and checks

Choose a collinear receding source first: the observed frequency must be lower. Then compare the two distinct transverse geometries, including angle transformation. Frequency is a rate measured by a local clock, so transform both phase and proper time. Two formulas written with the same angle symbol need not describe the same experiment. This removes an apparent source–observer asymmetry without assuming a preferred frame.
