> [!TLDR]
> Integrating proper time along a clock’s path gives elapsed time; the physical meaning of velocity differs between relative-frame and absolute-frame theories.

## Physical question and model

How should the time-dilation law be used for several moving clocks? Choose one coordinate chart, express every worldline and the same comparison endpoints in it, and integrate each proper-time increment. Transform the metric and path together if changing charts.

## Lecture account

The elementary moving-clock time at velocity $\mechanical{v}(t)$ is

$$
d\mechanical{\tau}=\sqrt{1-\frac{\mechanical{v}^2(t)}{\mechanical{c}^2}}\,dt. \tag{9.2}
$$

Its path integral $\int_{\mathrm{path}}d\mechanical{\tau}$ gives total clock time $\mechanical{\tau}$. The theories differ in the physical status of the two frames and velocity. In the STR, $\mechanical{v}(t)$ is the velocity of clock $C(\mechanical{\tau})$ in frame $O_\tau$ relative to a reference inertial frame $O_t$, chosen at rest, where clock $C(t)$ marks time. That reference clock has no dilation by choice, so the path integral is the relative time when $C(t)$ is the reference. Since inertial frames are equivalent, the description is symmetric: choose $O_\tau$ at rest and its clock is undilated, while moving $C(t)$ reads

$$
dt=\sqrt{1-\frac{\mechanical{v}^2(t)}{\mechanical{c}^2}}\,d\mechanical{\tau}. \tag{9.3}
$$

All STR relative dilation is quadratic in instantaneous relative speed. The clock selected as resting marks the fastest time and ages fastest. *Because rest-frame selection is arbitrary, no physical reason can be assigned to STR time dilation*; either clock can be chosen to run relatively slower.

In an absolute-frame theory, such as the Universe frame, dilation is physical and tied directly to the preferred frame’s properties. Velocity $\mechanical{v}(t)$ means real motion relative to that unique frame, so there is no ambiguity about which clocks dilate. The physical interaction changing a moving clock’s rate must still be explained, as Chapter 11 does. If $C(t')$ moves relative to a reference frame, inertial or otherwise, at $\mechanical{v}'(t)$, and that frame moves through the master frame at $\mechanical{u}(t)$, then

$$
d\mechanical{\tau}=\sqrt{1-\frac{\mechanical{v}^2(t)}{\mechanical{c}^2}}\,dt=\sqrt{1-\frac{|\mechanical{\mathbf v}'+\mechanical{\mathbf u}|^2}{\mechanical{c}^2}}\,dt=\sqrt{1-\frac{\mechanical{v}'^2+\mechanical{u}^2+2\mechanical{\mathbf u}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf v}'}{\mechanical{c}^2}}\,dt. \tag{9.4}
$$

Here $C_t$ is at rest in the absolute frame, unlike laboratory clock $C_{t'}$, moving at $\mechanical{u}(t)$. Its dilated time is

$$
dt'=\sqrt{1-\frac{\mechanical{u}^2(t)}{\mechanical{c}^2}}\,dt. \tag{9.5}
$$

Relative dilation between $C_\tau$ and the laboratory reference is

$$
d\mechanical{\tau}-dt'=\sqrt{1-\frac{\mechanical{v}'^2+\mechanical{u}^2+2\mechanical{\mathbf u}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf v}'}{\mechanical{c}^2}}\,dt-\sqrt{1-\frac{\mechanical{u}^2}{\mechanical{c}^2}}\,dt. \tag{9.6}
$$

For laboratory and clock speeds much below $\mechanical{c}$,

$$
1-\frac{\mechanical{v}'^2+\mechanical{u}^2+2\mechanical{\mathbf u}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf v}'}{2\mechanical{c}^2}-\left(1-\frac{\mechanical{u}^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}\right)=-\frac{\mechanical{v}'^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}-\frac{\mechanical{\mathbf u}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf v}'}{\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{9.7}
$$

The bilinear term in laboratory-relative clock velocity and absolute laboratory velocity is the crucial difference enabling a decisive test. But comparing clocks requires initial synchronisation together, transport at $\mechanical{v}'(t')=d\mechanical{x}'/dt'$, and return near the reference for another comparison. If the laboratory moves steadily at $\mechanical{\mathbf u}$, the term becomes

$$
\oint-\frac{\mechanical{\mathbf u}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf v}'}{\mechanical{c}^2}\,dt'=\oint-\frac{\mechanical{\mathbf u}\cdot d\mechanical{\mathbf x}'}{\mechanical{c}^2}=0. \tag{9.8}
$$

It vanishes for any closed path returning to its start. Reversing transport to compare with the laboratory clock therefore hides the absolute-frame term.

Consider an atomic clock taken from Mumbai to Paris at ground speed $1000\,\unit{km/h}$ and returned at $300\,\unit{km/h}$. The STR uses $-\mechanical{v}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)$ at these relative speeds and durations. Over the roughly $7000\,\unit{km}$ outward trip, speed $278\,\unit{m/s}$ gives about $0.43\,\unit{ps/s}$. Seven hours accumulates $10.8\,\unit{ns}$, measurable by a transportable atomic clock, but not directly testable in Paris since the reference remains in Mumbai. Return speed $83\,\unit{m/s}$ gives $0.039\,\unit{ps/s}$; over $23.33\,\unit{h}$ this accumulates $3.2\,\unit{ns}$. Total dilation is about $14\,\unit{ns}$. *The STR says the transported clock is slower than the laboratory reference throughout the journey.*

The absolute-frame scenario differs. Mumbai’s laboratory moves through the Universe at about $\mechanical{u}=390\,\unit{km/s}$, roughly in Earth’s orbital plane. Suppose Earth’s orbital velocity, $\mechanical{v}=30\,\unit{km/s}$, opposes this cosmic velocity during the experiment. The laboratory’s global velocity is $\mechanical{u}_0=\mechanical{u}-\mechanical{v}=360\,\unit{km/s}$. Earth’s rotation adds about $\mechanical{u}_r=450\,\unit{m/s}$, changing direction relative to the global velocity. All clocks initially share this cosmic-frame speed.

The laboratory reference has dilation $720\,\unit{ns/s}$ from its global speed of $360\,\unit{km/s}$. In the roughly $30.3\,\unit{h}$ total journey, it loses $0.78\,\unit{s}$ relative to a hypothetical absolute-rest clock. More accurately, laboratory speed is $\mechanical{u}=|\mechanical{\mathbf u}_0+\mechanical{\mathbf u}_r|$. The source writes

$$
\frac{\mechanical{v}_{\mathrm{ref}}^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}=\frac{\mechanical{u}_0^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}+\frac{\mechanical{u}_r^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}+\frac{\mechanical{u}_0\mechanical{u}_r\cos\mechanical{\omega}t+\mechanical{\theta}}{\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{9.9}
$$

> [!note]
> The source prints $+\mechanical{\theta}$ outside the cosine in equation (9.9). As a phase angle, it would belong inside $\cos(\mechanical{\omega}t+\mechanical{\theta})$; outside, the numerator adds quantities with incompatible units. The printed form is retained.

The variable term $(\mechanical{u}_0\mechanical{u}_r\cos\mechanical{\omega}t)/\mechanical{c}^2$, with Earth’s rotational frequency $\mechanical{\omega}$, contributes at most $\pm1.8\,\unit{ns/s}$.

For the transported clock, total absolute speed is $\mechanical{v}'=|\mechanical{\mathbf u}_0+\mechanical{\mathbf u}_r+\mechanical{\mathbf v}|$. We have

$$
\frac{\mechanical{v}'^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}=\frac{\mechanical{u}_0^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}+\frac{\mechanical{u}_r^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}+\frac{\mechanical{v}^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}+\frac{\mechanical{u}_0\mechanical{u}_r\cos\mechanical{\omega}t}{\mechanical{c}^2}+\frac{\mechanical{u}_0\mechanical{v}\cos[(\mechanical{\omega}\pm\mechanical{\delta})t]}{\mechanical{c}^2}\pm\frac{\mechanical{u}_r\mechanical{v}\cos\mechanical{\phi}}{\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{9.10}
$$

Here $\mechanical{\phi}$ is the angle between rotational and transport velocities. The signs identify transport directions. Only the difference in accumulated durations is measurable. The difference of dilation factors is

$$
\frac{\mechanical{v}'^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}-\frac{\mechanical{v}_{\mathrm{ref}}^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}=\frac{\mechanical{v}^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}+\frac{\mechanical{u}_0\mechanical{v}\cos[(\mechanical{\omega}\pm\mechanical{\delta})t]}{\mechanical{c}^2}\pm\frac{\mechanical{u}_r\mechanical{v}}{\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{9.11}
$$

> [!note]
> Equation (9.11) omits the factor $\cos\mechanical{\phi}$ present in (9.10); the following discussion restores it. The source does not explicitly state an alignment assumption at this step.

Large common absolute dilations remain hidden in the clocks, since only their difference is measurable. Ignore the oscillatory middle term temporarily. For east-to-west transport, as from Mumbai to Paris, relative dilation is $\mechanical{v}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)-\mechanical{u}_r\mechanical{v}\cos\mechanical{\phi}/\mechanical{c}^2$. If $\mechanical{u}_r\cos\mechanical{\phi}>\mechanical{v}/2$, as here, the transported clock runs faster and the stationary clock ages less. This contradicts the STR’s always-slower transported clock. Only an absolute-frame theory permits a travelling twin to return older. At $1000\,\unit{km/h}$, the outward clock gains about $19\,\unit{ns}$ relative to the reference. Bringing it back reverses the term: it then runs slower than the STR prediction by the same $19\,\unit{ns}$, hiding the evidence.

Reversing transport changes the signs of the last two terms, leaving only the first in total. Cancellation of the velocity-linear terms does not depend on speed: slower transport takes longer, leaving $\mechanical{v}t$, the distance, unchanged. Thus, returning clocks by reversing their trajectories conceals the history of relative dilation. The remaining $\mechanical{v}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)$ term agrees with the STR. Such return experiments cannot distinguish the theories; one-way precision-clock transport is crucial.

Clocks can reunite without reversing by travelling around Earth in the same sense. The oscillatory term cancels over the circuit, but the final term retains its positive or negative sign. This can distinguish the theories: its absence excludes an absolute motion frame; a measured contribution $\pm\mechanical{u}_r\mechanical{v}\cos\mechanical{\phi}/\mechanical{c}^2$ falsifies the STR. This is the key comparison.

For circuit duration $\mechanical{T}$, the final term accumulates $\pm\mechanical{T}\mechanical{u}_r\mechanical{v}\cos\mechanical{\phi}/\mechanical{c}^2=\mechanical{u}_r\mechanical{L}\cos\mechanical{\phi}/\mechanical{c}^2$, where $\mechanical{L}$ is Earth’s circumference. This matches Lorentz’s prescription that makes Galilean relative light speed look invariant. With $\mechanical{L}\approx40{,}000\,\unit{km}$, it is $\pm200\,\unit{ns}$ at the equator. STR dilation at speeds below $1000\,\unit{km/h}$ is $\mechanical{T}\mechanical{v}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)=\mechanical{L}\mechanical{v}/(2\mechanical{c}^2)$, below $62\,\unit{ns}$. Thus, *total relative dilation can have either sign, and a westward-circling clock can run faster than the stationary reference in an absolute-frame theory*. Only then can the traveller return older.

> [!note]
> If motional dilation depends on absolute velocity in a privileged frame such as the matter-filled Universe, a stationary Earth laboratory clock can dilate relative to a clock carried around the globe. A transported clock running faster than its stationary reference refutes the STR in the author’s argument.

We can now examine experimental data to choose between an absolute matter-filled-Universe frame and Einstein’s STR.

> [!note]
> The earlier source estimate of $0.78\,\unit{s}$ for the reference clock over $30.3\,\unit{h}$ is inconsistent with its stated rate $720\,\unit{ns/s}$. Their product is about $0.0785\,\unit{s}$. The original value is retained in the example.

## Interpretation and checks

The inverse of a clock-rate relation is not the same relation with its symbols exchanged; equation (9.3) repeats a factor where its reciprocal is needed. Galilean addition inside an exact relativistic square root is also not an exact change of inertial frame. In the slow-speed expansion, a constant reference velocity crossed with displacement integrates to zero on a closed spatial path. If the reference velocity varies, that cancellation requires further analysis.
