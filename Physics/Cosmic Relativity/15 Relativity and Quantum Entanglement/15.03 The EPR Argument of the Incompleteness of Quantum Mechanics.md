title: "The EPR Argument of the Incompleteness of Quantum Mechanics"

> [!TLDR]
> The EPR argument asks whether a local theory is complete when one can predict a distant particle's outcome without measuring it directly.

## Physical question and model

What conclusion follows from the EPR argument, and under which premises? EPR combines a criterion for elements of reality with locality and perfect prediction. Its incompleteness conclusion is conditional on that combination, rather than a direct consequence of correlation alone.

## Lecture account

Einstein's EPR argument [1, 5] examines whether a theory represents every real physical state. If a one-to-one correspondence between physical reality and theoretical representation is absent, the theory is called incomplete.

Two particles interact, separate, and are described by the nonseparable state $\Psi=\alpha\psi_{A+}\psi_{B-}+\beta\psi_{A-}\psi_{B+}$. If measuring A gives the negative outcome, the standard reduction rule selects $\psi_{A-}\psi_{B+}$. One can then predict a positive same-axis outcome at B without measuring B. A later measurement can check that prediction.

The source stresses that before either observation the pair has a joint entangled wavefunction, while neither particle has an individual pure-state wavefunction.

The source distinguishes the theory's instantaneous conditional wavefunction change from a necessary change in nature. In his version of the EPR reasoning, strict locality is assumed: a distant observation cannot instantly change a particle's real physical state. He does not require that particle to possess definite values for every observable.

If the formal wavefunction at B changes when A is measured, but B's real state cannot change through that distant action, then wavefunctions cannot stand in a one-to-one correspondence with real states. This is the sense of incompleteness the author attributes to Einstein.

Einstein reportedly found the published 1935 paper less clear than his own intended argument [7]. The source paraphrases *Physics and Reality* [1]: after two subsystems interact and separate, measurements on A permit different wavefunction assignments to B depending on the observable selected at A. Since B's real state cannot depend on that distant choice, those different assignments cannot each be a unique complete representation of B.

The author lays out three steps:

1. Different wavefunctions would represent different complete physical states.
2. A free choice of measurement at A selects which conditional wavefunction is assigned to B, such as a position or momentum state.
3. Locality prevents that choice from changing B's real state. Therefore the wavefunction is not a unique complete representation.

The source stresses that this argument concerns representational correspondence, not hidden definite values for all observables. Einstein's *Quantum Mechanics and Reality* [8] describes an entangled joint wavefunction, a sum of products rather than one product. Its support can lie in separated regions while individual subsystem wavefunctions are absent initially.

The author argues that later discussion confused this meaning of incompleteness with Einstein's separate concerns about indeterminism and with a demand for a new classical statistical theory assigning every variable a definite value.

Von Neumann used incompleteness in a different sense in *The Mathematical Foundations of Quantum Mechanics* [10]: one initial wavefunction permits several unpredictable outcomes. He asked whether added hidden parameters could restore deterministic dynamics and answered negatively for the class considered.

The author calls this causal incompleteness, distinct from Einstein's representational argument [11]. He argues that later discussion confused the two [12], although the EPR paper does not explicitly discuss indeterminism, hidden variables, or added statistical parameters. He cites Einstein–Born correspondence and Pauli's comments on it [13].

In the author's reading, Bell addressed hidden-variable causality but attributed the motivation to EPR by conflating these meanings of completeness. The source strongly criticises that historical interpretation. It next reviews hidden-variable correlations, proposes a conservation-law objection, and derives its own local action-wave correlations as outlined in Chapter 12.

## Interpretation and checks

Keep the historical completeness question separate from the later empirical Bell question. An ensemble interpretation can avoid treating state update as a literal physical collapse, but it must still specify joint outcome probabilities. Perfect same-axis prediction does not by itself settle correlations at different axes. Ask which premise an alternative theory changes and how that change affects a measurable joint distribution.
