> [!TLDR]
> Spin-½ measurements give discrete outcomes, while their ensemble average varies continuously with the angle between preparation and measurement directions.

## Physical question and model

How is an observed spin correlation constructed? Assign each paired trial a product of two normalised outcomes and average over the selected pairs. Conditional averages must be weighted by the fractions of trials in their subsets.

## Lecture account

For a spin component, outcomes are $+1$ and $-1$ in units of $\energy{\hbar}/2$. Their individual sequence is random, but their mean depends on the initial state and analyser direction.

If spin is prepared along unit direction $\mathbf n$ and measured along unit direction $\mathbf a$, repeated identically prepared particles give the mean $S_a=\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf a$. Quantum theory predicts this empirical average correctly.

![[cosmic-relativity-15-02-separated-spin-measurements.svg|**Fig. 15.2** A zero-total-spin source sends particles to independent analysers A and B. Local clocks record the measurements. The analyser directions differ by θ. This explanatory drawing replaces the source clock-face images.]]

Let a zero-total-spin source send identical particles in opposite directions to A and B. Independently chosen analyser directions have angles $\theta_A$ and $\theta_B$, with $\theta_A-\theta_B=\theta$. Each trial gives $A_i=\pm1$ and $B_i=\pm1$. The measured correlation is the mean product:

$$
C_e(\theta)=\frac1N\sum_i A_iB_i.
$$

The source claims that conservation of angular momentum alone requires $C_e(\theta)=-\cos\theta$ [14], independently of quantum theory. At equal settings, opposite outcomes give $C_e(0)=-1$.

For a general angle, the author divides the records into equal-sized subsets with $A_i=+1$ and $A_i=-1$. His printed relation is

$$
C_e(A,B,\theta)=\frac1N\sum_i A_iB_i
=\frac1{N/2}\sum_i(A_iB_i)|_{A_i=+1}
+\frac1{N/2}\sum_i(A_iB_i)|_{A_i=-1}. \tag{15.6}
$$

He assigns conditional means at B of $-\cos\theta$ and $+\cos\theta$, respectively. Averaging the two subsets then gives

$$
C_e=\frac12\{(+1)(-\cos\theta)+(-1)(+\cos\theta)\}=-\cos\theta.
$$

The source calls this an empirical conservation constraint. A viable theory must therefore yield $C_T=-\cos\theta$ to experimental precision. Quantum mechanics gives $C_{qm}=-\cos\theta$, but the author seeks a physical local account beyond the joint wavefunction.

> [!note]
> The right side of the printed equation (15.6) lacks the factor 1/2 weighting each subset mean. The following calculation includes it. The conditional angular averages are an additional step in the author's argument and are stated explicitly here rather than hidden in the word conservation.

De Broglie and Bohm sought deterministic hidden-variable accounts of apparently random outcomes. The source compares this with an unpredictable coin toss governed by definite classical conditions. A local hidden-variable theory (LHVT) assigns different hidden parameter values to different trials, preserving locality and deterministic dynamics. The author distinguishes this causal-completeness question from EPR representational completeness. He regards experiment as a direct test of whether an LHVT reproduces the measured correlation, rather than as a test that presumes quantum theory.

## Interpretation and checks

For an ideal singlet, opposite analyser directions give correlation plus one, equal directions give minus one, and orthogonal directions give zero. These are useful sign checks. The displayed subset sum omits its weighting factors, as recorded below. Conservation explains perfect equal-axis anticorrelation for the prepared singlet, but does not alone derive the cosine for every pair of axes. That step also uses the quantum state and measurement probabilities.
