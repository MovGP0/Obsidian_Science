---
title: "The Quantum Interference of Single Particles"
---

> [!TLDR]
> The source attributes interference from independently arriving particles to local interference of the action waves associated with their possible paths.

## Physical question and model

How do two coherent alternatives produce an interference pattern? Specify their amplitudes, relative action phase, and detector response. The intensity follows from the modulus squared of the summed complex amplitudes.

## Lecture account

The author proposes that local action-wave interference determines quantum arrival patterns even when indivisible particles arrive one at a time, independently.

Consider two paths, from A and B to a common location C. Aperture sizes or filters divide an ensemble of $N$ particles so that $a^2N$ use path 1 and the rest use path 2. The action-wave intensities have ratio $a^2:b^2$. The ensemble wavefunction coefficients are positive amplitudes $a=+\sqrt{\signal{\rho}_1}$ and $b=+\sqrt{\signal{\rho}_2}$. Phase signs belong to the action waves rather than to these real ensemble weights.

The source writes the two action contributions as $a\exp(i\energy{S}/\energy{\hbar})$ and $b\exp(i\energy{S}/\energy{\hbar})$. After evolution along their paths, they combine at C.

The source writes the combined action wave as

$$
\signal{\zeta}_B(\energy{S})=a\exp\left(\frac{i\energy{S}(a)}{\energy{\hbar}}\right)+b\exp\left(\frac{i\energy{S}(b)}{\energy{\hbar}}\right). \tag{12.22}
$$

Its intensity is

$$
\signal{\zeta}(\energy{S})\signal{\zeta}(\energy{S})^*=a^2+b^2+ab\exp\left(\frac{i\energy{S}(a)-i\energy{S}(b)}{\energy{\hbar}}\right)+ab\exp\left(\frac{-i\energy{S}(a)+i\energy{S}(b)}{\energy{\hbar}}\right). \tag{12.23}
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\signal{\zeta}(\energy{S})|^2&=a^2+b^2+ab\left[\exp\left(\frac{i[\energy{S}(b)-\energy{S}(a)]}{\energy{\hbar}}\right)+\exp\left(-\frac{i[\energy{S}(b)-\energy{S}(a)]}{\energy{\hbar}}\right)\right]\\
&=\signal{\rho}_1+\signal{\rho}_2+2\sqrt{\signal{\rho}_1\signal{\rho}_2}\cos\left(\frac{\energy{S}(b)-\energy{S}(a)}{\energy{\hbar}}\right).
\end{aligned} \tag{12.24}
$$

The source states that ensemble particle density follows this intensity. Local changes to either path action change $\Delta\energy{S}$ and hence the periodic interference pattern. Each particle is detected as one localised object, but repeated histories accumulate a pattern determined by action-wave overlap from all available paths. The author presents this as resolving Feynman's “only mystery” [11].

For spatial interferometry, $\energy{S}=\int\mechanical{p}\,d\mechanical{x}$ gives the usual double-slit or Mach–Zehnder forms. The author describes probabilities from zero to one as mapped onto the tiny action scale $\energy{\hbar}$. He attributes all observed periodicity to the action waves, including in photon experiments. In this interpretation, a material particle has neither wavelength nor frequency. If momentum is sharply defined, action periodicity can be expressed as a spatial wavelength $\signal{\lambda}=\energy{h}/\mechanical{p}$, but he rejects interpreting that wavelength as a physical matter wave.

> [!note]
> Equation (12.24) is the combined intensity as written. If the path weights sum to one, its maximum can still exceed one, for example reaching two for equal weights and equal phases. Conversion to a normalised probability for a specified output requires the appropriate apparatus or ensemble normalisation.

The source extends the interpretation to correlated particles. It rejects an instantaneous nonlocal influence and argues that the relevant joint wavefunction describes an ensemble rather than one pair. Correlations are attributed to local action-wave interference [1]. The author links this to cosmic time, simultaneity, and signal propagation, and postpones the detailed argument until after the chapters on spin and quantum transport.

## Interpretation and checks

For equal amplitudes, the interference term can make a maximum or a node as the relative phase changes. Averaging an uncontrolled uniform phase removes that term. The printed formulas use real path weights; general complex weights need conjugation. A detected particle at one output does not by itself show which interpretation of the propagating amplitude is correct. The proposed action-wave model must also explain which-path marking and loss or recovery of visibility.
