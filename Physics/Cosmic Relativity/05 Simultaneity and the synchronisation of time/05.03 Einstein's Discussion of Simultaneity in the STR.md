> [!TLDR]
> The source compares light-signal arrivals in relatively moving frames and argues that the light postulate produces a contradiction.

## Physical question and model

How does Einstein synchronisation assign a remote time using local measurements? Two-way light timing fixes the midpoint convention for the reflection event. A second inertial observer applies that rule to a different family of comoving clocks, so equal-time slices need not agree.

## Lecture account

Einstein discussed extensively the notion of simultaneity and its importance for the synchronisation of spatially separated clocks, both in his 1905 relativity paper and in the expository monograph, ‘Relativity: the Special and General Theory’ [6]. Since the postulate of the universal constancy of the relative velocity of light is entirely different from the nature of the Galilean waves, we expect a drastic difference between the Galilean simultaneity and Einstein's formulation of simultaneity.

Einstein discussed the core question of the relativity of simultaneity with the help of an example [6]:

> We suppose a very long train travelling along the rails with the constant velocity $\mechanical{v}$... Then every event which takes place along the line also takes place at a particular point of the train. ... As a natural consequence, however, the following question arises:
>
> Are two events (e.g. the two strokes of lightning A and B) which are simultaneous with reference to the railway embankment also simultaneous relatively to the train?

We know already our unambiguous answer to Einstein's query if the propagation of light is Galilean, like the propagation of sound, with a relative velocity that varies with the velocity of the observer ([[05.02 Galilean Simultaneity|Section 5.2]]). To repeat, *we found that simultaneity is relative when the messenger waves are Galilean waves*. Two events that an inertial observer O experiences as locally simultaneous are experienced as successive by another relatively moving observer O′. This is because, if the relative velocity of the waves is isotropic in one frame O, it cannot be isotropic in the rest frame of O′ who is relatively moving. Now we examine the temporal history of the events and the determination of simultaneity relative to each of these observers, assuming the validity of the light hypothesis of the STR.

![[cosmic-relativity-05-03-invariant-light-arrivals.svg|**Fig. 5.3** Temporal status of signals from events at separated locations A and B, according to relatively moving observers O and O′ in the source's STR analysis. Light has the same constant relative speed c for both observers. The events happen when O and O′ coincide at M, the midpoint of AB.]]

Let the observers coincide at the midpoint M when the events happen at the locations A and B, exactly as before. O and O′ are relatively moving; O′ is moving relative to O at the velocity $\mechanical{v}$ towards B, and O is moving relative to O′ at the velocity $-\mechanical{v}$ towards A (Fig. 5.3).

In the rest frame of the observer O′, the light signals from A and B arrive at the same relative speed $\mechanical{c}$. *Since the relative motion of the sources subsequent to the emission of the light is not relevant in the analysis, we need not bother about any relative motion of the sources*. Since A and B are equidistant when the events occurred, the light signals from A and B will reach the observer O′ simultaneously, after the delay $\delta t'=\overline{AB}/(2\mechanical{c})=\mechanical{L}/(2\mechanical{c})$.

In the rest frame of the observer O, the light signals from A and B arrive at the same relative speed $\mechanical{c}$. Since A and B are equidistant when the events occurred, the light signals from A and B will reach the observer O after the identical delays $\delta t=\mechanical{L}/(2\mechanical{c})$, i.e., simultaneously.

If the events happen at locations that are at unequal distances $\mechanical{d}_A$ and $\mechanical{d}_B$ from M, the signals will each in succession, after delays $\mechanical{d}_A/\mechanical{c}$ and $\mechanical{d}_B/\mechanical{c}$. But the light hypothesis guarantees that the order of the events and the quantitative lack of simultaneity will be identical for both observers. Therefore, *the definite answer to Einstein's query is that two events (e.g. the two strokes of lightning A and B) which are simultaneous with reference to O are also simultaneous relatively to O′, assuming the light hypothesis*.

Thus, we see that while a Galilean propagation of light, as it happens when an absolute reference frame really exists, leads to the relativity of simultaneity, the light hypothesis in the STR leads to universal simultaneity [5]. This latter result just reaffirms the central statement of Einstein's relativity, that all inertial observers are completely equivalent and that there is no physical sense in a privileged state of rest.

But, one can already sense the discomfort and even a sense of an imminent catastrophe lurking in this result of equal status for both observers, regarding simultaneity in the STR. When the light signals reach, after delay $\mechanical{L}/(2\mechanical{c})$, the two observers are not at the same spatial location; their relative motion at the velocity $\mechanical{v}$ separate them by the distance $\mechanical{d}_{OO'}=\mechanical{v}\mechanical{L}/(2\mechanical{c})$. How can two light signals, which started out at equal distances and travelling at equal speeds for both O and O′, arrive simultaneously at the two different locations separated by the distance $\mechanical{d}_{OO'}$? That does not sound like consistent physics. In fact, it is impossible. But, that is the conclusion reached from the rigorous application of the light hypothesis of the STR. The steps and the algebra involved are so elementary that we cannot escape the straightforward conclusion, which is nevertheless fatally destructive for the theory.

For the observer O′, the other observer O is moving away from the midpoint M′ of the simultaneous events. But, for the observer O, it is O′ who is moving away from the midpoint. So, *each observer would reckon that the other would receive the light flashes at a point that is away from the midpoint of the events*, and hence in succession, not simultaneously. Then we are in a bizarre situation where both the observers O and O′ experience the two events as simultaneous, but each observer concludes that the other one experiences the events in succession. But, each observer can testify without ambiguity whether two events are experienced as locally simultaneous in one's own rest frame. Therefore, there is no consistency between the physical experience and the simple theoretical deduction, in a theory (STR) in which the relative velocity of light is an invariant constant.

Given this embarrassing situation, let us see how Einstein analysed and answered the question. Then we will see, surprisingly, that Einstein made a cardinal error in the formulation of the STR, right in its central hypothesis of the constant relative velocity of light.

## Interpretation and checks

The equality of one-way coordinate speeds is part of this synchronisation rule, while closed-path timing remains operationally accessible with one clock. Construct a spacetime diagram with two stationary clocks in one frame and transform both worldlines. A moving observer's midpoint is not the same worldline as the original stationary midpoint. Keeping those locations distinct is the central check.
