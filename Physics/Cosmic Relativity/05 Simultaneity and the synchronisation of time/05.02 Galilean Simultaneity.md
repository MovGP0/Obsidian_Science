> [!TLDR]
> In the source's Galilean analysis, simultaneous local arrivals can depend on observer motion when the signals come from separated events.

## Physical question and model

How can reception order differ when a theory assumes one universal time? Model signals in a material medium with a specified propagation speed and let a receiver move towards one source and away from another. Equal emission times need not give equal arrival times.

## Lecture account

The Principle of Relativity—the invariance of physical processes during uniform motion, and the impossibility to detect uniform motion by any physical means—was clearly stated by Galileo [4]. The mathematical prescription for the coordinate transformations, from one frame O to another frame O′ that is relatively moving, reflects this principle; only the relative velocity between the frames is used for the transformation, instead of an absolute velocity referenced to some privileged frame. So, the transformed coordinate is $\mechanical{x}'=\mechanical{x}-\mechanical{v}t$, where $\mechanical{v}$ is the relative velocity between the frames. Time was assumed to be universal, and the same in all frames, $t'=t$. This is now called the Galilean transformations (GT). The coordinate relation has the reciprocal validity, giving the inverse transformation as $\mechanical{x}=\mechanical{x}'+\mechanical{v}t'$ (and $t=t'$). This in itself does not deny a common absolute frame, obviously. That is, the GT are consistent with the existence of a privileged frame. Many phenomena involving wave propagation presuppose a medium in Galilean physics. This is certainly conforming to the facts in the case of sound waves, or waves in a body of water. The velocity of a wave is determined by the properties of its medium of propagation. Hence, *the relative velocity of waves does not depend on the motion and the velocity of the source*. However, the Galilean propagation of a signal implies that the relative velocity of waves does depend on the velocity of the receiver, as $\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{v}$. In other words, the measurement of the relative velocity of such waves enables the unique determination of the velocity of the observer, with reference to the medium.

These simple and commonly known facts are sufficient to formulate the notion of Galilean simultaneity and its relativity. For concreteness in the discussion, we can assume that the messenger wave is sound. The first question we need to answer is how do we decide that two events are simultaneous?

It is clear that we can assert true simultaneity—defined as two events occurring at the same time—only when the events are verified to have occurred at the same spatial location. This need not necessary be at a location in our proximity. In short, true simultaneity is ensured only when both the temporal duration and the spatial separation between two events are zero. This kind of true simultaneity is invariant, being independent of the position or velocity of the observer. It is also independent of the nature of propagation of the messenger waves. I state also the obvious but very important fact that the measure of simultaneity needs a local physical clock that will serve as the common reference for all events. It is not sufficient to have the same reading of two separate clocks, somehow synchronised, at the separate locations of the events. This is because the synchronisation of two spatially separated clocks depends on the postulates of the theory. More on this crucial point will be discussed later.

There are several instances, in fact the most often encountered, when the experience of two events is simultaneous in time, but the events themselves are occurring at different spatial locations. We can agree to call such events simultaneous, but this is not an invariant notion. We will now carefully examine the relativity of simultaneity in Galilean physics [5].

![[cosmic-relativity-05-01-local-clock-signals.svg|**Fig. 5.1** To determine the simultaneity of events at spatially separate locations, signals from both events must be available at the same spatial location with a local clock.]]

We will consider in detail how the two events that happen at two spatially separate location A and B are experienced by an observer in arbitrary inertial motion (Fig. 5.1). Are the events perceived as simultaneous in time or in succession, one after the other? The observer can answer this question unambiguously and quantitatively with reference to a single local clock. To maintain a strict rigour, I use the phrase ‘locally simultaneous’ to refer to the simultaneous experience of an observer determined by his local clock. To determine whether the two events happened simultaneously at the two locations, we need additional information about the distances to the two locations. Now we ask the question *whether two events that are perceived as simultaneous by one observer O, are also simultaneous for another observer O′ who is moving relatively*. Let us assume, to match the scenario in the discussions by Einstein in the context of the STR [6], that when the two events occurred, the observers O and O′ were at the midpoint of the separation $\overline{AB}$ between the events, while relatively moving at the velocity $\mechanical{v}$. (Einstein discussed the example of an observers O′ in a moving train and an observer O on a platform. We will examine Einstein's analysis later.)

![[cosmic-relativity-05-02-galilean-arrival-times.svg|**Fig. 5.2** Relativity of simultaneity in Galilean physics. Observers O and O′ are in relative motion at velocity v. They coincide at midpoint M between locations A and B when the events occur. Arrows show relative velocities. Galilean messenger waves, such as sound, bring the signals. If one observer perceives simultaneous arrivals, the other perceives successive arrivals.]]

The first point we recognise is that it is the real motion of the observer in the medium, or in a Galilean absolute frame, and not the motion of the sources that determines whether the events are perceived simultaneously. This is because *the velocity of the Galilean messenger waves (sound for example) does not depend on the motion of the sources* (relative to the medium). Let us consider the situation when O′ receives the two signals from A and B simultaneously at his location. This implies that the relative velocity of the waves are equal from A and B to O′, because it is known that the events occurred at equal distances from O′ as well as from O. Clearly, the relative velocity of the Galilean waves cannot be the same for two observers who are relatively moving. If it is $\mechanical{c}_w$ for one observer, it should be $\mechanical{c}_w\pm\mechanical{v}$ for another who is relatively moving at the velocity $\mechanical{v}$ (Fig. 5.2). Therefore, it is clear that the messenger waves from the events (that were at equal distances when the events occurred) will reach O at different times, travelling at different relative velocities. We conclude that *the events are successive and not simultaneous as perceived by O*. This situation reverses if the events were simultaneous for O. Then they cannot be simultaneous in the reference frame of O′.

We can quantify the ‘lack of simultaneity’ $\Delta t$. If the distance between A and B is $\mechanical{L}$, the duration of propagation to the midpoint is $t=\mechanical{L}/(2\mechanical{c}_w)$. For an observer to whom the relative velocity of the waves is isotropic, the two events are simultaneous, $\Delta t=0$. Since the relative velocity of the Galilean waves for the other observer is $\mechanical{c}_w+\mechanical{v}$ and $\mechanical{c}_w-\mechanical{v}$ in the two opposite directions, the durations for the propagation are $\mechanical{L}/(2(\mechanical{c}_w+\mechanical{v}))$ and $\mechanical{L}/(2(\mechanical{c}_w-\mechanical{v}))$. Then the lack of simultaneity is the difference between the arrival times of the signals from A and B:

$$
\Delta t=\frac{\mechanical{L}}{2(\mechanical{c}_w+\mechanical{v})}-\frac{\mechanical{L}}{2(\mechanical{c}_w-\mechanical{v})}
=-\frac{\mechanical{L}\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}_w^2}{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}_w^2}. \tag{5.1}
$$

This is an insightful expression, containing a core result of the theory of relativity, if we consider light instead of sound. Note that if the separation between the points A and B in the moving frame is not $\mechanical{L}$, but a length-contracted separation $\mechanical{L}'=\mechanical{L}\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}$, then the difference in the arrival of the light signals is

$$
\Delta t'=-\frac{\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}\,\mechanical{L}\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}^2}{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}
=-\frac{\mechanical{L}\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}^2}{\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}}. \tag{5.2}
$$

We recognise this expression as Lorentz's local time, when the velocity is that of (Galilean) light. This is exactly the difference of the time in a stationary clock and the time in a clock at the distance $\mechanical{L}$, moving at the velocity $\mechanical{v}$, according to the Lorentz transformation. I stress the fact that we arrived at this expression by considering the propagation of Galilean light with its relative velocity linearly dependent on the velocity of the observer. This gives us the vital insight about the exact interpretation of the LT. Since $|\Delta t'|$ *is the extra time required for light to propagate the distance $\mechanical{L}$ at the relative velocity $\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{v}$*, compared to the propagation at the relative velocity $\mechanical{c}$, *subtracting this term from the clock time at the distance $\mechanical{x}$ itself can hide the fact that the signal had reached late by this amount*. Then, with this ‘theoretically adjusted’ clock synchronisation, one can maintain that the relative velocity of light is the invariant $\mechanical{c}$, and not the factual $\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{v}$. This is what Lorentz achieved by defining the mathematical local time $t-\mechanical{x}\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}^2$ in a clock at the separation $\mechanical{x}$. In Einstein's theory, this (first order) clock adjustment was taken literally, without any empirical support.

In fact, we do not need to assume that there is length contraction because it is deduced from the real null results of the pre-relativity experiment by Michelson and Morley and the requirement of the PoR (the experimental result is never exact, hence the PoR is needed as a theoretical requirement). Then the factor $1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2$ in Eq. (5.1) should be distributed identically in the distance and duration, as $\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}$. As a consequence, this factor cancels out in the ratio of the distance and duration, which is the relative velocity. *Then we can pretend that the relative velocity of light is an invariant constant when factually it is not, implementing the Lorentz local time theoretically*. As we saw in our detailed analysis, the factual Galilean propagation remains hidden. This derivation of the full Lorentz transformations from the simple fact of the Galilean propagation of light and its consistency with the PoR, is a significant insight in understanding the physical content of relativity. I have shown that *when Galilean waves are messenger signals, simultaneity is relative*. Two events that an inertial observer O′ experiences as locally simultaneous are experienced as locally successive by another relatively moving observer O. *The reason for this relativity of simultaneity is the Galilean nature of the relative velocity of the messenger waves, which depends on the velocity of the observer's reference frame*. Both observers agree about each other's experience—the observer who perceives the signals simultaneously (O′ in this example) would reason that the relatively moving observer O moves away from the point where the signals are simultaneous; therefore the events from the locations A and B would be perceived in succession by O. *There is full consistency in the conclusions of O and O′*. That is, the perceived experience of simultaneity is different for O and O′ when there is relative motion. But, they both agree on which one of the two observers perceives simultaneity and which one perceives the events in succession; there is no inconsistency or ambiguity. One may say that the quantitative lack of simultaneity is absolute, even though the experience of simultaneity is relative. Now we analyse how this conclusion on the relativity of simultaneity changes if we assume the light hypothesis, as Einstein did. Remembering that all inertial observers in the STR are completely equivalent, and that any observer can claim the state of rest, one can guess that if one observer determines (experiences) that two events are simultaneous, then all observers should as well, in their own reference frames. Let us carefully climb the logical steps, holding the light hypothesis.

## Interpretation and checks

The signal-interception calculation determines arrival delays, not a new definition of universal time. Reversing receiver motion reverses the leading delay. Close to the signal speed the small-speed expansion fails. A propagation calculation based on a preferred medium can be internally consistent, but it should not be combined with Lorentz clock and rod rules without explaining which model supplies each ingredient.
