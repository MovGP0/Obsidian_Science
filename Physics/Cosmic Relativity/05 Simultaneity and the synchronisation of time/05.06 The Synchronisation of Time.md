> [!TLDR]
> Clock synchronisation assigns a shared time to separated locations through a protocol for exchanging signals.

## Physical question and model

How do remote clocks acquire a common time label? The two-way midpoint protocol assumes a specified stationary path and a reciprocal travel-time convention. Slow clock transport instead depends on an ideal-clock model and the full transport history.

## Lecture account

The words ‘synchronisation’ and ‘simultaneity’ may seem synonymously related. They both refer to a notion of *sameness* in time. But, they are operationally different in physics because one often requires to synchronise time at different locations. The protocol for the synchronisation of spatially separated clocks was discussed well before the advent of modern relativity. Maintaining an accurate global time is essential for applications such as commerce and navigation. The developments in wireless radio communication at the end of 19th century provided a practical method for global synchronisation of clocks with precision and reliability.

The standard protocol for the synchronisation of two clocks was studied and prescribed by H. Poincaré, when he was in the *Bureau des Longitudes*, in France. He had fully analysed the issue of simultaneity and the synchronisation of spatially separated clocks in the context of Lorentz's ether relativity [9]. The protocol starts with sending out a signal from a clock at the location A at its local time $t_{A1}$ to another clock at a different spatial location B. The clocks we are considering are in the same reference frame, at relative rest. The distance between the clocks need not be known, and in any case it cannot be known with a precision sufficient for the accurate synchronisation of atomic clocks. (The modern atomic clocks can show a relative difference in time smaller than a femtosecond, but an uncertainty of a millimetre in the spatial location of a clock can amount to an uncertainty in synchronisation several thousand times larger, of $3\,\unit{ps}$.) When the signal reaches the clock B, its time is set as $t_{B0}$ and a return signal is sent back to the clock A. When the return signal reaches the clock A at time $t_{A2}$, the midpoint of the two-way time delay is determined as $t_{A0}=(t_{A2}-t_{A1})/2$. If one is sure that the two clocks at relative rest are also at rest in an absolute sense, then it is guaranteed that the propagation of light is at the same speed both ways. Then, and only then, the time in the clocks at A and B is truly synchronised as

$$
t_{B0}=t_{A1}+(t_{A2}-t_{A1})/2=\frac{1}{2}(t_{A1}+t_{A2}). \tag{5.3}
$$

But we cannot be sure that the frame is at absolute rest, according to the PoR. The two clocks could be in inertial motion at some velocity $\mechanical{v}$ while being at relative rest. Then the duration of propagation of light each way depends on what exactly is assumed as a hypothesis for the relative velocity of light in an inertial frame. In the STR, an invariant velocity is assumed; then the synchronisation of the clocks according to the expression Eq. (5.3) can be taken as valid [2]. The midpoint of the duration of the two-way propagation $(t_{A1}+t_{A2})/2$ is independent of the velocity of the frame in the STR, *by hypothesis*.

However, the situation is different if an absolute frame for the propagation of light really exists. Then the relative velocity is different in the two directions, and the midpoint $(t_{A1}+t_{A2})/2$ is not the instant at which the synchronisation pulse from A reaches B. All one can do is to accept the protocol for the synchronisation as an *agreed convention*. This is what we really did in the case of the STR when we agreed to accept the light hypothesis as the basis for synchronisation. In accordance with the PoR, there is no way to ensure any unique synchronisation.

Though the propagation of light is Galilean in the ether relativity, the Principle of Relativity does not allow the detection of an inertial motion using this physical fact. Stressing this fundamental point, Poincaré remarked [9] (italics are mine),

> I suppose that observers placed in different points set their watches by means of optical signals; that they try to correct these signals by the transmission time, but that, *unaware of their translational motion and thus believing that the signals travel at the same speed in both directions*, they content themselves with crossing the observations, by sending one signal from A to B, then another from B to A.

The physical difference between the synchronisation protocols in the STR and when there is an absolute reference frame, using light as the synchronisation signal, is illustrated in Fig. 5.6. As we have discussed earlier, the difference in the durations for propagation at the velocity $\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{v}$ and at the velocity $\mechanical{c}$ is

$$
\Delta t=\frac{\mechanical{L}}{\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{v}}-\frac{\mechanical{L}}{\mechanical{c}}=\frac{\mechanical{L}\mechanical{v}}{\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{5.4}
$$

Whether the ‘synchronisation gap’ $\mechanical{L}\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}^2$ really exists or not cannot be determined in any experiment, in accordance with the PoR. In the STR it is taken on faith that such a gap does not exist. Therefore, in effect, the claim of clock synchronisation is a convention that remains consistent in most situations. Later we will see special configurations where the assumption made in Einstein's protocol breaks down, revealing an inconsistency ([[11.06 Cosmic Relativity and Clock Synchronisation|Chapter 11]]).

It is then clear that *two hypothetical clocks that are truly synchronised will show the exact nature of the propagation of light*. Assume that two such clocks are present in a frame that is moving at the velocity $\mechanical{v}$ in a unique absolute reference frame for dynamics. If light propagates the distance $\mechanical{L}$ from A to B at the expected relative velocity $\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{v}$, the duration will be seen as $t_B-t_A=\mechanical{L}/(\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{v})$. However, if we decree that we should subtract the duration $\mechanical{L}\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}^2$ from the clock at B to consider it synchronised with the clock at A, by some ‘law of synchronisation’, then the duration of propagation is adjusted to

$$
t_B-t_A=\frac{\mechanical{L}}{\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{v}}-\frac{\mechanical{L}\mechanical{v}}{\mechanical{c}^2}=\frac{\mechanical{L}}{\mechanical{c}}. \tag{5.5}
$$

> [!note] Source approximation signs
> Equations (5.4)–(5.5) use equal signs for first-order results in $\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$. The exact difference in Eq. (5.4) is $\mechanical{L}\mechanical{v}/(\mechanical{c}(\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{v}))$. The printed equalities are retained.

We have *arranged to make light propagate at the invariant relative velocity $\mechanical{c}$* relative to all inertial frames! This is what Lorentz did when he theoretically proposed a local time $t'=t-\mechanical{v}\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{c}^2$; it was Lorentz's ‘law of synchronisation’ [1]. This is the real physical content of the first order term in the Lorentz transformation of time. The relative velocity of light is not really an invariant. But it can be made to look like an invariant by readjusting the time of every clock at the distance $\mechanical{x}$ in a moving frame by the amount $-\mechanical{v}\mechanical{x}/\mechanical{c}^2$.

Einstein addressed the situation from the opposite direction, by postulating an invariant relative velocity of light, when no empirical fact demanded the drastic hypothesis. Then he could derive the Lorentz transformation, but he had to pay the heavy price of an inherent inconsistency (which he did not realise). The discussion of simultaneity with his ‘train and platform’ scenario is but one physical situation where this inconsistency is revealed clearly. We will see later that this inconsistency and the Galilean propagation of light can be proved in a real laboratory experiment, thereby directly falsifying Einstein's theory of relativity.

One can now consider the alternate possibility of placing two already synchronised (pre-synchronised) clocks at the desired spatial locations. Then, no light signals are involved and it is immaterial whether the propagation of light is Galilean or Einsteinian. Let us examine such a protocol. Two clocks are synchronised at the same location. Of course, the clocks cannot be exactly in the same location; they can only be at close proximity, with a minimum separation that is practically possible.

After the local synchronisation at the location A, we can move one of the clocks to the location B. For this, we need to use the theory of relativity. Since the clock is moved at some velocity $\mechanical{v}$, there will be the relativistic motional time dilation, $T_B=T\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}$, where $T$ is the duration of the transport. For inertial transport, $T=\mechanical{L}/\mechanical{v}$. If the transport is done at a small speed, $\mechanical{v}\ll\mechanical{c}$, then $\Delta T\simeq-T\mechanical{v}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)$. So, it seems possible to manage a true synchronisation of the clocks without the ambiguity of the light hypothesis, after correcting for the time dilation $\Delta T$.

But we have forgotten one crucial fact, well-hidden by the PoR. The frame (like the Earth) containing the clocks might be inertially moving at some unknown velocity. Though this is of no physical consequence in the STR, it is of significant consequences if true relativity has an absolute determining frame (like the Universe). Then the time dilation needs to be calculated with the *true velocity of both the clocks in the absolute frame*, and not just with the relative velocity between the two clocks. This is straightforward when the velocity of the common frame is also small, $\mechanical{u}\ll\mechanical{c}$. For the illustration, I assume that the velocity of the frame $\mechanical{u}$ and the relative velocity $\mechanical{v}$ of the clock are in the same direction.

The time dilation of the transported clock is

$$
\Delta T_B=-T\frac{(\mechanical{u}+\mechanical{v})^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}
=-T\frac{\mechanical{u}^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}-T\frac{\mechanical{v}^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}-T\frac{\mechanical{u}\mechanical{v}}{\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{5.6}
$$

The first term, which is unknowable according to the PoR, is not troublesome because the reference clock A that is moving with the frame has the identical dilation. Therefore, it does not affect the synchronisation, which *demands only that the clocks have no relative difference in time*. The second term can be corrected because the relative velocity $\mechanical{v}$ is precisely known. Since $T\mechanical{v}=\mechanical{L}$, the separation between the locations A and B, the third term is equal to $-\mechanical{L}\mechanical{u}/\mechanical{c}^2$. So, when the clock reaches B, it is lagging behind the clock at A by the duration $\mechanical{L}\mechanical{u}/\mechanical{c}^2$ even after all the possible corrections are made; *it is no longer synchronised*. All we can do is to assume that the clocks are synchronised, by an agreed convention and protocol.

Now the interesting thing is that if such clocks with a true but unverifiable synchronisation gap are used to measure the relative velocity of light, the Galilean nature of the propagation remains hidden; the experiment will return the result $\mechanical{c}$ even if the true relative velocity is $\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{u}$. This is because light takes the duration $T_{AB}=\mechanical{L}/(\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{u})\simeq(\mechanical{L}/\mechanical{c})+\mechanical{L}\mechanical{u}/\mechanical{c}^2$ to reach from A to B. But the clock at B is running behind by exactly the excess duration $\mechanical{L}\mechanical{u}/\mechanical{c}^2$ that light takes. Since we believe that the clocks were synchronised, when they were not, we take the duration of propagation as $\mechanical{L}/\mechanical{c}$ instead of $T_{AB}$, and conclude that the relative velocity of light is always $\mechanical{c}$. This is exactly what happened in all the past experiments that claimed a measurement or a verification of the one-way velocity of light [11]. We will see the details of specific experiments later, in [[11.04 The Experiment on the One-Way Relative Velocity of Light|Chapter 11]]. Then we will realise that all such experiments were ill-conceived, and their claimed results based on a naive understanding of the time dilation in relativity are invalid.

There is another curious side to the entanglement between clock synchronisation and the propagation of light. A clock transported to a distance $\mechanical{L}$, in a frame moving at the inertial velocity $\mechanical{u}$, will have an excess time dilation $\mechanical{L}\mechanical{u}/\mechanical{c}^2$. It is just that we are not able to see it once the clock is transported away. But, that is old wisdom, instilled in us by the PoR. Using a cosmological reference like the Cosmic Microwave Background Radiation (CMBR), we can actually know the velocity of the frame's inertial motion. With the cosmological evidence included, we can no longer claim that we are at rest when we are actually moving through the Universe (we are jumping ahead a bit, about topics that we will discuss in great detail later). Therefore, two clocks can be truly synchronised across a distance $\mechanical{L}$ in principle, though practically there are difficulties. However, when such truly synchronised clocks are used, experiments on the one-way propagation of light will show the *factual duration of propagation* $T_{AB}\simeq(\mechanical{L}/\mechanical{c})+\mechanical{L}\mechanical{u}/\mechanical{c}^2$ instead of $\mechanical{L}/\mechanical{c}$. Needless to say, this will directly invalidate the STR.

## Interpretation and checks

The midpoint average has time units and reduces to the reflection time for equal outbound and return delays. In equations (5.4)–(5.5), the equalities that replace a reciprocal speed denominator by a first-order correction are approximations: second-order terms remain. On a rotating network, a single global Einstein synchronisation around a loop gives a Sagnac time gap. One must state the chosen network time instead of mixing a local reciprocal rule with a global rotating chart.
