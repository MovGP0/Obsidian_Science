> [!TLDR]
> The proposed theory includes the cosmic gravitational potential in electron dynamics and uses its motion-induced vector potential to alter state degeneracy.

## Physical question and model

How could cosmic gravity change a laboratory Hall observable? The model must state which potential differences, gradients, currents, or phases enter the electron Hamiltonian. A large spatially constant potential does not automatically produce a large measurable spectral effect.

## Lecture account

Charge carriers have mass as well as electric charge. Their wavefunction phases can therefore couple to gravitational and electromagnetic potentials. The author argues that the small terrestrial gravitational contribution does not justify neglecting interaction with all cosmic matter [2]. The added Hamiltonian term is

$$
\energy{H}_g=-\mechanical{m}\mechanical{\Phi}_u,
\qquad\mechanical{\Phi}_u\sim\mechanical{c}^2.
$$

He calls this the largest term in a condensed-matter Hamiltonian. Its normally passive role becomes active for the noninertial electron motion in the QHE. The proposed effect is a change in the number of available states for weakly interacting electrons, rather than a change of particle type. See [[14.07.01 The Quantum Degeneracy of the Landau Levels]].

## Interpretation and checks

Adding the same constant to every energy gives a common phase at fixed particle number. It does not change transition frequencies or the Landau state count. The proposed vector coupling is therefore the crucial extra input. Specify its units, source, frame, and boundary conditions before calculating its curl or action. A rest-energy-sized scalar estimate alone cannot establish the proposed fractional transport mechanism.
