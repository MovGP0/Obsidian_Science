> [!TLDR]
> The Hall effect produces a transverse voltage in a current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field.

## Physical question and model

Why does a transverse voltage appear in a current-carrying conductor? Magnetic deflection initially separates charge. In the steady one-carrier model, the transverse electric force balances the magnetic force, leaving no net transverse carrier drift.

## Lecture account

The quantum Hall effect (QHE) is the quantised form of the classical Hall effect [3], discovered by Hall in 1879 [4]. Consider a rectangular conductor of length $\mechanical{L}$, width $\mechanical{w}$, and smaller thickness $\mechanical{d}$. A battery drives a longitudinal current

$$
\electric{I}=\frac{\electric{V}}{\circuit{R}}.
$$

For two points a and b along its length, Ohm's law gives $\electric{V}_{ab}=\circuit{R}_{ab}\electric{I}$. Current is the same along the sample by charge conservation. Without a magnetic field, opposite points across its width or thickness have zero potential difference because no current flows in those directions (Fig. 14.1).

![[cosmic-relativity-14-01-classical-hall-effect.svg|**Fig. 14.1** (1) Longitudinal current without a magnetic field gives zero transverse voltage. (2) A perpendicular field deflects carriers and produces a steady Hall voltage. (3) The Hall voltage is linear in magnetic field.]]

Hall first tested whether a transverse magnetic force increased wire resistance. He found no appreciable change despite sensitivity to one part in a million. He then reasoned that the force could produce transverse electrical pressure. H. Rowland had tried this unsuccessfully and advised improvements. Hall used thin gold leaf on glass, pressed between brass contacts and attached to an electromagnet pole. A perpendicular field of $0.2$–$0.3\,\unit{T}$ gave a reliable transverse signal.

The Hall voltage is proportional to applied field and changes sign on field reversal. Its magnitude and sign depend on the material. Magnetic deflection creates a steady charge imbalance until the resulting transverse electric field balances the magnetic force. There is no steady transverse current. The ratio $\electric{V}_H/\electric{I}$ is called Hall resistance by analogy with Ohm's law. Hall measurements characterise conductors and measure currents and fields.

Some outer atomic electrons can move through a conductor under a small applied potential. Their full dynamics is quantum mechanical, but a carrier-density description suffices here. For free-electron density $\semiconductor{n}$, mean drift speed $\mechanical{v}$, and cross-section $\mechanical{A}=\mechanical{w}\mechanical{d}$,

$$
\frac{\electric{I}}{\mechanical{A}}=-\semiconductor{n}\electric{e}\mechanical{v}.
$$

The magnetic force is $\mechanical{F}=-\electric{e}\mechanical{v}\magnetic{B}$. Its balance with the transverse electric field gives

$$
-\frac{\electric{e}\electric{V}_H}{\mechanical{w}}=-\electric{e}\mechanical{v}\magnetic{B}. \tag{14.1}
$$

Thus $\mechanical{v}=\electric{V}_H/(\mechanical{w}\magnetic{B})$ and $\electric{I}/(\mechanical{w}\mechanical{d})=-\semiconductor{n}\electric{e}\electric{V}_H/(\mechanical{w}\magnetic{B})$, giving

$$
\semiconductor{n}\electric{e}=-\frac{\electric{I}\magnetic{B}}{\electric{V}_H\mechanical{d}}. \tag{14.2}
$$

Measuring Hall voltage therefore gives carrier density. In some semiconductors, conduction is better represented as bound electrons hopping into vacant orbitals, or holes moving oppositely with effective positive charge. The Hall-voltage sign identifies the carrier type. The ratio $\electric{I}/\electric{V}_H=1/\circuit{R}_H$ is called Hall conductance, although it does not describe transverse conduction.

## Interpretation and checks

The Hall-voltage sign depends on carrier charge, magnetic-field direction, and contact orientation. Check each before using the magnitude to infer density. The displayed density is a volume density because sample thickness appears; a two-dimensional sheet uses a sheet density instead. The single-carrier result can fail for several carrier types or nonuniform current. The transverse Hall voltage is not itself a longitudinal dissipative voltage drop.
