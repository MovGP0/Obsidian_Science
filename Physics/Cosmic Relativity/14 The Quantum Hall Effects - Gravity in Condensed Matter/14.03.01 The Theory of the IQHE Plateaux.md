> [!TLDR]
> Chiral edge transport explains low dissipation, while impurity-localised states help extend Hall quantisation over finite filling intervals.

## Physical question and model

Why do plateaux have finite width? In the usual integer-effect account, disorder can localise bulk states between extended states while conducting boundaries preserve transport. A spectral gap and a mobility gap are related but different concepts.

## Lecture account

Landau quantisation alone does not explain finite plateau width or zero longitudinal resistivity. Conduction usually involves electrons near the Fermi level, the highest occupied energy. Pauli exclusion permits two opposite-spin electrons in a spatial orbital. Within one Landau level the mean spatial distribution is uniform. Its states per area equal $\magnetic{B}/\magnetic{\phi}_0$, with $\magnetic{\phi}_0=\energy{h}/\electric{e}$. Once full, electrons enter the next level, $\energy{E}_{n+1}=\energy{E}_n+\energy{\hbar}\signal{\omega}$.

In an applied electric potential, microscopic cyclotron orbits drift and carry current. At complete Landau filling, the source describes a transition from bulk Ohmic conduction to current along sample edges. Magnetic deflection at a boundary preserves the sense of orbiting, even after a collision with a boundary or impurity. These edge states therefore propagate without back-scattering. Their direction is fixed by field direction, and the lack of back-scattering explains vanishing longitudinal dissipation.

![[cosmic-relativity-14-05-chiral-edge-transport.svg|**Fig. 14.5** Schematic edge transport in a two-dimensional sample. Enlarged cyclotron paths turn in the same sense. Skipping motion travels right along the top edge and left along the lower edge; a lower-edge obstacle does not reverse the net transport.]]

Impurities broaden Landau levels into bands. Quantum interference localises most states away from each band's centre. Localised electrons do not carry bulk current; extended states near the band centre can. This separation permits Hall-resistance quantisation over a finite filling-factor interval around an integer [5] (Fig. 14.6).

**Fig. 14.6** Schematic explanation of finite Hall plateaux. Broadened Landau bands have extended states near their centres and localised tails. The Hall response changes while extended states are crossed and remains constant between them. Red curves mark the narrow conducting centres. This graph illustrates the source mechanism rather than measured state densities.

With the Fermi level between Landau centres, bulk states are localised and only edges conduct. The bulk spatial distribution remains unchanged, so Hall voltage is constant and longitudinal resistance vanishes. Increasing field raises each level's degeneracy. Electrons from higher levels occupy new lower-level states.

When the Fermi level crosses extended states, ordinary bulk conduction returns and Hall voltage changes. The source notes a small longitudinal magnetoresistance in practice. Further depletion returns the Fermi level to localised states and another plateau. In the independent-level narrative this repeats down to the lowest level, $n=0$, with $\energy{E}_0=\energy{\hbar}\signal{\omega}/2$. The source therefore expects the final plateau at $\nu=1$, with none below it.

The same account must work at fixed field while a gate voltage changes sheet density. The confining structure acts capacitively; the gate can add electrons or holes. Both increasing field and decreasing density lower $\nu=\semiconductor{\rho}/\magnetic{b}$. A plateau at density $\semiconductor{\rho}$ and field $\magnetic{B}$ should recur at $\semiconductor{\rho}/2$ and $\magnetic{B}/2$, though the energy gap is smaller and orbit radius larger. The author stresses that a correct theory must describe both sweeps. This makes the existence of plateaux below $\nu=1$ a challenge to the integer-level account.

> [!note]
> In this paragraph the source prints the degeneracy as g=eB/φ₀, although it previously defined φ₀=h/e and the degeneracy as B/φ₀. The extra charge factor is a source inconsistency.

Tsui, Stormer, and Gossard overturned this expectation by observing a plateau at $\nu=1/3$ in 1982 [7]. Further plateaux appeared below and above one. D. C. Tsui, H. L. Störmer, and R. B. Laughlin, who supplied an early theoretical account [8], received the 1998 Nobel Prize.

## Interpretation and checks

Changing particle number can fill localised states without adding a new conducting channel. This explains why a changing density need not continuously change Hall conductance. Keep the per-spin Landau count separate from unresolved spin degeneracy. A perfectly clean Landau spectrum alone does not reproduce the whole plateau mechanism. Compare the localisation and boundary conditions in [Tong’s integer-effect treatment](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.06687), rather than treating every state as an identical classical orbit.
