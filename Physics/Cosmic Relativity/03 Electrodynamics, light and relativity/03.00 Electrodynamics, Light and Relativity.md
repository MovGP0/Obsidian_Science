## Physical question and model

Why did optics force a comparison between mechanical and electromagnetic descriptions of motion? The chapter combines travel-time measurements, phase comparisons, and induced electrical effects. These experiments do not all measure the same quantity or use the same clock procedure.

## Lecture account

## Abstract

Electrodynamics and the study of light were the parents of the modern theories of relativity that emerged at the turn of the 20th century. The study of the propagation of light in space and in different media, experiments on the properties of light, experiments involving moving conductors and magnets etc. led to the mature theory of Maxwell electrodynamics, set in the ‘ether’. The decisive Michelson-Morley experiment prompted the study of the relativistic invariance of electrodynamics, and the theories of relativity. We explore the prominent milestones in this journey, before examining Einstein's Special Theory of Relativity.

## Introduction

We can see at least five distinct tracks in the early developments of the modern physical science, from the 15th century to the end of the 19th century. The first was a mature tradition and knowledge of celestial astronomy, with extensive observations, documentation, and models, but without a proper theory, in the modern sense. The second was the rapid development of mechanics, which accelerated after Newton formulated the ‘laws of motion’ and the ‘law of gravity’, providing a complete theory for dynamics and celestial mechanics. The formal mathematical developments in mechanics, like Lagrange's and Hamilton's mechanics, flourished because of their wider and more general applicability. These advances kept strengthening the structure of physics. The science of materials and their structural properties also benefited by these developments. The third was the study and applications of ‘heat’, as a resource to extract mechanical work and as a physical entity in itself; the study of thermodynamics led to a new mechanics as well as new machines. It also gave birth to the paradigm of statistical mechanics, which is the physics of the dynamics of innumerable particles, as in a gas. Thermodynamics was the resourceful link between the gross properties in the continuum, like heat and pressure, and atoms, those discrete constituents of matter. The fourth track was the study of electricity and magnetism. The rapid transformation from the study of naturally occurring electric and magnetic phenomena to the fascinating, and very often amazing, discoveries and applications in designed experiments in the laboratories, happened in the 19th century. These culminated in the theory of ‘electrodynamics’, which was the stepping board for the theories of relativity. However, the concrete steps required the results from a fifth stream of study, which was the ancient and ever fascinating study of light, or ‘optics’. Light was really the only means of observing both the celestial world and our immediate worlds. Therefore, the study of its properties was always very important. It was also immediately impactful in the society, being explicit in life, arts, and vision. Its spectral variety and its finite but immense speed were already known, and quantified by the time the realisation matured that light behaved like waves in many phenomena.

During the mid-19th century, a turning point happened in the coming together of the sciences of astronomy, electromagnetism and light, in an enveloping unity of the principles of mechanics. Certain physical puzzles related to observational astronomy, like the stellar aberration, had partial solutions in the nature of light and its propagation. The ‘coincidence’, that the product of the measured values of the constants of electric and magnetic (current) interactions (the electric permittivity $\electric{\epsilon}_0$ and the magnetic permeability $\magnetic{\mu}_0$, in modern terminology) was related to the measured speed of light, was noticed. The assertion that light was propagating oscillations in the electromagnetic field, with the speed equal to $\mechanical{c}=\sqrt{1/\electric{\epsilon}_0\magnetic{\mu}_0}$, was established by J. C. Maxwell from the mathematical representation of the known electromagnetic phenomena. The propagation of any wave requires a medium, and when nothing was observable as a medium for the propagation of light, the existence of a new kind of medium was postulated. The ‘electromagnetic ether’ was the all pervading hypothetical medium for all electromagnetic actions. Maxwell wrote in 1861 (‘On the physical lines of force’, part III) [1],

> The velocity of transverse undulations in our hypothetical medium, calculated from the electromagnetic experiments of M. Kohlrauch and Weber, agrees so exactly with the velocity of light calculated from the optical experiments of M. Fizeau, that we can scarcely avoid the inference that light consists in the transverse undulations of the same medium which is the cause of electric and magnetic phenomena.

> [!note] Source notation
> The source writes the radical as $\sqrt{1/\electric{\epsilon}_0\magnetic{\mu}_0}$ without a grouped denominator. Its prose identifies the product of permittivity and permeability. The expression is retained as printed.

Experimental studies on the propagation of light in vacuum and material media had a profound influence on the theories of relativity. In this chapter we examine several such experiments and their interpretations. We will see that the entirety of experiments with light was the stimulus for the theories of relativity, including the Special Theory of Relativity. We will examine several different aspects of the propagation of light in this chapter: (a) the determination of the velocity of light, (b) the Doppler shift of light, (c) the stellar aberration, (d) the dragging of light in moving material media, and (e) the attempt to detect the ether medium by studying the anisotropy of the propagation of light in frames moving through the stationary ether.

Though there were several non-optical electromagnetic experiments that provided the clues to the modern theories of relativity, we will discuss only two of these, to emphasise some subtleties and also to indicate some open issues. These are the Trouton-Noble experiment (1901–02), and Faraday's experiments on the ‘unipolar induction’ (∼1832).

The theories of relativity emerged as the definitive reconciliation of the experimental results in electrodynamics and optics with the system of mechanics. While the Lorentz-Poincaré theory of relativity was anchored on electrodynamics, Einstein's theory of relativity aspired to encompass a more general framework. The central point in the theories of relativity is the Principle of Relativity, which is the statement of the universal applicability of the same physical laws in all frames of reference that are in uniform motion. Since there are phenomena in electrodynamics that depend on motion, like the induction of a current when a conductor moves in a magnetic field, the statement is not that the empirical situation remains the same (invariant) when there is motion. Rather, the statement, learned from the experiments, is that the empirical results cannot be used to deduce which of the two reference frames involved in the physical phenomenon is in motion; only their relative motion has a physical meaning. To make this idea precise, we will also examine a class of experiments that involve the relative motion between conductors and magnets, which actually served as one of Einstein's motivations for the construction of his Special Theory of Relativity (STR).
