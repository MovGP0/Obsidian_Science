> [!TLDR]
> The Doppler effect changes the observed frequency when a wave source and an observer move relative to one another.

## Physical question and model

Which change in wave phase produces a measured Doppler shift? For sound, source and observer velocities are measured relative to the medium. For vacuum light, specify the emission direction and compare source and receiver proper times with a common inertial description.

## Lecture account

The noticeable change of pitch in the sound from moving sources is a common phenomenon that is familiar to everybody. Therefore, it is rather surprising that the phenomenon was noticed only around 1842, by Christian Doppler, that too ‘theoretically’ [11]. He advanced a hypothesis and a formula in the context of Bradley's aberration theory for stellar light. Doppler considered the possibility of a change in the colour of stars due to the orbital motion of the Earth, but realised that the velocity was too small for a discernible change. But, he was hopeful of large changes in the colour of binary stars, because he thought that they could be moving at speeds comparable to the speed of light. He developed the theory on the basis of a geometric analysis of familiar water waves and a moving observer. It is only after the theory was discussed by Doppler, that the first attempts to confirm the effect were taken up, for the practical and easier case of sound.

**Fig. 3.5** A moving source (S) of Galilean waves and an observer (O), used to derive the Doppler shift of the detected frequency. The source and observer motions have different effects on the frequency.

I will systematically derive the expressions for the Doppler shift, considering the motion of a source and of an observer, as shown in Fig. 3.5. For the familiar Galilean waves, the shift in the frequency follows two different formulae, when the source is in motion and when the observer is in motion. Consider a source moving, with velocity $\mechanical{U}_s$. The stationary observer receives the waves at angle $\mechanical{\phi}$. The component of the velocity along the source-observer line is denoted $\mechanical{U}_s\cos\mechanical{\phi}=\pm\mechanical{u}_s$. Each wave of the wavelength $\signal{\lambda}$ (and the frequency $\signal{\nu}$) is emitted at the regular interval $\signal{T}=1/\signal{\nu}$, but each is emitted at a progressively changing distance between the source and the observer, $\pm\mechanical{u}_s\signal{T}$. Thus, the wavelength at the emission is changed to $\signal{\lambda}'=\signal{\lambda}\pm\mechanical{u}_s\signal{T}=\signal{\lambda}\pm\mechanical{u}_s/\signal{\nu}=\signal{\lambda}(1\pm\mechanical{u}_s/\mechanical{c}_w)$. This is detected with a modified frequency, since the velocity of the waves remains the same; the relative velocity of the waves is not affected by the motion of the source (this is true for both sound and light, and indeed for all waves). The frequency at reception is

$$
\signal{\nu}'=\mechanical{c}_w/\signal{\lambda}'=\frac{\signal{\nu}}{1\pm\mechanical{u}_s/\mechanical{c}_w}. \tag{3.4}
$$

The positive sign of $\mechanical{u}_s$ is for the source moving away from the observer (Fig. 3.5). This is approximately $\signal{\nu}(1\mp\mechanical{u}_s/\mechanical{c}_w)$, when $\mechanical{u}_s\ll\mechanical{c}_w$. On the other hand, if the observer is moving at the velocity $\mechanical{U}_o\cos\mechanical{\phi}=\pm\mechanical{u}_o$, the relative velocity of the waves changes to $\mechanical{c}'=\mechanical{c}\pm\mechanical{u}_o$, resulting in the altered frequency

$$
\signal{\nu}'=(\mechanical{c}\pm\mechanical{u}_o)/\signal{\lambda}=\signal{\nu}\pm\frac{\mechanical{u}_o}{\signal{\lambda}}=\signal{\nu}(1\pm\mechanical{u}_o/\mechanical{c}_w). \tag{3.5}
$$

The positive sign of $\mechanical{u}_o$ corresponds to the observer moving towards the source (Fig. 3.5). The dependence of the velocity of the source and the observer is very different—the velocity of the source appears in the denominator whereas the velocity of the observer is in the numerator. In general, the two situations together provide the complete non-relativistic Doppler formula

$$
\signal{\nu}'=\signal{\nu}\frac{1\pm\mechanical{u}_o/\mechanical{c}_w}{1\pm\mechanical{u}_s/\mechanical{c}_w}. \tag{3.6}
$$

When we consider the frequency shift of light, $\mechanical{c}_w=\mechanical{c}$. With the motional modification of time in relativity, the expression for the Doppler shift is modified to include the second order Lorentz factor. The time dilation effect is a second order ($\mechanical{u}^2/\mechanical{c}^2$) phenomenon that affects moving clocks, whereas the Doppler effect is a first order ($\mechanical{u}/\mechanical{c}$) effect linked to straightforward kinematics. There is an extremely subtle and important fact related to the time dilation here. When the source is in motion, the time (clock) associated with the source runs at a lower rate and any frequency (number/second) is diminished by the factor $(1-\mechanical{u}_s^2/\mechanical{c}^2)^{1/2}$. The combined effect of the time dilation and the Doppler shift is usually called the relativistic Doppler effect, but the physical distinction of the two contributions is important. The relativistic expression for the Doppler spectral shift, when only the source moves, is then

$$
\signal{\nu}'=\signal{\nu}\frac{\sqrt{1-\mechanical{u}_s^2/\mechanical{c}^2}}{1\pm\mechanical{u}_s/\mechanical{c}}. \tag{3.7}
$$

For a receding source of light ($\mechanical{u}_s$ positive), we get

$$
\signal{\nu}'=\signal{\nu}\frac{\sqrt{1-\mechanical{u}_s^2/\mechanical{c}^2}}{1+\mechanical{u}_s/\mechanical{c}}
=\signal{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{1-\mechanical{u}_o/\mechanical{c}}{1+\mechanical{u}_o/\mechanical{c}}}. \tag{3.8}
$$

When $\mechanical{u}_s/\mechanical{c}\ll1$, this approximates to

$$
\signal{\nu}'\approx\signal{\nu}\approx1-\mechanical{u}_s/\mechanical{c}+\mechanical{u}_s/2\mechanical{c}^2. \tag{3.9}
$$

When the observer moves, *it is the clock associated with the observer that runs at a lower rate*. Therefore, the observer will detect a larger number of cycles per (stretched) unit time. Then the spectral shift is

$$
\signal{\nu}'=\signal{\nu}\frac{1\pm\mechanical{u}_o/\mechanical{c}}{\sqrt{1-\mechanical{u}_o^2/\mechanical{c}^2}}. \tag{3.10}
$$

When the time dilation factor is combined with the Doppler shift, there is no difference between the movement of the source and that of the observer. When the observer recedes from a source of light ($\mechanical{u}_o$ negative), we get

$$
\signal{\nu}'=\signal{\nu}\frac{1-\mechanical{u}_o/\mechanical{c}}{\sqrt{1-\mechanical{u}_o^2/\mechanical{c}^2}}
=\signal{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{1-\mechanical{u}_o/\mechanical{c}}{1+\mechanical{u}_o/\mechanical{c}}}. \tag{3.11}
$$

This is the same expression as the one when the source recedes from the observer.

In the Lorentz-Poincaré theory of relativity (LPTR), the general expression for the spectral shift of light when both the source and the observer move along the line of sight ($\mechanical{\phi}=0$) is

$$
\signal{\nu}'=\signal{\nu}\frac{(1\pm\mechanical{u}_o/\mechanical{c})(1-\mechanical{u}_s^2/\mechanical{c}^2)^{1/2}}{(1\pm\mechanical{u}_s/\mechanical{c})(1-\mechanical{u}_o^2/\mechanical{c}^2)^{1/2}}
=\sqrt{\frac{1\pm\mechanical{u}_o/\mechanical{c}}{1\mp\mechanical{u}_o/\mechanical{c}}}\sqrt{\frac{1\mp\mechanical{u}_s/\mechanical{c}}{1\pm\mechanical{u}_s/\mechanical{c}}}. \tag{3.12}
$$

Thus, the expression is symmetrical for radial (collinear) motion, and the phenomenon looks the same whether the source moves or the observer moves, keeping the sense of their relative motion. However, when the movement is not collinear, this apparent symmetry is broken. When the motion of the source is transverse to the line joining the source and the receiver, the component of the velocity along that line is zero. Then we get

$$
\signal{\nu}'=\signal{\nu}\sqrt{1-\mechanical{u}_s^2/\mechanical{c}^2}\approx\signal{\nu}(1-\mechanical{v}^2/2\mechanical{c}^2). \tag{3.13}
$$

The detected frequency is less than the emitted frequency, as expected from the time dilation at the source. Though such an effect was looked for, ever since the second order effects in Lorentz's theory became evident, it was confirmed only in 1937, in a famous experiment by Ives and Stilwell [12].

When the observer is in transverse motion, instead of the source, the result is dramatically different,

$$
\signal{\nu}'=\frac{\signal{\nu}}{\sqrt{1-\mechanical{u}_s^2/\mechanical{c}^2}}\approx\signal{\nu}(1+\mechanical{v}^2/2\mechanical{c}^2). \tag{3.14}
$$

Now *the detected frequency is more than the emitted frequency*, due to the time dilation of the observer's clock. This asymmetry between the moving source and the moving observer might seem like a crucial experimental demarcation to choose the correct theory of relativity, because such an asymmetry should not be present in Einstein's STR that respects only the relative motion as physically significant. But, the effect of aberration, which is present only when the observer is in transverse motion, complicates the situation. We will explore this closely in the next chapter when we discuss Einstein's theory in detail, by directly following his 1905 landmark paper. In fact, his prediction for the Doppler shift in the 1905 paper was the expression Eq. (3.14), but what was verified in the Ives-Stilwell experiment was the expression Eq. (3.13)! I note here that the situation where the observer is in transverse motion, instead of the source, is not yet adequately probed in experiments. It is of course very difficult to move a detector at a speed large enough to detect a second order ($\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2$) effect. However, if the ‘observer’ (detector) is the fast-moving atoms themselves, the test is feasible, but as cautioned, the Doppler effect and the aberration effect are intermingled.

The radial (or line of sight) spectral shift is of great importance in cosmology because the entire theoretical basis of modern cosmology relies on the Hubble expansion and its interpretations. Empirically the phenomenon is the shift of the radiation from distant sources towards the lower frequencies, and the monotonic correlation of the shift $z\equiv(\signal{\nu}-\signal{\nu}')/\signal{\nu}$ with the distance $\mechanical{d}$. For $z\ll1$, one has the Hubble relation $z=H\mechanical{d}$, where $H\approx70\,\unit{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$ (1 Mpc = 3.26 light years). The interpretation of the spectral shift in the GTR is based on the expansion of space itself, but *there is no way to distinguish it from the interpretation as a motional spectral shift due to the actual relativistic motion of the sources and the observer*, possibly mixed up with some gravitational redshift. Taking the cosmological redshift as a motional Doppler phenomenon, we can formalise it with the relation derived above, with the source moving away at the speed $\mechanical{u}_s$ and the observer moving towards the source at the smaller speed $\mechanical{u}_o$.

$$
\signal{\nu}'=\signal{\nu}\sqrt{\frac{1-\mechanical{u}_s/\mechanical{c}}{1+\mechanical{u}_s/\mechanical{c}}}\sqrt{\frac{1+\mechanical{u}_o/\mechanical{c}}{1-\mechanical{u}_o/\mechanical{c}}}
=\signal{\nu}\left(\frac{1-(\mechanical{u}_s-\mechanical{u}_o)/\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{u}_s\mechanical{u}_o/\mechanical{c}^2}{1+(\mechanical{u}_s-\mechanical{u}_o)/\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{u}_s\mechanical{u}_o/\mechanical{c}^2}\right)^{1/2}. \tag{3.15}
$$

where $\mechanical{u}_s-\mechanical{u}_o$ is the relative velocity $\mechanical{u}_r$. When the velocities are small compared to $\mechanical{c}$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\signal{\nu}'&\approx\signal{\nu}\left((1-\mechanical{u}_r/\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{u}_s\mechanical{u}_o/\mechanical{c}^2)(1-\mechanical{u}_r/\mechanical{c}+\mechanical{u}_s\mechanical{u}_o/\mechanical{c}^2+\mechanical{u}_r^2/\mechanical{c}^2)\right)^{1/2}\\
&\approx\signal{\nu}(1-2\mechanical{u}_r/\mechanical{c}+2\mechanical{u}_r^2/\mechanical{c}^2)^{1/2}
\approx\signal{\nu}\left(1-\frac{\mechanical{u}_r}{\mechanical{c}}+\frac{\mechanical{u}_r^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}\right).
\end{aligned} \tag{3.16}
$$

This expression is the same as the one we derived for the Doppler shift when only the source moves away (Eq. (3.9)).

## Interpretation and checks

All frequency ratios must be dimensionless and tend to one at zero relative speed. Several printed lines mix a frequency with a dimensionless expansion or change source and observer symbols. Use a receding collinear configuration to check the sign: received frequency decreases. Transverse Doppler formulas depend on whether the observation angle is fixed in the source or receiver frame. A zero first-order shift can leave a second-order time-dilation term.
