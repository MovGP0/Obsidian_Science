> [!TLDR]
> Unipolar induction experiments compare the electrical effects of moving a conductor, a magnet, or both together.

## Physical question and model

How can a steady magnetic field produce an emf in a moving conductor? The relevant quantity is force per charge integrated along a specified circuit. Include the velocity of each conductor segment, the electric field in the same frame, and the sliding contacts or return path.

## Lecture account

In general, the term unipolar induction refers to the generation of a current or voltage when a conductor and a magnet are relatively moved over one pole of the magnet, often with a constant magnetic field. Thus, this is not the usual Faraday induction, in Maxwell's equation, $\nabla\times\electric{\mathbf E}=\partial\magnetic{\mathbf B}/\partial t$. In modern terms it is described by the Lorenz Force, $\mechanical{\mathbf F}=\electric{e}\mechanical{\mathbf v}\times\magnetic{\mathbf B}$. At once we are faced with the fundamental problem of deciding the frame in which we measure the velocity—absolute or relative?

The phenomenon of the unipolar induction was studied by Faraday first, and he was thoroughly puzzled by its nature. These studies were discussed in the Bakerian lectures of 1832 [26, 27]. Some of these experiments were pursued by Ampère.

If a conducting straight wire is moved uniformly above a long bar magnet, the charges in the wire start moving (Fig. 3.9). This current is proportional to the velocity and the strength of the (uniform) magnetic field. Interestingly, exactly the same phenomenon, with the same current, is produced when the wire is held stationary and the magnet is moved uniformly in the opposite direction. When the conductor and the magnet are moved together, there is no current, consistent with what one might expect from the previous experiments. Without bothering about the microscopic reasons for these unipolar phenomena, one may say that the induced current depends only on the relative velocity between the magnet and the conductor. In fact, one can go a step further with these experiments and consider a linear motion with acceleration. An oscillatory motion of either the wire or the magnet is one such example. Then, an oscillatory current that is proportional to the relative velocity results.

![[cosmic-relativity-03-09-linear-unipolar-induction.svg|**Fig. 3.9** Unipolar induction in linear motion, which strongly motivated Einstein's theory. (a) Conductor moving, (b) magnet moving, and (c) both moving together. The source labels conductor and magnet velocities v_C and v_M and the induced electromotive force E.]]

The same kind of induction can be observed when a conductor is moved azimuthally (angular motion) over one pole of a disc-shaped magnet (Fig. 3.10a). What puzzled Faraday was that such currents are generated even when there is absolutely no relative motion between the magnet and the conductor, as it happens when they are co-rotating. When a magnet and a conductor attached to one of the poles (say, a metal disc attached to a disc shaped magnet) are rotated together, without any relative motion between them, large currents flow radially (Fig. 3.10c). In fact, one can just rotate a conducting (iron) magnet, and a current will flow in an external circuit between the centre of the magnet and the periphery. It is as if the entire physical character of the unipolar phenomenon changes when the bar magnet is bent around to make a disc. The potential difference generated across the conductor matches what one gets by adding up the elementary contributions $\mechanical{v}(\mechanical{r})\magnetic{B}$ across the disc. This shows that the velocity involved in the general phenomenon is not the relative velocity, contradicting the previous hypothesis prompted by the restricted class of linear experiments.

![[cosmic-relativity-03-10-rotating-unipolar-induction.svg|**Fig. 3.10** Unipolar induction in relative rotation, which Faraday and Ampère found puzzling. (a) Conductor rotating, (b) magnet rotating beneath a stationary conductor, and (c) both rotating together. The source reports nonzero emf for (a) and (c), and zero emf for (b).]]

Continuing with this class of experiments, there are situations where no current is generated even when there is a relative motion between the magnet and the conductor. While an electromotive force (emf) of the expected magnitude appears when a conductor is moved azimuthally over a disk-shaped magnet (Fig. 3.10), no such emf appears in the reciprocal situation of the magnet rotating under the stationary conductor. In the latter situation, there is zero induction. So, the totality of the experimental results shows unambiguously that it is important to consider the ‘real’ (absolute) motion rather than a relative motion in the description of the unipolar induction phenomena. Faraday himself thought of a partial solution: that the magnetic field did not rotate with magnet. But that solution is unsatisfactory and inconsistent because of the linear case, where the magnetic field moves with the magnet. In any case, even a limited survey of the existing experimental results shows that the unipolar induction cannot be understood as a phenomenon that depends only on the relative velocity between the magnet and the conductor. It is a two-century-old puzzle and a deep physical problem for which the correct and complete solution is obtained only within a new paradigm that recognises an absolute master frame.

> [!note] Source sign and spelling
> The printed Maxwell induction equation omits the usual minus sign. The source also calls the Lorentz force the “Lorenz Force”. Both are retained above.

## Interpretation and checks

A time-independent magnetic field need not imply zero motional emf. Faraday's differential law has a minus sign for the field curl, while the magnetic force on moving charges contributes separately through the circuit motion. The source's opening curl relation omits that sign. Predict what happens when the magnet alone turns but an axially symmetric field pattern stays fixed. Do not infer emf merely from relative motion of two extended objects.
