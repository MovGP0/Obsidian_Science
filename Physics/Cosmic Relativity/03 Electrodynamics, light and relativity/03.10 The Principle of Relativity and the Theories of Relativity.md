> [!TLDR]
> The source distinguishes the Principle of Relativity from theories that implement it with different physical assumptions.

## Physical question and model

How is a relativity principle different from a particular theory that implements it? A principle constrains possible laws; a theory adds transformation rules, dynamics, and measurement procedures. Different interpretations can satisfy the same limited test set.

## Lecture account

We discussed the Principle of Relativity in the previous chapter. The crucial distinction between the ‘Principle of Relativity’ and a ‘Theory of Relativity’ is very important. It was Galileo who first discussed in detail the impossibility to detect uniform motion using any local mechanical experiment. This became important in the later debate concerning the motion of the Earth. All empirically consistent theories of relativity should necessarily incorporate the PoR. Yet, there can be different theories of relativity, even mutually contradictory in their other physical aspects and consequences.

The universal Principle of Relativity, encompassing all of physics, was clearly stated and discussed by Poincaré, in 1904 or even earlier. Poincaré defined it as the invariance of the laws of physics in all inertially moving systems, in accordance with the impossibility to detect the motion of the Earth through the hypothetical ether by any local experiment. This was a conclusion arrived at from the collective experience from the several experiments we mentioned. The invariance implied by the Lorentz transformations was in the context of electrodynamics. Poincaré reasoned that only if the same transformations were applicable to all physical phenomena, including gravity, the PoR could be maintained as a general principle in physics.

Einstein called the same invariance in his paper by the identical name, ‘The Principle of Relativity’ [25]. The principle is common to the LPTR and the STR, though the two theories have entirely different physical basis; the LPTR has an absolute preferred frame (the ether) and Galilean propagation of light, whereas the STR rejects any notion of absolute rest and the relative velocity of light is an invariant constant in all inertial frames. Thus, it is clear that the PoR can be maintained without the assumption of the invariance of the relative velocity of light. In fact, we saw in this chapter that a major experimental demonstration of the PoR—the null result of the Michelson-Morley experiment—is explained after explicitly assuming that the relative velocity of light is Galilean, $\mechanical{c}\pm\mechanical{v}$. The important conclusion then is that the validity of the PoR in an experiment is not a proof of the correctness of the STR. The PoR is necessary for the validity of the STR, but is not sufficient. This should be obvious from the fact that the STR relies on the additional light hypothesis.

No experiment to date has shown any evidence for a violation of the PoR. Uniform inertial motion has absolutely no observable effect on any phenomenon in the physical world. It is important to stress the word ‘observable’, as we will see later. Unobservable does not mean non-existent in physics. Any valid theory of relativity should incorporate the PoR, either as a fundamental hypothesis or as a physical consequence of the structure of the theory. But, the PoR alone does not uniquely determine a theory of relativity; this is evident from the drastic differences between the STR and the LPTR.

> [!note]
> The Principle of Relativity and the null result on the Michelson-Morley experiments are consistent with the Lorentz-Poincaré theory of relativity, with an absolute frame of the ether and the Galilean propagation of light, as well as with Einstein's Special Theory of Relativity, with an invariant relative velocity of light in empty space.

The relevant question then is what is the correct theory of relativity and relativistic physical effects under motion that is consistent with the PoR and all other empirical results? The question is easily answered now, if we add the necessary requirement that the theory should be consistent with the factual cosmology. Another way of answering this question, emphasizing on the experimental evidence in the laboratory, is by exploring whether there are observable physical effects that depend on the absolute velocity of the moving body, referenced to a master preferred frame, or whether all motional physical effects depend only on the relative velocity between two reference frames. In the latter case, the entire physical analysis involves only the two frames in the situation, with no reference to the rest of the external world. The extensive studies during the development of electrodynamics, on physical effects during the relative motion between a conductor and a magnet, are of this kind.

When Einstein published the Special (Restricted) Theory of Relativity in 1905, he relied on a limited class of empirical results as his motivations to postulate the foundational principles [25, 28, 29]. We will carefully examine these empirical factors, the postulates, and the resulting theory in the next chapter.

## Interpretation and checks

Choose one experiment and specify the complete alternatives. A shared null result is not evidence that their untested premises are all correct. A useful transfer problem is to compare an isolated mechanical cabin with an optical experiment exposed to a moving material medium: the external environment adds physical structure without automatically changing the relativity principle for the fundamental equations.
