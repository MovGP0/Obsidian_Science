> [!TLDR]
> Michelson's laboratory measurements followed earlier methods that timed light over a known round-trip path.

## Physical question and model

How does a rotating mirror turn a light-travel delay into a measurable angular displacement? Define the complete outbound and return path and the mirror rotation rate. The readout depends on how much the mirror turns while the pulse is away, with an additional optical factor from reflection geometry.

## Lecture account

In the ‘modern’ era of laboratory investigations, a series of experiments were done by Albert Michelson, starting in the 1880s and continuing till his demise, to determine the speed of light [3]. These should not be confused with his more discussed experiment with E. W. Morley to detect the motion of the Earth through the hypothetical ether, by looking for a change in a pattern of the optical fringes in an interferometer.

There were already two notable measurements of the speed of light in the 19th century, one by H. Fizeau (1850) and another by L. Foucault (1862). Fizeau used a “toothed wheel” to chop light periodically on its propagation towards a mirror at the distance $\mechanical{L}$, as well as on its return after reflection. Since the wheel rotates to partially block light during the interval $2\mechanical{L}/\mechanical{c}$, the speed of light can be calculated from a careful comparison of the relative extinction. With a distance of about $8.2\,\unit{km}$ and a wheel with 720 teeth, a rate of just about $12.5\,\unit{s^{-1}}$ (revolutions per second) was enough to periodically block light, on its return. The speed of light was measured to a relative accuracy of a few percent. Foucault improved on the technique by replacing the toothed wheel with a lens, a rotating plane mirror, and a concave mirror at the focus. The light ray returned to the source point after reflection, retracing its path irrespective of the angle of the plane mirror, as long as the mirror was static. However, if the mirror rotated through an angle $\mechanical{\theta}$ *between the instant light bounced off the mirror in its path towards the concave mirror and the instant of its second encounter during its return*, then the image after the lens would be shifted from the source position. Knowing the rotation angular frequency $\mechanical{\omega}$ and the distance, the speed of light is calculated. Michelson's experiments to measure the speed of light signal relied on this idea used earlier by Foucault, but with many modifications to improve the precision by two orders of magnitude.

The idea of the experiment is illustrated and explained in the schematic diagram Fig. 3.2. The actual optical arrangement was much more elaborate, in an era when lasers were not available. The distance of $35\,\unit{km}$ was measured from precision surveying, accurate to $0.5\,\unit{m}$. The best measurements in these yield a value of $299793\,\unit{km/s}$ with a standard deviation of a few km/s, or a part in $10^5$. Later he added a measurement in an evacuated tube, $1.6\,\unit{km}$ in length. This had a slightly lower precision.

The returned image coincides with the source only if the next facet replaces the earlier facet exactly, from face 1 to face 2. For the octagon, ωT = 45°. The source's matching condition is $\mechanical{\theta}=\mechanical{\omega}\signal{T}=45^{\circ}$ with $\signal{T}=2\mechanical{L}/\mechanical{c}$.

I should emphasise that such measurements always involve the two-way propagation of light. The toothed wheel or the rotating mirror method do not have the precision to address the question whether the velocity of light depends on the velocity of the reference frame of the Earth. Considering its orbital velocity of $30\,\unit{km/s}$, the maximum change in the one-way relative velocity could be about $\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}\approx10^{-4}$. But, with a two-way measurement, the signal is only of the order of $\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2<10^{-8}$. Therefore, such experiments give just the average speed of light in (nearly) empty space.

Later direct measurements that used light modulation with Kerr cells, and electronic fast detection methods, managed to improve the precision by a factor of 10, to a part in a million. However, the systematic error is dominated by the need to do accurate surveying of the distance by purely mechanical means. Though the precision achieved in the measurement of the distance by direct mechanical surveying is remarkable ($10^{-6}$), it limits the determination of the speed of light to the same level of accuracy. The more recent determinations chose to measure the frequency and wavelength of light (microwaves) in cavities and then multiply together these quantities to obtain a notional (theoretical) ‘velocity’ [4]. The precision of course is better, but one should keep in mind two facts about such determinations. One, the technique necessarily requires the *two-way propagation of light* and two, the measurement is of the wavelength and frequency, and not of the velocity, which is the ratio of the distance and the duration of propagation.

## Interpretation and checks

Check the limiting case of zero mirror rotation: there is no timing-dependent angular offset. A longer path or faster mirror gives a larger offset for the same light speed. Alignment, refractive index of residual gas, path calibration, and angular calibration limit the result. This apparatus measures propagation delay and must be distinguished from Michelson's separate interferometer test for directional anisotropy.
