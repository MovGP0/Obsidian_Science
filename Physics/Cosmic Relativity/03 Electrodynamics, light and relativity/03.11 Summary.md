## Lecture account

In this chapter, I recounted certain important milestones in the early experimental research in electrodynamics and optics that decisively influenced the development of the theories of relativity. The different experiments that I discussed pertained to (a) the determination of the velocity of light by Roemer, Fizeau, Foucault, and Michelson, (b) the Doppler shift of light, (c) the phenomenon of stellar aberration and its explanation by Bradley and Fresnel, (d) the dragging of light in moving material media, its analysis by Fresnel and experiment by Fizeau and (e) the attempt to detect the ether medium by studying the anisotropy of the propagation of light in frames moving through the stationary ether, represented by the famous experiment of Michelson and Morley.

After clarifying that the M-M experiment with its two-way propagation of light was meant as a test of the Principle of Relativity, I explained the simple and significant proof that it cannot demarcate between the Galilean propagation of light in a theory of relativity with an absolute frame, and the invariant relative velocity of light in the STR. It was further stressed that the PoR is an experimentally verified principle that is common to different theories of relativity, whereas the propagation of light rests on an independent hypothesis that requires independent empirical verification.

There were several non-optical electromagnetic experiments that provided the clues to the modern theories of relativity. I discussed the Trouton-Noble experiment, of monitoring the angle of a suspended charged parallel plate capacitor. This was prompted by the perception that the PoR could be violated due to the difference in the total energy of such a capacitor, when in absolute motion. Then, the features of the historically and operationally important phenomena of unipolar induction were discussed, highlighting the aspects that are clearly in conflict with a theory in which the observable physical effects depend only on the relative velocity between a conductor and an electromagnetic source like a magnet. We discussed Faraday's experimental observations that contradict Einstein's assertions regarding such phenomena.

I concluded this chapter with a clarification on the difference between the Principle of Relativity and the more general framework of a ‘theory of relativity’, which typically has additional postulates. This was illustrated citing the Lorentz-Poincaré theory and Einstein's STR, both being consistent with the experimental evidence. The observed validity of the PoR alone does not guarantee the correctness of a theory of relativity.

Having surveyed, albeit briefly, some important steps and physical facts in the development of the theories of relativity, it is now appropriate to review the conceptual, physical, and the empirical basis of the Special Theory of Relativity, as it emerged in 1905.
