> [!TLDR]
> The author argues that motion through a matter-filled Universe requires cosmic gravity to be included in dynamics.

## Physical question and model

Does a measured cosmic rest frame require a preferred form of the local laws? An observer moving through the cosmic radiation can measure an anisotropic distribution. This is a property of the state of that radiation. Distinguish it from a failure of the symmetry of the equations that describe local experiments.

## Lecture account

Cosmic Relativity is necessary for the logical and empirical consistency with the observed fact that the Universe is filled with matter distributed uniformly, nearly at the critical density. It is easy to see that any motion in a space that is isotropically and homogeneously filled with matter breaks the isotropy of space, in contrast to the motion in an empty space, which is always isotropic and homogeneous. Both spaces are homogeneous and isotropic for an observer who is in a state of rest. While an empty space remains isotropic for an observer in motion as well, a matter-filled space becomes anisotropic for an observer in motion, because of the current of matter in the frame of the observer, flowing past at the velocity of the motion of the frame. This is not just a superficial kinematic difference, but a crucial physical difference because of the relativistic gravitational effects of the matter. In particular, there are new gravitational effects akin to the magnetic effects in electromagnetism. This obviously implies a fundamental difference in the basis of dynamics and relativity in a factually matter-filled space, compared to the dynamics and relativity in an empty space. Cosmic Relativity is the resulting paradigm and theory.

The unavoidable necessity and importance of Cosmic Relativity can be seen easily by considering a quantitative thought experiment, based on the known and verified physical effects in electrodynamics. Imagine that there is a tenuous distribution of electric charges in the Universe, with very small density, uniformly and isotropically distributed. This could be due to a slight hypothetical difference in the charge of an electron and a proton, as Einstein and later H. Bondi had considered [9, 10], but at a much smaller and undetectable level of less than a part in $10^{40}$. Even such a tiny charge asymmetry will generate a uniform electric potential of more than a volt. If we did not know about such a distribution in the Universe, we would not be able to detect this by any experiment in the laboratory in which the charges particles move uniformly (inertially); then the usual Maxwell's equations and the Lorentz force law would remain valid. However, if charged particles were accelerated, they would experience new forces, without any obvious sources around, because in reality there is the invisible uniform charge distribution. The electromagnetic potentials would become time-dependent for an accelerated particle, through the time-dependent velocity. A moving charged particle would deflect in any slowly rotating laboratory, without any apparent magnetic field present, and a ghostly torque would try to align a magnet in a rotating laboratory! We would be forced to call the new forces, proportional to the electric charge, ‘electromagnetic pseudo-forces’, and include them in an ad hoc manner, in the scheme of the dynamical equations of electromagnetism. The physics of electromagnetism would remain incomplete and inconsistent until the tiny charge asymmetry in the cosmic matter distribution is discovered. Fortunately, the Universe is electrically neutral and Maxwell electrodynamics does not face this problem. But, Einstein's gravity theory does, because his equations of gravity are written with a non-existent empty space and the Minkowski metric as its fundamental background. There is no background gravity in Einstein's General Theory of Relativity (GTR). But the factual real space is not empty—the vast Universe is filled with matter and energy and these are precisely the charges of gravity. *The Universe is gravitationally charged, and the density of the matter distribution is large enough to generate easily detectable inductive gravitational potentials and forces that affect and control dynamics*. While the resulting potentials remain uniform and undetectable during any uniform inertial motion, they become time-dependent and act as real gravitational forces for accelerated particles.

Therefore, the prevailing understanding of the Principle of Relativity needs to be revised. The reason for the invariance of physical phenomena and their laws under uniform motion is not the featureless nature of the empty background space (vacuum). The factual space of physics is not at all empty. On the contrary, it is everywhere filled with matter and energy. The matter-energy in the Universe is the source of a large uniform gravitational potential, which remains uniform, with no spatial and temporal variations, in inertial motion through the homogeneous matter-filled Universe. But uniform potentials cannot be detected; all physical measurements are comparisons that require a deviation from uniformity in space or time. Therefore, the state of motion cannot be detected in any experiment until space-time variations in the relativistic potentials are induced by a non-inertial (accelerated) motion through the matter-energy distribution.

Cosmic Relativity is also a necessary logical consequence of thinking about clocks, waves and quantum entities moving in the Universe in the presence of vast amounts of matter. Gravity has a physical influence on the phase of such entities, as any interaction has, and this is easily computable when a finite amount of matter is present. One consequence, easily understood, is that the gravitational potential experienced by a particle at rest relative to a mass distribution is different from what it experiences when in motion. Conventional theories of relativity including the standard theory of Special Relativity do not rely on such physical effects, and the observed effects of time dilation etc. are attributed to the *relative motion between different frames*. Thus, a physical change is not linked to a physical interaction in the STR. However, we know definitely that galaxies and clusters of galaxies exist and even the gravitational potential of matter up to the Virgo cluster generates a measurable difference in the time dilations experienced by moving atomic clocks. But, even such an obvious physical effect is missing from the current theoretical description of motional time dilation. This is a very serious matter. If we consider the larger extent of the Universe that is causally connected to us today, it becomes clear that the cosmic gravitational effect on moving clocks is as large as what we observe in experiments with different kinds of clocks. There is only one logical explanation—that the time dilation effect we conventionally attributed in the STR to the kinematic relative motion between observers' frames, is in fact a gravitational effect of the matter in the Universe. Then, there is nothing like the kinematic time dilation of Special Relativity! In Cosmic Relativity, the clock that moves relative to the matter-frame of the Universe is the one experiencing time dilation, by a factor $1/\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^{2}/\mechanical{c}^{2}}$, where $\mechanical{v}$ is an ‘absolute velocity’ that is possibly time-dependent. This velocity is the same as what is measured using the dipole anisotropy of the Cosmic Microwave Background Radiation (CMBR). There is no distinction between inertial and non-inertial motion in Cosmic Relativity. Note that this picture leaves no room for paradoxes. Not a single problem with possible ambiguities leading to multiple answers arises in the framework of Cosmic Relativity.

## Interpretation and checks

A gas can define a rest frame even when its microscopic equations are Lorentz covariant. The same distinction matters for the cosmological matter distribution. A discriminating experiment must find a local observable with a velocity dependence not accounted for by known environmental interactions. Predict what changes when a detector moves relative to an isotropic radiation bath; absorption rates can change without redefining the vacuum light-speed law.
