> [!TLDR]
> This section examines the gravitational effect of the matter and energy distributed through the Universe.

## Physical question and model

How large is the potential of an idealised cosmic mass distribution? The shell calculation assumes a static Euclidean sphere, constant density, Newtonian superposition, and a chosen outer radius. It estimates a potential scale; it is not a solution of the expanding-Universe field equations.

## Lecture account

All physical phenomena happen in our real matter-filled Universe. It is in this matter-filled Universe that we construct and test our theories. As I emphasised already, the current fundamental physical theories of relativity and dynamics were considered as mature and complete before we acquired any significant knowledge about the factual Universe and its matter content. Therefore, our current theories assume, as their basis, the *empty Minkowski space-time, which remains isotropic and homogeneous in every inertial frame*. Then, there is no privileged reference frame to define the true motion and there is no physical sense in the notion of absolute rest. There is no gravity in such a space. But the space of the factual Universe is not empty; *there is no place in the Universe we can find as empty space, devoid of matter and gravity*. We know that gravity affects and modifies spatial and temporal intervals, and affects the general features of dynamics of particles and light. There are enormous conceptual and quantitative differences between the physical phenomena in empty space and those in the gravitational potentials of vast amounts of matter.

We can get an idea of the missing physical content in the current theories, by estimating approximately the gravitational potential (g-potential) generated by all the matter in the Universe, but not included in these theories (Fig. 1.1). The g-potential of the Earth in my room is about $\mechanical{\phi}_e=-\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}_e/\mechanical{R}_e\simeq10^8\,\unit{m^2/s^2}$. Of course, we ‘feel’ only the gradient of this interaction potential, $\mechanical{g}=-\nabla\mechanical{\phi}_e\approx9.8\,\unit{m/s^2}$, because our body or our instruments cannot sense an interaction potential directly. The Sun's pull of gravity is much weaker, less than $0.01\,\unit{m/s^2}$, but its g-potential at the Earth ($\mechanical{\phi}_s=-\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}_s/\mechanical{R}_s\simeq10^9\,\unit{m^2/s^2}$) is 10 times larger than the Earth's g-potential! If one calculates the potential of the entire mass in the Milky-Way galaxy near the Earth, we get a value that is a thousand times larger, $10^{11}\,\unit{m^2/s^2}$. This should not be surprising because the g-potential of a mass varies as $1/\mechanical{r}$, whereas the amount of distributed matter and its mass increase with the volume of the space occupied, as $\mechanical{r}^3$. Then, the total g-potential increases with the extent of the matter distribution, as $\mechanical{r}^2$; that is how the distant matter dominates in its contribution to the g-potential.

We can estimate the g-potential of the entire matter in the Universe approximately, assuming that the cosmic matter is uniformly distributed on the large scale, with the observed average density $10^{-26}\,\unit{kg/m^3}$ and a spatial extent that is approximately the Hubble radius $\mechanical{c}T\simeq10^{26}\,\unit{m}$ (Fig. 1.2). Since the age of the Universe ($T$) is finite, the potential is also finite. The size of the Universe might be much larger than what we can see today, but what is important for the calculation is only the matter up to a distance $\mechanical{d}=\mechanical{c}T_u$. The matter outside this ‘sphere of causal influence’ is not yet visible, and its gravity is not yet felt here. The magnitude of the potential due to the annular volume is $d\mechanical{\phi}=(4\pi\mechanical{r}^2d\mechanical{r}\times\mechanical{G}\mechanical{\rho})/\mechanical{r}$. Integrating, we get

$$
\mechanical{\Phi}_u\simeq\int_0^{\mechanical{c}T_u}4\pi\mechanical{G}\mechanical{\rho}\mechanical{r}\,d\mechanical{r}=2\pi\mechanical{G}\mechanical{\rho}\mechanical{c}^2T_u^2. \tag{1.1}
$$

The approximate value of the uniform Newtonian gravitational potential of the cosmic matter-energy is $\mechanical{\Phi}_u\simeq10^{17}\,\unit{m^2/s^2}$, which is about a billion times larger than the potential due to the Earth. It is not only very large and dominating, but it is also physically very interesting and significant because it is numerically very close to the square of the speed of light!

> [!note]
> The magnitude of the Newtonian gravitational potential of the matter and energy in the Universe is close to the square of the velocity of light, $\mechanical{\Phi}_u\simeq\mechanical{c}^2$. This suggests a deep connection between relativistic effects and cosmic gravity.

A movement through the Universe converts the average isotropic distribution of the matter into an anisotropic current of matter, directed opposite to the motion. The modification of the familiar ‘scalar’ gravitational potential into a multi-component tensor of velocity-dependent potentials, due to the motion, is behind most of the novel results in Cosmic Relativity. It is well known from electrodynamics that the Coulomb potential transforms to a multi-component vector potential when relative motion is involved. The physical situation in gravity is similar, only more varied and complex. At the primary fundamental level of matter, there are just the distributions of the charges and their currents, as the primary sources of all phenomena, both in electrodynamics and gravity. In a local theoretical description, these sources generate the potentials. In gravity, the motion relative to the cosmic frame at the velocity $\mechanical{v}$ results in the vector-like potential $-\mechanical{v}\mechanical{\Phi}_u/\mechanical{c}$. This is very similar to the generation of the magnetic vector potential in electrodynamics. All the physical effects in relativity and dynamics that we have ever observed are the consequences of these relativistic cosmic gravitational potentials.

## Interpretation and checks

A shell contributes a mass proportional to its radius squared times its thickness; dividing by the radius in the Newtonian potential leaves an integrand proportional to radius. The integral therefore grows as the outer radius squared. Check that gravitational constant times density times radius squared has units of speed squared. Doubling the cutoff multiplies the estimate by four.

This cutoff sensitivity is essential: a Hubble radius, a particle horizon, and light speed times cosmic age are not generally identical. A spatially constant Newtonian potential has an arbitrary zero and no force gradient. Its claimed effect on inertia needs a separate coupling law. Standard cosmology already includes cosmic stress–energy; it is not an empty-universe theory. Compare the model assumptions with [Carroll's relativity lectures](https://arxiv.org/abs/gr-qc/9712019).
