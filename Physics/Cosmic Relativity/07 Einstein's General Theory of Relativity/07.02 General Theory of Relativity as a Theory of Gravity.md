## Physical question and model

What distinguishes a gravitational field equation from a coordinate transformation? The equation links curvature to stress–energy and must recover the Poisson equation in a weak, nearly static limit. Specify the curvature-sign and metric-sign conventions before comparing formulas.

## Lecture account

How does extending the applicability of the STR and the Principle of Relativity to general motion become a relativistic theory of gravity, a fundamental interaction between matter? Einstein clarified this in his 1916 GTR paper [8]:

> It will be seen from these reflections that in pursuing the general theory of relativity we shall be led to a theory of gravitation since we are able to ‘produce’ a gravitational field merely by changing the system of co-ordinates.

Einstein explicitly referred to the GTR treating gravity as a fictitious force that could be ‘produced’ at will by choosing suitably accelerated frames without matter. Thus, Einstein’s gravity is not tied to matter sources, just as fictitious forces such as centrifugal force are thought to be. Centrifugal force can likewise be ‘produced’ by moving in a curved path. It is remarkable that assuming relativity in a frame in general motion, then extending it to a uniform gravitational field through the equivalence principle, produced a *complete theory of gravity*. The correspondence between the gravitational field and the mathematical representation of reference-frame coordinates is explained in Einstein’s monograph, *Meaning of Relativity* [1].

> Space-time regions of finite extent are, in general, not Galilean, so that a gravitational field cannot be done away with by any choice of co-ordinates in a finite region. There is, therefore, no choice of co-ordinates for which the metrical relations of the special theory of relativity hold in a finite region. But the invariant $d\mechanical{s}$ always exists for two neighbouring points (events) of the continuum. This invariant $d\mechanical{s}$ may be expressed in arbitrary co-ordinates. If one observes that the local $d\mechanical{X}_\nu$ may be expressed linearly in terms of the co-ordinate differentials $d\mechanical{x}$, $d\mechanical{s}^2$ may be expressed in the form $d\mechanical{s}^2=\mechanical{g}_{\mu\nu}d\mechanical{x}_\mu d\mechanical{x}_\nu$. The functions $\mechanical{g}_{\mu\nu}$ describe, with respect to the arbitrarily chosen system of co-ordinates, the metrical relations of the space-time continuum and also the gravitational field.

Interaction potentials, gravitational or electromagnetic, are not observable locally; only their spatial and temporal variations (gradient, curl, time derivative, etc.) are observable. Thus, metric components $\mechanical{g}_{\mu\nu}$ corresponding to potentials are not directly measurable, although their relative values across an interval have physical meaning. Since the equivalence principle nullifies the gravitational field $\mechanical{g}$ by free fall, and $\mechanical{g}=-\nabla\mechanical{\phi}_g$, first derivatives of metric components are not invariant physical observables either. The field symbol ‘g’ and metric symbol ‘g’ are unrelated. Hence, *the true gravitational field should be described by second derivatives of the metric*. Such gravity was known for one metric component, $\mechanical{g}_{00}\simeq-(1+\mechanical{\phi}/\mechanical{c}^2)$, namely Newton’s law in Poisson form,

$$
\nabla^2\mechanical{\phi}_g(\mechanical{x})=4\pi\mechanical{G}\mechanical{\rho}(\mechanical{x}), \tag{7.1}
$$

where $\mechanical{\rho}(\mechanical{x})$ is matter density. The relation between $\mechanical{g}_{00}$ and $\mechanical{\phi}_g$ was known from the elementary derivation of gravitational time dilation through the equivalence principle. However, $\nabla^2\mechanical{\phi}_g$ is not the gravitational field $\mechanical{g}$, but represents tidal fields: spatial gradients in three directions of the field’s three components, giving nine components. Across a separation $\delta\mechanical{l}$, these force gradients are proportional to $(\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}(\mechanical{r})/\mechanical{r}^3)\delta\mechanical{l}$. Time dependence of the three spatial field components adds another three. The characteristic relativistic phenomena—relative time dilation for separated clocks and its time dependence—bring the total to 16 components. Symmetry under coordinate exchange leaves ten independent components for describing gravity.

The actual situation is more complicated than this simplified description: curvature is fully described by the Riemann tensor with 20 independent components. Only the full tensor can describe tidal fields in matter-free regions. A limited generalisation of the source, the right side of (7.1), to include mass–energy equivalence replaces $\mechanical{\rho}$ with $\mechanical{\rho}+\energy{\epsilon}/\mechanical{c}^2$, where $\energy{\epsilon}$ is energy density. This is insufficient because the metric has many more components. Both matter density and its currents (momenta) must be included in the source matrix.

The final theory was much harder to develop than expected. To compare Einstein’s early attempts, it is useful to display his final gravity equation, connecting geometry (curvature terms $\mechanical{R}_{ik}$ and $\mechanical{R}$, and metric $\mechanical{g}_{ik}$) with the energy–momentum of matter $\energy{T}_{ik}$:

$$
\mechanical{R}_{ik}-\frac12\mechanical{g}_{ik}\mechanical{R}=\frac{8\pi\mechanical{G}}{\mechanical{c}^4}\energy{T}_{ik}. \tag{7.2}
$$

This was reached after many wrong destinations. In a paper presented to the Prussian Academy on 4 November 1915 [9], Einstein wrote about equations developed and published the previous year:

> My efforts in recent years were directed toward basing a general theory of relativity, also for nonuniform motion, upon the supposition of relativity. I believed indeed to have found the only law of gravitation that complies with a reasonably formulated postulate of general relativity;... Renewed criticism showed to me that this truth is absolutely impossible to show in the manner suggested.

This was a large diversion after years of work. Einstein referred to his faulty field equations in the previous year’s paper [10], covariant only under restricted linear transformations. In that paper, he was critical of his theory’s strange features: “...I cannot help but see, after these confessions, a pitying smile creep over the face of the reader...”. He then allowed arbitrary coordinate (frame) transformations, but the renewed equation remained incomplete:

$$
\mechanical{R}_{ik}=\mechanical{\kappa}\energy{T}_{ik}. \tag{7.3}
$$

Here $\mechanical{R}_{ik}$ is the Ricci curvature tensor, $\energy{T}_{ik}$ is the energy–momentum tensor, and $\mechanical{\kappa}=8\pi\mechanical{G}/\mechanical{c}^4$. To obtain this equation, Einstein assumed without foundation that the matter tensor obeyed $\sum\energy{T}_{ii}=0$, as the electromagnetic energy–momentum tensor does. This was an act of desperation: he knew that $\sum\energy{T}_{ii}\geq\mechanical{\rho}$ (mass density) for non-relativistic matter and is generally nonzero. This supposition can hold for vacuum results, where there is no matter. Then $\mechanical{R}_{ik}=0$. Thus, even in his 18 November 1915 paper calculating Mercury’s perihelion precession, “no gravitation theory had achieved till then”, he had not reached the final GTR equation.

> [!note]
> The source compares $\sum\energy{T}_{ii}$ directly with mass density $\mechanical{\rho}$. In SI units, an energy-density comparison requires the corresponding factor of $\mechanical{c}^2$ or an explicit unit convention.

Just a week later Einstein reached the final equation, helped partly by D. Hilbert’s account of an independent mathematical derivation of the correct field equations including the Ricci scalar $\mechanical{R}$. Einstein returned to an *earlier realisation*: conservation of energy–momentum on the right side demands a matching tensor for gravity on the left. This is now called the Einstein tensor,

$$
\mechanical{G}_{ik}=\mechanical{R}_{ik}-\frac12\mechanical{g}_{ik}\mechanical{R}=\mechanical{\kappa}\energy{T}_{ik}. \tag{7.4}
$$

Einstein wrote the equation as

$$
\mechanical{R}_{ik}=\mechanical{\kappa}\energy{T}_{ik}-\frac{\mechanical{\kappa}}2\mechanical{g}_{ik}\energy{T}. \tag{7.5}
$$

Since full contraction gives $\mechanical{R}=\mechanical{\kappa}\energy{T}$, both forms are equivalent. Without matter, $\energy{T}_{ik}=0$ and $\mechanical{R}=0$, so the equation reduces to $\mechanical{R}_{ik}=0$.

> [!note]
> The source prints $\mechanical{R}=\mechanical{\kappa}\energy{T}$. Contracting equation (7.4) in four dimensions instead gives $\mechanical{R}=-\mechanical{\kappa}\energy{T}$, which yields equation (7.5). The source’s sign has been retained above.

Einstein derived the relativistic equation of motion, resembling Newton’s $\mechanical{a}=\mechanical{F}/\mechanical{m}$, by first assuming inertial motion for a free particle. In a straight line in a freely falling frame, with no forces, acceleration is zero:

$$
\frac{d^2\mechanical{x}^i}{d\mechanical{\tau}^2}=0. \tag{7.6}
$$

Transformation to a general coordinate frame gives

$$
\frac{d^2\mechanical{x}^i}{d\mechanical{\tau}^2}+\mechanical{\Gamma}^i_{jk}\frac{d\mechanical{x}^j}{d\mechanical{\tau}}\frac{d\mechanical{x}^k}{d\mechanical{\tau}}=0. \tag{7.7}
$$

The quantities $\mechanical{\Gamma}^i_{jk}$ involve derivatives of the metric for the chosen coordinates and are analogues of acceleration. Later, Einstein derived this equation from the vacuum equation $\mechanical{R}_{ik}=0$.

The binding foundation of the EEP can be seen in (7.7). When $d\mechanical{x}/(\mechanical{c}\,dt)=\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$ is small, terms corresponding to $\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$ and $\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2$ can be neglected relative to the $dt/d\mechanical{\tau}$ term. Then

$$
\frac{d^2\mechanical{x}^i}{d\mechanical{\tau}^2}+\mechanical{\Gamma}^i_{00}\left(\frac{\mechanical{c}\,dt}{d\mechanical{\tau}}\right)^2=0. \tag{7.8}
$$

The quantity $\mechanical{\Gamma}^i_{00}$ is

$$
\frac12\mechanical{g}^{ij}\left(\frac{d\mechanical{g}_{j0}}{\mechanical{c}\,dt}+\frac{d\mechanical{g}_{0j}}{\mechanical{c}\,dt}-\frac{d\mechanical{g}_{00}}{d\mechanical{x}^j}\right).
$$

Since $\mechanical{g}_{0i}\simeq\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$ and time derivatives for the car are small, only the last term is significant:

$$
\mechanical{\Gamma}^i_{00}\simeq-\frac12\mechanical{g}^{ij}\frac{\partial\mechanical{g}_{00}}{\partial\mechanical{x}^j}. \tag{7.9}
$$

For weak acceleration, $\mechanical{g}_{ij}=\eta_{ij}+\mechanical{h}_{ij}$ with small $\mechanical{h}_{ij}$. Thus $\mechanical{g}_{00}=1+\mechanical{h}_{00}$, and the equation of motion in a general accelerated frame is

$$
\frac{d^2\mechanical{x}^i}{dt^2}=\frac{\mechanical{c}^2}{2}\nabla\mechanical{h}_{00}. \tag{7.10}
$$

*This equation of motion in an accelerated (coordinate-transformed) frame can be related to motion in a gravitational field if, and only if, we assume the EEP.* A gravitational field is ‘produced’ from the acceleration $\mechanical{\Gamma}^i_{00}=\nabla\mechanical{h}_{00}$ by applying the EEP. Then $(\mechanical{c}^2/2)\nabla\mechanical{h}_{00}=\mechanical{\mathbf g}=-\nabla\mechanical{\phi}$, giving Newtonian dynamics in a gravitational field:

$$
\frac{d^2\mechanical{\mathbf x}}{dt^2}=\mechanical{\mathbf g}. \tag{7.11}
$$

## Interpretation and checks

The contracted Bianchi identity makes the covariant divergence of the Einstein tensor vanish. A source proportional only to the Ricci tensor does not generally satisfy that conservation condition; the trace term matters. Check the trace-reversed equation against the original before substituting matter variables. A geodesic equation then predicts motion of an ideal test body only when nongravitational forces, finite-size effects, and relevant spin couplings are negligible.
