> [!TLDR]
> Moving and rotating masses generate gravitomagnetic potentials and fields, producing effects such as Lense–Thirring precession and torques on spin.

## Physical question and model

How do rotating sources affect gyroscopes in general relativity? Use the weak-field, slow-rotation approximation and specify a gyroscope worldline and reference orientation. The off-diagonal metric terms encode source-current effects, but their coordinate values alone are not the measured precession.

## Lecture account

One unexpected GTR prediction was ‘magnetic-like’ gravitational potentials and fields (Fig. 7.5 in [[07.08 The Achievements of the GTR]]). This would have been an early guess if Maxwell electrodynamics had guided a relativistic gravity theory, at least approximately, but was unexpected along the actual route. Its source is simple: relative matter currents, or momentum densities $\mechanical{\rho}\mechanical{v}_i$, generate additional ‘vector’ potentials $\mechanical{g}_{0i}$, whose derivatives produce forces. The potentials are of order $\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$, and forces or torques from rotation are proportional to $\mechanical{B}_g\propto\mechanical{\Omega}\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}/(\mechanical{R}\mechanical{c}^2)$. Attainable laboratory values of $\mechanical{B}_g$ are no larger than about $10^{-22}$, so terrestrial effects are very small. They are significant in astrophysical systems with compact, rapidly moving and rotating masses. Their spatial structure is the familiar magnetic dipole structure. Einstein’s pre-GTR paper on gravitational induction, analogous to electromagnetic induction, answered the question affirmatively [22]. Other researchers analysed it soon after publication of the GTR; its prominent effect is now called Lense–Thirring precession [5, 24].

Earth’s surface gravitomagnetic field is about $\mechanical{\Omega}\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}/(\mechanical{R}\mechanical{c}^2)\simeq10^{-14}\,\unit{rad/s}$. Its effect on a freely falling gyroscope (gravitational spin moment) is analogous to magnetic torque and precession, $\mechanical{\boldsymbol\tau}=\magnetic{\boldsymbol\mu}\times\magnetic{\mathbf B}$. The gravitomagnetic torque on a spinning body is $\mechanical{\boldsymbol\tau}_g=\mechanical{\mathbf s}\times\mechanical{\mathbf B}_g/2$, where $\mechanical{\mathbf s}$ is spin moment (see Chapter 13 for a detailed treatment). The key difference is that the precession rate does not depend on spin magnitude, owing to equivalence of inertia and mass. The effect has been measured to about $5\%$ accuracy through the small orbital precession of LAGEOS artificial satellites and in Gravity Probe-B, a dedicated space experiment monitoring an ultraprecise superconducting gyro assembly (see the source’s Section 7.10).

This also reveals a ‘cosmic gap’ in the GTR. With an empty-space Minkowski metric as its basis and no cosmic matter as the factual gravitational background, it misses the largest, most visible gravitomagnetic effects, such as the Coriolis force. Beyond centrifugal and Coriolis forces (the $\mechanical{\mathbf v}\times\mechanical{\mathbf B}_g$ magnetic force), many phenomena arise from the cosmic gravitomagnetic potential and induced fields in linear motion and uniform rotation. Examples are Fresnel drag, stellar aberration, forces and torques on spinning particles in rotating frames, and fine-structure splitting of atomic spectra. We will see these as physical effects in Cosmic Relativity.

## Interpretation and checks

A precession comparison needs the spin transport law and the instrument's axes. Separate frame dragging from geodetic precession caused by motion in a static curved background. Check that reversing source spin reverses the dragging contribution while the static-source part can remain. Laboratory estimates need stated units and geometry; a bare small number for a field strength is not a complete observable prediction.
