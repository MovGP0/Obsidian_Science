> [!TLDR]
> Mach’s proposal that inertia arises through interaction with cosmic matter guided Einstein’s early work, but conflicted with other demands on his theory.

## Physical question and model

How much of Mach's idea follows from Einstein's equations? Distinguish a matter solution selecting a convenient cosmic rest frame from the stronger requirement that all inertial structure be uniquely determined by matter. That stronger condition depends on the admissible solutions and boundary data.

## Lecture account

Beyond the Principle of Relativity and mass–inertia equivalence, Einstein was guided by what he called Mach’s principle [16]. This referred to Mach’s suggestion [17] that inertial forces on a moving body arise through interaction with the Universe’s other matter, rather than absolute space defining true motion as Newton proposed (Section 2.2). Einstein’s developing writings show this influence. In his 1913 article ‘On the present state of the problem of gravitation’ [28], he wrote:

> ...if the inertia of a body can increase due to cumulation of masses in its vicinity, then we have no choice but to view the inertia of a point as being caused by the existence of the other masses. Thus, inertia appears to be caused by a sort of interaction between the mass point to be accelerated and all of the other mass points. ...that inertial resistance is nothing else but a resistance of the considered body A to relative acceleration with respect to the totality of all of the other bodies B, C, etc. It is well known that this standpoint was first defended with perfect rigour and lucidity by E. Mach in his history of mechanics, so that I can simply refer to his arguments here... I shall call the conception sketched here ‘the hypothesis of the relativity of inertia’.

His next sentence—“To avoid misunderstandings, let me repeat here that, just as Mach, I do not think that the relativity of inertia is a logical necessity.”—shows that he missed the implication that *Mach’s conception needs an absolute frame, whose only logical and physical possibility is the Universe with all its matter and gravity*. The phrase “a sort of interaction”, without identifying gravity, is already problematic. But gravity was not an interaction in his theory. Inertia resulting from interaction with all other matter implies a matter-filled Universe and a universal interaction. Such a Universe is a unique privileged frame: only one frame has matter at rest, apart from angular orientation. Thus, relativity of inertia is logically and physically necessary in any Universe with matter and gravity. General coordinate invariance and Mach’s principle are incompatible: the latter implies an absolute dynamical reference frame, Galilean light propagation, and denial of the STR.

In his obituary of E. Mach, Einstein wrote:

> The contemplations on Newton’s experiment with the pail demonstrate how close his mind was to the demands of relativity in a wider sense (relativity of accelerations). Admittedly, the vivid consciousness was missing that the equivalence of inertial and gravitational mass elicits a postulate of relativity in a wider sense, because we are not in a position to decide by experiments if the falling of a body relative to a coordinate system is caused by the presence of a gravitational field or by a state of acceleration of the coordinate system.

The relation to Mach’s idea is explicit in the 1918 GTR review [18]:

> The theory, as I see it today, is based upon three fundamental aspects which, however, are by no means independent of one another... a) Principle of Relativity..., b) Principle of Equivalence... c) Mach’s Principle. The G-field is completely determined by the masses of the bodies. Since mass and energy are—according to the results of the special theory of relativity—the same, and since energy is formally described by the symmetric energy tensor, it follows that the G-field is caused and determined by the energy tensor of matter.

Einstein distinguished Mach’s principle from the other two and explained why it might not hold despite his conviction:

> Principle (b) was the starting point of the entire theory, and has brought with it the formulation of principle (a); it certainly cannot be abandoned as long as one clings to the basic ideas of the theoretical system. Mach’s principle (c) is a different story. The necessity to uphold it is by no means shared by all colleagues; but I myself feel it is absolutely necessary to satisfy it.
>
> Originally, I suggested for the field equations of gravitation

$$
\mechanical{G}_{\mu\nu}=\mechanical{\kappa}(\energy{T}_{\mu\nu}-\mechanical{g}_{\mu\nu}\energy{T}/2). \tag{7.32}
$$

> However, these field equations do not satisfy postulate (c), because they allow for the solution $\mechanical{g}_{\mu\nu}=\text{constant}$, $\energy{T}_{\mu\nu}=0$. In contradiction to Machs principle, the equations allow for a G-field without any generating matter.

Einstein and many colleagues did not recognise that *there is only one Universe to consider, because the theory operates and is tested in this Universe*. He missed the significance of Mach’s statement that “the Universe is not twice given”. As Einstein said, GTR equations allow a field $\mechanical{g}_{\mu\nu}$ without a source $\energy{T}_{\mu\nu}$, contrary to Mach’s principle. *Since the Universe with all its matter exists, the equations are incomplete in this Universe.* This should follow from fact and logic; otherwise, reflection on everything’s unavoidable gravitational connection to everything else may help [29].

Einstein continued that a constant $\mechanical{\lambda}$ term could make the equations respect Mach’s principle. This was the wrong direction: the energy–momentum trace would then represent abnormal matter, now called the cosmological constant,

$$
\mechanical{G}_{\mu\nu}-\mechanical{\lambda}\mechanical{g}_{\mu\nu}=\mechanical{\kappa}(\energy{T}_{\mu\nu}-\mechanical{g}_{\mu\nu}\energy{T}/2). \tag{7.33}
$$

This was a desperate effort to retain Mach’s principle. Crucial empirical knowledge of the Universe’s size and matter content was lacking in 1918 and would remain so for at least a decade. Einstein still tried to retain the principle after examples suggesting ‘gravitational fields without matter’:

> It would be unsatisfactory, in my opinion, if a world without matter were possible. Rather, it should be the case that the $\mechanical{g}_{\mu\nu}$-field is fully determined by matter and cannot exist without the latter. This is the core of what I mean by the requirement of the relativity of inertia. (Einstein’s letter to W. de Sitter, 24 March 1917)

This attraction to Mach’s principle eventually declined to extreme rejection, partly through his effort to ‘derive’ all matter from a unified field. His final statement was:

> In my view one should no longer speak of Mach’s principle at all. It dates back to the time in which one thought that the “ponderable bodies” are the only physically real entities and that all elements of the theory which are not completely determined by them should be avoided. (Einstein’s letter to F. Pirani, 1954)

An analysis of this change is outside our subject. Its reason is confusion between Machian ‘relative existence’, with respect to a unique absolute frame defined by matter and interaction, and Newtonian ‘relative description’ without a sensible immovable background, in nonexistent empty space with only notional coordinate markers.

## Interpretation and checks

Vacuum solutions and free gravitational degrees of freedom show why a broad Machian slogan is not equivalent to the standard equations. A precise alternative must state which of those solutions it excludes and why. The printed trace terms also require checking whether the symbol denotes the Einstein or Ricci tensor. Ask what observable changes when a boundary condition is replaced by a proposed cosmic requirement.
