> [!TLDR]
> Sagnac measured a rotation-dependent phase difference between oppositely directed light beams around a closed interferometer.

## Physical question and model

How does the Sagnac time difference arise in the slow-rotation limit? During propagation the detector moves around the loop, so co- and counter-propagating beams return at different events. In an inertial chart each vacuum segment still travels at light speed.

## Lecture account

In 1913, Sagnac published “L'éther lumineux démontré par l'effet du vent relatif d'éther dans un interféromètre en rotation uniforme” [18]: the luminiferous ether demonstrated by its relative-wind effect in a uniformly rotating interferometer. His idea resembled Michelson's, but instead of Earth's slow rotation he rotated the whole laboratory interferometer at about $2\,\unit{Hz}$. He intended to establish an ether as the absolute propagation medium, while Michelson sought Earth's rotation relative to it.

The clockwise and anticlockwise paths have equal lengths in the rotating apparatus frame. The source argues that isotropic propagation at equal relative speeds would give equal durations and phases. With an ether, the relative speeds would instead be $\mechanical{c}+\mechanical{v}$ and $\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{v}$, where $\mechanical{v}=\mechanical{R}\signal{\omega}$. For a circular path of radius $\mechanical{R}$,

$$
\delta T=\frac{2\pi\mechanical{R}}{\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{v}}-\frac{2\pi\mechanical{R}}{\mechanical{c}+\mechanical{v}}\approx\frac{4\pi\mechanical{R}\mechanical{v}}{\mechanical{c}^2}=\frac{4\pi\mechanical{R}^2\signal{\omega}}{\mechanical{c}^2}=\frac{4\mechanical{\mathbf A}\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{11.26}
$$

For a closed path of any shape and constant angular-velocity magnitude, the source gives

$$
\delta T=\frac{2}{\mechanical{c}^2}\oint\mechanical{\mathbf v}\cdot d\mechanical{\mathbf s}=\frac{2}{\mechanical{c}^2}\iint(\nabla\times\mechanical{\mathbf v})\cdot d\mechanical{\mathbf A}=\frac{4\mechanical{\mathbf A}\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\mechanical{c}^2}.
$$

The differential optical phase is $\delta\signal{\varphi}=2\pi\signal{\nu}\delta T$, where $\signal{\nu}$ is light frequency. With rotation frequency $2\,\unit{Hz}$ and enclosed area about $860\,\unit{cm^2}$, Sagnac could photograph the predicted interference-fringe shift (Fig. 11.7). A follow-up paper in the same year described further apparatus details [19].

Sagnac's conclusion, translated from the quoted French, was:

> The measurements show that, in the surrounding space, light propagates at a speed $\mechanical{V}_0$ independent of the overall motion of source O and the optical system. This property of space experimentally characterises the luminiferous ether.

The source describes Sagnac's unsuccessful efforts to persuade his Paris colleagues as acceptance of Einstein's theory grew. During Einstein's 1922 Paris visit, Sagnac raised his objections but was eventually ignored [20]. The author argues that Langevin's metric treatment then obscured Sagnac's interpretation [21]. He notes that the rotating interferometer remains widely used in physics, engineering, and navigation as an optical gyroscope.

The source compares the Sagnac expression (11.26) with the GNSS timing expression (11.25) [14]. It attributes the factor four rather than two to the two interferometer beams, compared with a single GNSS satellite-to-receiver signal. The author argues that calling the GNSS correction a Sagnac term implicitly acknowledges Galilean propagation while continuing to describe the system as based on STR.

## Interpretation and checks

The leading difference is proportional to enclosed oriented area and angular velocity, divided by light speed squared. It reverses sign with rotation and vanishes for zero projected area. Convert to a fringe phase with the actual optical frequency and path multiplicity. The use of one local detector removes a separated-clock ambiguity, but it does not establish a Galilean local velocity law.
