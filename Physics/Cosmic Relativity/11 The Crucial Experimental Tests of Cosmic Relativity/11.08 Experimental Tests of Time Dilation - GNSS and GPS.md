> [!TLDR]
> GNSS provides long-running atomic-clock comparisons that combine satellite motion, gravitational clock shifts, and signal-based position measurement.

## Physical question and model

Why are satellite clocks useful tests of relativistic timing? They provide repeated orbital histories and continuously compared signals. Clock performance, orbit determination, atmospheric propagation, and coordinate-time conventions must be separated.

## Lecture account

The Hafele–Keating clocks were just stable enough to reveal the effects despite drift and fluctuations. Averaging several identically constructed transported clocks helped. The author describes it as a one-time experiment not repeated in detail, despite its importance.

GNSS now supplies high-precision clocks for detailed time-dilation studies. The source lists GPS, Galileo, GLONASS, and Bidou. Satellite constellations carry stable commercial atomic clocks and antennas that regularly broadcast digitally coded position and time information to receivers. The source describes typical orbital periods as about half a day. Some countries also use more distant, nearly geostationary regional systems, including India's NavIC (IRNSS) and Japan's Michibiki (QNSS), as named in the source.

It gives satellite speed as about $3.6\,\unit{km/s}$ at geocentric distance about $26000\,\unit{km}$. Satellite clocks have both motional dilation and gravitational dilation from Earth's potential $\mechanical{\phi}_s$ at their height. Ground reference clocks are stationary in their laboratory but experience a larger-magnitude gravitational potential $\mechanical{\phi}_0$ at Earth's surface.

The source therefore describes the satellite-to-ground clock difference as the combination of GTR gravitational dilation and STR motional dilation [14]. The effects exceed those in Hafele–Keating, and years of continuous GNSS data permit repeated precision tests by anyone with data access.

## Interpretation and checks

A working engineering system tests a combined model repeatedly, but its algorithmic form alone does not determine a unique spacetime interpretation. Check whether a claimed anomaly is a rate shift, a periodic orbit term, a receiver-clock offset, or a propagation delay. Those effects can have different fitted parameters and signatures. A new theory should predict an unabsorbed difference across several orbits and receiver configurations.
