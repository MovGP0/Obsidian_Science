---
title: "The Absence of a Relative Time Dilation in Accelerated Frames"
---

> [!TLDR]
> Cosmic Relativity predicts no relative clock dilation from common acceleration because the induced cosmic potential is spatially homogeneous.

## Physical question and model

What would count as observing no accelerated-frame clock effect? Specify the separation, acceleration history, signal comparison, expected magnitude, and actual sensitivity. A theoretical estimate below the measurement floor cannot be falsified by a null readout.

## Lecture account

The source presents the absence of relative dilation between separated clocks in an accelerated frame as an EEP violation [1]. See [[10.11 The EEP and the Time Dilation of Clocks in an Accelerated Frame]].

The potential $\mechanical{\phi}$ and field $\mechanical{\mathbf g}=-\nabla\mechanical{\phi}$ near a massive source have different physical effects from the induced gravitomagnetic potential $\mechanical{A}_g$ and its time derivative $\mechanical{f}_g=d\mechanical{A}_g/dt$. Both fields are experienced as forces. The author argues that the induced field does not change the relative rates of spatially separated clocks, whereas the local gravitational field does cause gravitational time dilation.

For uniform local gravity, the usual relative shift is $\Delta T/T=\mechanical{g}\mechanical{d}/\mechanical{c}^2$. Different clock positions must have different potentials. In an accelerated frame, the cosmic potentials $\mechanical{\Phi}_u$ and $\mechanical{v}\mechanical{\Phi}_u/\mechanical{c}$ remain homogeneous at each instant despite varying with time. The author therefore predicts failure of EEP for relative clock dilation.

A rigorous experiment must initially synchronise and finally compare clocks in identical configurations. Clocks synchronised nearby and separated during acceleration must be brought back together, or compared with electromagnetic signals. The source argues that signal comparison must account for Galilean propagation. It states that a spatial uncertainty of $10\,\unit{mm}$ corresponds to about $33\,\unit{fs}$.

For acceleration $\mechanical{a}=1\,\unit{m/s^2}$ sustained for $1000\,\unit{s}$, a frame reaches $1\,\unit{km/s}$. With clock separation $\mechanical{d}=10\,\unit{m}$, EEP would predict only

$$
\Delta T=\frac{T\mechanical{a}\mechanical{d}}{\mechanical{c}^2}\approx10^{-13}\,\unit{s}.
$$

The source says that spatial uncertainty must be much less than $30\,\unit{nm}$. It contrasts the difficulty with a static Earth-field test, where clocks can remain $10\,\unit{m}$ apart for days, and states that the differential shift accumulates to a nanosecond in about a day.

> [!note]
> Source scale estimates are retained. A $10\,\unit{mm}$ light-path uncertainty corresponds to about $33\,\unit{ps}$, not $33\,\unit{fs}$. An interval of $10^{-13}\,\unit{s}$ corresponds to about $30\,\unit{\mu m}$, not $30\,\unit{nm}$. For clocks separated vertically by 10 m near Earth's surface, the stated gravitational formula gives roughly 0.1 ns per day rather than 1 ns per day.

The proposed conceptual experiment begins with high-precision atomic clocks C1 and C2 synchronised together inside an accelerable vehicle. They are then separated by $\mechanical{L}$ along the acceleration direction. The source suggests a $100\,\unit{m}$ train accelerating at $0.5\,\unit{m/s^2}$ for $200\,\unit{s}$ to reach $100\,\unit{m/s}$, or $360\,\unit{km/h}$.

If acceleration caused the same relative clock shift as gravity, then after $T$ seconds the difference would be $\Delta T=T\mechanical{a}\mechanical{L}/\mechanical{c}^2\approx10^{-13}\,\unit{s}$, with C1 slower. After acceleration, one would test whether C1 reads $T$ while C2 reads $T+T\mechanical{a}\mechanical{L}/\mechanical{c}^2$.

Suppose C2 sends its reading towards C1 opposite to the vehicle velocity. In the source's Galilean account, signal speed is $\mechanical{c}+\mechanical{v}$, giving an early arrival by $\delta T\approx\mechanical{L}\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}^2$. Since $\mechanical{v}=\mechanical{a}T$, the advance equals $\mechanical{L}\mechanical{a}T/\mechanical{c}^2$, matching the hypothetical EEP clock difference. Ignoring propagation would falsely assign this timing advance to C2 running faster. The author predicts one such signal-sized difference if EEP fails and twice that difference if it holds.

A similar issue arises if C2 is slowly transported towards C1 at speed $\mechanical{u}$ inside the now uniformly moving vehicle. Its absolute speed is $\mechanical{v}-\mechanical{u}$, rather than merely $\mechanical{u}$, giving

$$
\frac{\delta T}{T}\approx-\frac{(\mechanical{v}-\mechanical{u})^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}\approx-\frac{\mechanical{v}^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}+\frac{\mechanical{v}\mechanical{u}}{\mechanical{c}^2}.
$$

Compared with C1 moving at $\mechanical{v}$, the source writes the transport contribution as $\delta T\approx T\mechanical{v}\mechanical{u}/\mechanical{c}^2=-\mechanical{v}\mechanical{L}/\mechanical{c}^2$, with C1 slower. It then gives C2's reading as $T+\mechanical{v}\mechanical{L}/\mechanical{c}^2=T+T\mechanical{a}\mechanical{L}/\mechanical{c}^2$. The author argues that unrecognised motional dilation during the final comparison would again mimic the expected acceleration-induced gravitational shift.

> [!note]
> The source changes from a negative transport difference to a positive addition to C2's reading. The reported readings and signs are retained; comparing them requires a consistent choice of directed transport displacement and of which clock difference is being defined.

## Interpretation and checks

The weak-field or Rindler approximation predicts a separation-dependent rate difference for clocks stationary in an accelerating laboratory. It vanishes as separation tends to zero. The clock hypothesis does not give an extra universal point-clock ageing proportional simply to acceleration. Separate these two statements before declaring an equivalence violation. A proposed test must control gravitational gradients and acceleration-induced instrument shifts at the relevant scale.
