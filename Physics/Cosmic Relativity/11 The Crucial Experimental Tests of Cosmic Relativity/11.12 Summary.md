> [!TLDR]
> The chapter presents one-way propagation tests, transported clocks, and GNSS timing as evidence for the author's absolute cosmic frame.

## Physical question and model

Use this summary to separate three claims: a measured timing or phase, its propagation interpretation, and a proposed cosmic cause. The chapter's two main targets are light propagation and accumulated clock time.

## Lecture account

The chapter focuses on two predictions: Galilean light propagation and clock dilation determined by absolute velocity relative to cosmic matter. The author treats these as decisive contrasts with STR because they require an absolute dynamical frame.

One-way light propagation needs an appropriately designed test rather than a Michelson–Morley-style two-way comparison. The chapter reviews earlier claims of invariant one-way speed and argues that light propagation and separated-clock synchronisation are unavoidably entangled. The author states that this prevents those experiments from distinguishing Galilean from invariant propagation, despite repeated contrary interpretations.

His proposed experiment addresses two problems: looping the one-way path brings emission and reception together, and forming that loop from straight sections is intended to preserve inertial observer motion. A corresponding sound experiment validates the method in his account. He interprets the optical results as establishing relative speed $\mechanical{c}'=\mechanical{c}\pm\mechanical{v}$, where $\mechanical{v}$ is reference-frame velocity.

The chapter combines the sound and light results into a pulse-coincidence argument against invariant relative light speed. It uses emission and reception coincidences shared by stationary and moving observers. The author argues that treating light speed as invariant then conflicts with the principle of relativity.

For transported atomic clocks, he revisits the 1971 Hafele–Keating westward clock gain. The source treats this as contradicting STR and agreeing with Cosmic Relativity's absolute-velocity predictions.

GNSS atomic clocks provide continuous precision tests of both dilation and propagation. The author contrasts his STR clock formula with the Cosmic Relativity formula and states that operational GNSS uses the latter, corresponding to an absolute frame.

He also argues that GNSS ranging differs from an invariant-speed interpretation. STR gives $\mechanical{d}=\mechanical{c}\Delta t$, while Cosmic Relativity gives

$$
\mechanical{d}=|\mechanical{\mathbf c}-\mechanical{\mathbf v}_o|\Delta t.
$$

Receiver velocity $\mechanical{\mathbf v}_o$ adds a range correction involving $\mechanical{\mathbf v}_o\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf d}/\mechanical{c}$. The source states that GNSS algorithms use this correction. The author presents the clock data and ranging procedure as strong support for Cosmic Relativity and argues that GPS technical documentation allows the comparison to be checked by nonspecialists.

The chapter also predicts an EEP violation for separated clocks accelerated together at $\mechanical{a}$ for duration $T$. The source argues that clock transport or signal comparison necessarily introduces an apparent difference only during the final comparison, while the accelerating phase itself produces no differential dilation. It gives the comparison effect as $\mechanical{v}\mechanical{L}/\mechanical{c}^2$.

In this summary, the source prints the hypothetical gravitational contribution as $\mechanical{a}T/\mechanical{c}^2$. With $\mechanical{v}=\mechanical{a}T$, it states that EEP would give $\Delta T=2\mechanical{a}T/\mechanical{c}^2$, while Cosmic Relativity gives half this, $\Delta T=\mechanical{a}T/\mechanical{c}^2$.

> [!note]
> These three summary expressions omit the clock separation $\mechanical{L}$ needed for time units. The detailed argument in section 11.9 uses $\mechanical{a}T\mechanical{L}/\mechanical{c}^2$. The summary's printed forms are retained here.

The historical review covers Lodge, Michelson, and Sagnac. For rotation at $\signal{\omega}$, observer speed is $\mechanical{v}=\mechanical{r}\signal{\omega}$ and the proposed directional light speeds are $\mechanical{c}'=\mechanical{c}\pm\mechanical{r}\signal{\omega}$. Sagnac interpreted his rotating optical result as an ether-based absolute frame. A decade later, Michelson and Gale used a large evacuated interferometer and reported Earth's rotation relative to a stationary ether. The author argues that these results support any absolute propagation frame, without uniquely requiring an electromagnetic ether.

The chapter then derives Langevin's rotating metric from Galilean transformations and presents it as contrary to invariant light speed. Its concluding historical comparison describes Cosmic Relativity as restoring a universal gravitational dynamical basis after what the author regards as STR's kinematic retreat, made possible by earlier limited knowledge of the Universe and its gravitational influence.

## Interpretation and checks

For a final check, explain why a translating closed loop cancels a uniform first-order directional contribution while a rotating loop can retain one. Then describe a clock comparison using complete worldlines rather than an isolated relative-speed rule. A decisive test requires alternatives that disagree after these standard geometric effects and the instrument uncertainty are included.
