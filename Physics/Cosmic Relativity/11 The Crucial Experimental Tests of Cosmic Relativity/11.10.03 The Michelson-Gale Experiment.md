> [!TLDR]
> Michelson and Gale built a large evacuated interferometer to test Earth's rotational motion through differential light propagation around a loop.

## Physical question and model

What does a terrestrial large-area interferometer measure about Earth rotation? Its projected loop area and Earth's angular velocity determine the leading Sagnac phase under the stated propagation model. Latitude and orientation set the projection.

## Lecture account

The source states that Michelson did not accept invariant light speed in empty space without a propagation medium. By 1925, he obtained support and funding for an ambitious test of Earth's rotation relative to a stationary ether.

The apparatus had a total optical path of $2\,\unit{km}$, shown in Fig. 11.7 in [[11.10.02 The Experiments by G. Sagnac]]. Evacuated metal water-supply pipes of $1\,\unit{ft}$ diameter enclosed the light path to reduce optical-phase fluctuations from residual-air density changes.

Michelson explained the motivation and theory in the *Astrophysical Journal* paper “The effect of the Earth's rotation on the velocity of light” [22]. The following passage gives its abstract.

> Theory of the effect of the rotation of the earth on the velocity of light as derived on the hypothesis of a fixed ether.
>
> Historical Remarks.—The theory was given originally in 1904. The experiment was undertaken at the urgent instance of Dr. L. Silberstein. A preliminary experiment at Mount Wilson in 1923 showed that it was necessary to resort to an exhausted pipeline.

Besides evacuating the long pipes, the important innovation was a small auxiliary closed-path interferometer whose sought rotation effect was negligible. It provided a fringe-shift reference.

Michelson and H. G. Gale described the experiment in a second paper [24]. A carbon arc supplied the light to the $613\,\unit{m}$ by $339\,\unit{m}$ rectangle. Equal beam splitting produced opposite-direction paths that recombined into fringes. A much smaller rectangle inside the large loop supplied the fiducial reference. At about $42^\circ$ north latitude, Earth's rotation rate $2\pi/86400\,\unit{rad/s}$ gave an expected shift of $0.236\pm0.002$ fringe. The abstract stated:

> The two beams returning to the original mirror produced interference fringes. The beam traversing the rectangle in a counter-clockwise direction was retarded. The observed displacement of the fringes was found to be $0.230\pm0.005$, agreeing with the computed value $0.236\pm0.002$ within the limits of experimental error.

The source describes the 1925 result as detection of Earth's rotation effect on light under a fixed-ether hypothesis. Michelson later wrote in *Studies in Optics* (1927) [23]:

> The existence of an ether appears to be inconsistent with the theory; a fixed ether would imply the possibility of a measurement of ‘absolute’ motion. But without a medium how can the propagation of light-waves be explained? ...
>
> It is to be hoped that the theory may be reconciled with the existence of a medium, either by modifying the theory, or, more probably, by attributing the requisite properties to the ether; for example, allowing changes in its properties (dielectric constant, for instance) due to the presence of a gravitational field.

The author highlights Michelson's gravitational speculation. He argues that cosmic gravity supplies a different physical explanation: the matter-filled Universe defines the propagation frame, without changing hypothetical ether or empty-space properties.

## Interpretation and checks

A result matching that area dependence is evidence for rotation-sensitive propagation geometry, not by itself for an electromagnetic ether. Compare the same result with the standard rotating-coordinate metric. Environmental path changes and imperfect cancellation in a reference loop limit the inference. Predict how reversing the loop orientation changes the phase while leaving an orientation-independent delay unchanged.
