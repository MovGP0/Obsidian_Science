> [!TLDR]
> The author compares transported atomic-clock data with an absolute-frame cross term that predicts different eastward and westward clock changes.

## Physical question and model

What would an atomic-clock transport experiment need to distinguish? The standard prediction is the difference of two proper-time integrals, including gravity if relevant; it is not generally a function only of instantaneous speed relative to a chosen moving reference clock.

## Lecture account

The comparison turns on one criterion: is velocity $\mechanical{v}(t)$ measured relative to a unique absolute cosmic frame, or is it only the kinematic velocity $\mechanical{v}_r(t)$ relative to a chosen reference clock? The source assigns the first to Cosmic Relativity and the second to STR. In its STR comparison, motion $\mechanical{u}(t)$ of the reference laboratory relative to the Universe does not enter.

Differences are most apparent for speeds much smaller than $\mechanical{c}$. [[09.06 The Predictions for Motional Time Dilation]], equation (9.7), gives the same absolute dilation as (11.13), now interpreted as gravity from the velocity-dependent cosmic potential.

In the [[09.08 The Hafele-Keating Experiment|Hafele–Keating experiment]] [13], caesium clocks travelled around Earth in commercial aircraft and were compared with stationary laboratory clocks. The source's STR comparison depends on relative speed magnitude alone, not direction. For finite segments at a common speed relative to clocks at rest with the laboratory, it prints

$$
\Delta T_{\mathrm{STR}}=\sum_i\delta t_i=-\sum_i\frac{t_i\mechanical{v}_i^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}=\frac{\mechanical{L}\mechanical{v}}{2\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{11.16}
$$

Here $T=\sum_i t_i$ is total trip duration, beginning and ending at the reference laboratory. The distance $\sum_i t_i\mechanical{v}_i$ is the trip circumference $\mechanical{L}$.

The source contrasts Cosmic Relativity with

$$
\Delta T_{\mathrm{CR}}=\sum_i\delta t_i=-\sum_i\frac{t_i(\mechanical{\mathbf v}_i+\mechanical{\mathbf u}_i)^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}-\frac{T\mechanical{u}^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}=-\frac{\mechanical{L}\mechanical{v}}{2\mechanical{c}^2}-\sum_i\frac{\mechanical{\mathbf l}_i\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf u}_i}{\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{11.17}
$$

> [!note]
> Source sign inconsistencies are retained. The final term in (11.16) lacks the minus sign in its preceding sum. In (11.17), cancelling the common laboratory dilation requires adding $T\mechanical{u}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)$ rather than subtracting it, as also noted for (11.13).

For two-way transport, the source states that the final term vanishes because the directed segment lengths have both signs. For one-way transport around Earth it does not vanish. Solar-system cosmic velocity and Earth's orbital velocity average out in the vector products over the complete path. The laboratory's rotational contribution remains and accumulates.

The total is given as

$$
\Delta T_{\mathrm{CR}}=\sum_i\delta t_i=-\frac{\mechanical{L}\mechanical{v}}{2\mechanical{c}^2}-\frac{\mechanical{L}\mechanical{u}}{\mechanical{c}^2}\approx-\frac{\mechanical{L}\mechanical{v}}{2\mechanical{c}^2}\mp\mechanical{R}_e\signal{\omega}_e\frac{\mechanical{L}\cos\lambda}{\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{11.18}
$$

Here $\mechanical{R}_e$ is Earth's radius, $\signal{\omega}_e$ its rotation rate, and $\lambda$ the trip latitude. The circumference is $\mechanical{L}=2\pi\mechanical{R}_e\cos\lambda$.

The author identifies the last term as evidence for an absolute frame. At the equator and aircraft speeds below $250\,\unit{m/s}$, the first term has magnitude about $55\,\unit{ns}$. The proposed absolute term can reach $\mp207\,\unit{ns}$, independent of aircraft relative speed. Eastward and westward trips therefore have strongly asymmetric motional dilation.

For the Hafele–Keating aircraft speed $230\,\unit{m/s}$, the source gives the first term's magnitude as about $42\,\unit{ns}$. At average latitude $\lambda\approx33^\circ$, the absolute term is $\mp146\,\unit{ns}$. The eastward loss is then about $188\,\unit{ns}$ instead of $42\,\unit{ns}$ in the source's STR comparison.

For westward transport, the author predicts that the clocks return older than the stationary laboratory clocks: $\Delta T_{\mathrm{CR}}\approx+104\,\unit{ns}$ rather than $-42\,\unit{ns}$. He calls this a relative time contraction, or faster clock rate, and states that it agrees with the experiment's results in Table 9.2.

> [!note]
> The source treats a transported clock gaining time relative to a stationary laboratory clock as evidence for an absolute frame and as contradicting its stated STR premise that relative motion always causes relative clock loss.

The author concludes that the 1971 Hafele–Keating experiment had already contradicted the premise that only transported-clock velocity relative to the reference clocks determines motional dilation. He argues that the lack of a physical explanation then prevented recognition of the significance of the faster westward clocks. Comparing this with Ruderfer's one-way-light erratum in [[11.01.01 Past Experiments on the One-Way Propagation of Light]], he suggests that established theories can bias the interpretation of experiments.

## Interpretation and checks

On Earth, a rotating ground reference and transported clock have different velocities in an approximately inertial chart. Their difference produces direction-dependent terms within standard relativity. Check the reference subtraction, endpoint settings, and the sign of every predicted interval. The printed sums in (11.16)–(11.17) have sign inconsistencies already marked in the lecture notes. An exclusion claim needs a residual after those issues and clock drift are resolved.
