> [!TLDR]
> The source introduces historical rotating-frame light experiments, from early ether proposals to the Sagnac and Michelson–Gale measurements.

## Physical question and model

Why is a rotating light path different from a uniformly translating one? Rotation gives a position-dependent velocity field with nonzero circulation. A closed-loop comparison can then detect rotation without referring to a global distant-clock convention.

## Lecture account

Failure to detect an electromagnetic ether, including in Michelson–Morley, did not immediately remove ether from electrodynamics. The source gives two reasons: the belief that transverse waves require a medium, and the success of FitzGerald–Lorentz contraction in retaining Galilean ether propagation while accounting for null results. Since contraction became part of LT, support for an ether-like absolute frame persisted.

Between 1894 and 1925, even after STR gained acceptance, experiments sought a stationary ether through light propagation in rotating frames. These reference frames have noninertial motion. The following sections review proposals by O. Lodge and A. A. Michelson, then experiments by G. Sagnac and Michelson with H. G. Gale.

## Interpretation and checks

A rotating observer family is not one global inertial frame. Standard relativity permits different counter-propagating return times around it while preserving the local vacuum light speed. Identify the area vector, angular velocity, and which clock records the returns. The small-rotation formula neglects higher powers of rim speed divided by light speed. This distinction is essential before comparing the historical ether interpretations.
