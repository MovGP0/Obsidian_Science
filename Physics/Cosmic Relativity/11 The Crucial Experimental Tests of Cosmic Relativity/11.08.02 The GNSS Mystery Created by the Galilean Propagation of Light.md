> [!TLDR]
> The author interprets receiver-motion corrections in GNSS signal timing as evidence for Galilean light propagation in an absolute frame.

## Physical question and model

Why does receiver motion appear in a ranging equation? The signal must intersect the moving receiver. Expressing that geometry in rotating Earth coordinates gives an area-dependent correction while local propagation remains relativistic.

## Lecture account

The source regards its claimed GNSS motional-dilation discrepancy as fundamental but small in everyday navigation. In (11.21), the observer-speed-squared term is negligible, while $\mechanical{\mathbf v}_s\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf v}_o/\mechanical{c}^2$ is only about $\pm0.001\,\unit{ns/s}$. That gives $3\,\unit{mm}$ error in $10\,\unit{s}$. The author nevertheless treats it as significant because the mean clock accuracy is better than $0.001\,\unit{ns/s}$. He considers the implications of signal propagation for navigation algorithms more serious.

The GNSS localisation strategy is described as using invariant relative light velocity. A moving satellite emits at position $\mechanical{\mathbf x}(t_e)$ and time $t_e$. Source motion does not change light speed. The receiver records reception time $t_r$, giving $\Delta\tau=t_r-t_e$. With STR's light postulate, distance from the satellite's emission location is $\mechanical{d}_{sr}=\mechanical{c}\Delta\tau$.

Ranging to at least four satellites determines a three-dimensional receiver position. The fourth measurement also permits solution for an inaccurate receiver clock, which is usually not an atomic standard. The source describes the invariant-speed relation $\mechanical{d}=\mechanical{c}\Delta\tau$ as the crucial assumption that would remove concern about source or receiver motion.

Cosmic Relativity instead makes the range depend on receiver motion. For observer velocity $\mechanical{\mathbf v}_o$, it gives $\mechanical{\mathbf c}'=\mechanical{\mathbf c}-\mechanical{\mathbf v}_o$, and range $\mechanical{c}'\Delta\tau$. The source gives a satellite altitude of $20000\,\unit{km}$ and typical travel time $\Delta\tau=67\,\unit{ms}$. At receiver speed $\mechanical{v}_o\approx300\,\unit{m/s}$, it claims a $40\,\unit{m}$ difference from invariant-speed ranging. It states that the error can exceed a hundred metres for high supersonic receiver speeds.

> [!note]
> For the stated one-way interval and receiver speed, the first-order correction $\mechanical{v}_o\Delta\tau$ is approximately $20\,\unit{m}$. The source's $40\,\unit{m}$ value is retained, but the supplied figures alone do not produce it.

The source repeats (11.15) for GNSS range:

$$
\mechanical{d}_{sr}=\mechanical{c}(t_r-t_s)\left(1+\frac{\mechanical{v}^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}-\frac{\mechanical{\mathbf c}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf v}}{\mechanical{c}^2}\right)\approx\mechanical{c}(t_r-t_s)-\frac{\mechanical{\mathbf d}_{sr}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf v}}{\mechanical{c}}. \tag{11.23}
$$

To explain the final term, the author considers receiver velocity $\mechanical{v}_o$ directed towards the satellite. The invariant-speed reference duration is $\Delta\tau\approx\mechanical{d}/\mechanical{c}$. In the receiver frame, he takes relative signal speed as $\mechanical{c}'=\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{v}$. Range changes from $\mechanical{c}\Delta\tau$ to $(\mechanical{c}-\mechanical{v})\Delta\tau\approx\mechanical{d}_{sr}(1-\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c})$. The correction $-\mechanical{d}_{sr}\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$ becomes the vector-product correction in the general case.

Because GNSS algorithms use timing quantities, the source rewrites this as

$$
t_r=t_s+\frac{\mechanical{d}_{sr}}{\mechanical{c}}+\frac{\mechanical{\mathbf d}_{sr}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf v}}{\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{11.24}
$$

The author states that all GNSS algorithms use this expression and interprets it as a Cosmic Relativity or absolute-frame result rather than an STR result. He presents this as strong evidence against STR.

> [!note]
> The source's boxed conclusion describes GNSS ranging with $\mechanical{d}=(\mechanical{\mathbf c}-\mechanical{\mathbf v})\Delta t=\mechanical{d}-\mechanical{\mathbf d}\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf v}/\mechanical{c}$, rather than $\mechanical{d}=\mechanical{c}\Delta t$. Its notation uses the same distance symbol for the corrected and uncorrected values. The author concludes that GPS refutes STR and supports Cosmic Relativity.

GNSS literature calls the last term in (11.24) the *Sagnac term*, which the author attributes to its resemblance to a rotating-interferometer expression [14]. He argues that the derivation is not specific to rotating receivers, although it also applies to a receiver fixed on rotating Earth (Fig. 11.6). He interprets the term as evidence for Galilean GNSS signal propagation relative to any moving receiver.

For a receiver fixed on Earth's rotating surface, the source gives the absolute timing term as $\delta\tau=-\mechanical{\mathbf d}_{sr}\cdot(\mechanical{\mathbf R}\times\boldsymbol{\omega})/\mechanical{c}^2$. It states that GPS literature rewrites it as

$$
\delta\tau=-\frac{1}{\mechanical{c}^2}(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mechanical{\mathbf d}_{sr})\cdot\mechanical{\mathbf R}=\frac{1}{2\mechanical{c}^2}(\mechanical{\mathbf R}\times\mechanical{\mathbf d}_{sr})\cdot2\boldsymbol{\omega}=\frac{2\mechanical{\mathbf A}\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega}}{\mechanical{c}^2}. \tag{11.25}
$$

Here $\mechanical{\mathbf A}$ is the oriented area of the triangle defined by $\mechanical{\mathbf R}$ and $\mechanical{\mathbf d}_{sr}$. The source points to a fuller explanation in *Gravity's Time* [1]. The resulting area expression resembles the differential duration in a Sagnac interferometer, hence the name. The author argues that calling it a Sagnac correction hides its general receiver-motion origin. He describes Sagnac's original rotating experiment as a test intended to reveal an absolute propagation frame.

The source claims a general linear velocity can be written $\mechanical{\mathbf v}=\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mechanical{\mathbf r}$, with $\omega=\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{r}$. It rewrites the corresponding Galilean term schematically as $-\mechanical{\mathbf d}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mechanical{\mathbf r})=(\mechanical{\mathbf d}\times\mechanical{\mathbf r})\cdot\boldsymbol{\omega}$ and names a fictitious area $\mechanical{\mathbf A}=\mechanical{\mathbf d}\times\mechanical{\mathbf r}$. He argues that the same correction is required for a linearly moving projectile in air or space, and presents routine GNSS operation as confirming it.

> [!note]
> The triangle area used in (11.25) is half a cross product, whereas the later schematic “fictitious area” is the full cross product. These two source area conventions are retained.

## Interpretation and checks

The first-order area term is the standard Sagnac geometry of the coordinate system. It is not unique evidence for a changed local speed of light. Distinguish open-path coordinate delay from the full counter-propagating closed-loop time difference, which has a different factor. State the positions and area orientation before comparing coefficients. A timing residual must also be separated from clock and orbit errors.
