> [!TLDR]
> The author examines Faraday's unipolar induction experiments as a test of physical rotation and the cosmic gravitational frame.

## Physical question and model

What produces the measured voltage in a unipolar device? Specify conductor motion, the complete circuit, field geometry, and contacts. Electric and magnetic forces in a given frame together determine charge redistribution.

## Lecture account

Different relativity theories explain effects such as Fresnel drag with different physical accounts while agreeing with the experiments. The author argues that STR does not identify the physical causes, and that some electrodynamic problems have lacked a satisfactory explanation for nearly two centuries. He presents Cosmic Relativity as a solution, particularly for induction during relative rotation of a conductor and magnet.

The source contrasts simple linear-motion cases with the more general Faraday observations, discussed in [[03.09 Experiments on the Unipolar Induction]]. In 1831, Faraday studied currents induced in conductors moving through uniform magnetic fields [19]:

> The experiments described combine to prove that when a piece of metal (and the same may be true of all conducting matter) is passed either before a single pole, or between the opposite poles of a magnet, or near electro-magnetic poles, whether ferruginous or not, electrical currents are produced across the metal transverse to the direction of motion.

This electrodynamic action became known as the Lorentz force, $\mechanical{F}=\electric{q}\mechanical{v}\times\magnetic{\mathbf B}$. Faraday was surprised to obtain the same result when the magnet rotated. Whether the magnet was stationary or rotating at an arbitrary rate and direction made no difference. The source next quotes his Bakerian lectures [20].

> ... a copper disc was cemented upon the end of a cylinder magnet, with paper intervening; the magnet and disc were rotated together, and collectors (attached to the galvanometer) brought in contact with the circumference and the central part of the copper plate. The galvanometer needle moved as in former cases, and the direction of motion was the same as that which would have resulted, if the copper only had revolved, and the magnet been fixed. Neither was there any apparent difference in the quantity of deflection. Hence, rotating the magnet causes no difference in the results; for a rotatory and a stationary magnet produce the same effect upon the moving copper.

The same current was induced at any relative velocity between conductor and magnet, including zero. Faraday's next experiment, using only a magnet, confirmed this:

> That the metal of the magnet itself might be substituted for the moving cylinder, disc, or wire, seemed an inevitable consequence, and yet one which would exhibit the effects of magneto-electric induction in a striking form. ...The magnet was then revolved by a piece of string passed round it, and the galvanometer-needle immediately indicated a powerful current of electricity... Thus a singular independence of the magnetism and the bar in which it resides is rendered evident.

The author identifies the relevant quantity as the conductor's absolute local velocity, equal to the velocity of its charges. Faraday's magnetic-line picture made the difference between rotational and linear cases a paradox. His proposed explanation in 1851 [21] was:

> When lines of force are spoken of as crossing a conducting circuit, it must be considered as effected by the translation of a magnet. No mere rotation of a bar magnet on its axis, produces any induction effect on circuits exterior to it...The system of power about the magnet must not be considered as revolving with the magnet, any more than the rays of light which emanate from the sun are supposed to revolve with the sun.

The author argues that Einstein, unaware of induction without relative motion, treated only charge–magnet relative motion as relevant. He disputes the identification of $\mechanical{v}$ in induction and the Lorentz force with relative velocity, and claims the full evidence instead establishes the absolute velocity of charges in the conductor.

Another group of experiments from 1910–1920 helps separate the contributions to unipolar induction. A rotating metal magnet with an external stationary circuit also involves conducting wires, a galvanometer, and other components. All belong to the electrodynamic system under study. A more direct test measures the potential difference between two radial locations without an external circuit during rotation.

The author quotes the opening of S. J. Barnett's PNAS paper [22], communicated by R. A. Millikan:

> If a cylindrical condenser is placed in a uniform magnetic field with lines of induction parallel to its axis, and is short-circuited and rotated, together with the short-circuiting wire, about this axis, it becomes charged to a potential difference equal to the motional electromotive force in the wire, or the rate at which the wire cuts magnetic flux. If, however, the condenser and wire are fixed and the agent producing the magnetic field rotates, the relative motion being the same as before, the condenser does not become charged, as was proved by precise experiment in 1912. This is the first case in electromagnetic induction in which the observed effect does not depend entirely on the relativity of the motion.

Barnett's 1912 paper [23] studied induction without an external current-sensing circuit during rotation. Two coaxial cylindrical capacitor plates were shorted by a wire. A multi-turn coil or electromagnet supplied a uniform magnetic field and could rotate relative to the stationary capacitor. If relative motion alone caused induction, magnet rotation should charge the capacitor through the shorting wire. Barnett broke the plate connection while the magnet rotated, so that any capacitor charge would remain. The source quotes his description:

> A cylindrical condenser is placed in an approximately uniform magnetic field parallel to its axis, and the magnets producing the field are rotated while the condenser is short-circuited by a wire fixed in space. While the magnets are still in rotation, the connection between the armatures of the condenser is broken; and the condenser is tested for charge after the field is annulled, or the rotation stopped, or the condenser removed.

The result agreed with the deduction from Faraday's experiment. Barnett concluded:

> The investigation leads conclusively to the result that the condenser system, when it remains at rest and the system producing the field rotates, receives not more than a minute fraction of the charge it would receive for the same relative motion if the system producing the field remained at rest.

E. H. Kennard made similar investigations during the same decade, with similar results. In 1917 he concluded [24]:

> ...electromagnetic induction depends in part upon absolute rotation in the mechanical sense. Analysis in terms of electrons seems to make necessary the existence of a stationary aether in order to explain the observed effect; so that the phenomenon seems to present difficulties for those relativists who reject the aether.

The author now proposes a complete solution, identifying absolute conductor and magnet motion as crucial to locating the electromotive force. He states that this also gives new predictions.

The source states that the two forces have equal magnitudes for linear motion.]]

For panel b, the moving charge gives current $\electric{i}_1=\electric{q}\mechanical{v}$, which interacts with the magnet's equivalent internal current $\electric{I}_m$. The source gives $\mechanical{F}\propto\electric{i}_1\electric{I}_m=\electric{q}\mechanical{v}\magnetic{B}$. For panel c, it gives $\mechanical{F}\propto\electric{q}(\electric{I}_m d\mechanical{v}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2))=\electric{q}\electric{E}=\electric{q}\mechanical{v}\magnetic{B}$.

> [!note]
> The source's caption uses current–force proportionalities without specifying their geometric factors, and does not define the symbol $d$ in the panel-c expression. These expressions are retained as the source's schematic relations.

The author stresses that the physical mechanisms differ when conductor motion is exchanged for magnet motion. He argues that reducing them to one relative-motion phenomenon prevents STR from explaining general unipolar induction. The first mechanism is the familiar interaction between currents in Ampère's experiments and theory. A current element $d\electric{i}=\electric{i}_1\,d\mechanical{l}=\electric{\lambda}\mechanical{v}_i\,d\mechanical{l}$ interacts with another current $\electric{i}_2=\electric{q}\mechanical{v}$:

$$
\mechanical{F}=-\frac{\magnetic{\mu}_0}{2\pi}\int\frac{\electric{i}_1\,d\mechanical{l}\cdot\electric{i}_2}{\mechanical{r}}=-\frac{\magnetic{\mu}_0}{2\pi}\int\frac{\electric{\lambda}\mechanical{v}_i\electric{q}\,d\mechanical{l}\cdot\mechanical{v}}{\mechanical{r}}=-\electric{q}\mechanical{v}\times\magnetic{\mathbf B}. \tag{10.40}
$$

Here $\electric{\lambda}$ is charge density. Any magnet is effectively a closed current (Fig. 10.8a), $\electric{i}_m=\electric{\lambda}\mechanical{v}_i$. The source interprets (10.40) as a magnetic field $\magnetic{B}=\magnetic{\mu}_0\electric{i}_1/(2\pi\mechanical{r})$ acting on another current or moving charge.

When a metal conductor moves at $\mechanical{v}_c$, its charges move with it and form the second current. Positive ions and electrons both move, so the net current is zero, but only electrons respond freely to forces along the conductor. Above a magnet pole, the conductor's current $\electric{\lambda}\mechanical{v}_c$ interacts with the magnet's opposite boundary currents (Fig. 10.8b). Parallel currents attract and opposite currents repel, giving a total force on a moving charge.

The source writes this force as

$$
\mechanical{F}=\frac{\magnetic{\mu}_0}{2\pi}\frac{\electric{q}\electric{i}_m\mechanical{\mathbf v}\cdot d\mechanical{\mathbf l}}{\mechanical{r}}=-\electric{q}\mechanical{v}\magnetic{B}. \tag{10.41}
$$

It drives the conductor's charges. Taking the magnet current as the source of the field gives the usual Lorentz expression $\mechanical{F}=\electric{q}\mechanical{v}\times\magnetic{\mathbf B}$. The author identifies Ampère's symmetric, bilinear current–current interaction as the force on free charges in the moving conductor. No second-order relativistic effect in $\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2$ is required.

When the magnet moves linearly but the conductor is stationary, only the magnet has a closed current. The conductor has no motion-induced current, so the author excludes the previous current–current interaction. Instead, real magnet motion produces second-order length contraction of the material and its charge densities. The source writes

$$
\mechanical{L}\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}-\mechanical{L}\approx-\frac{\mechanical{L}\mechanical{v}^2}{\mechanical{c}^2}.
$$

Though the contraction is small, its proposed effects are large. The important change is unequal contraction of the circulating current densities at opposite magnet boundaries. Positive-ion density increases uniformly by the factor $\mechanical{v}^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)$. For already moving negative charges, the change is anisotropic: the effective current density along the magnet's motion increases with factor $(\mechanical{v}+\mechanical{v}_i)^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)$, while that at the opposite edge decreases with factor $(\mechanical{v}-\mechanical{v}_i)^2/(2\mechanical{c}^2)$. Here $\mechanical{v}_i$ is the average velocity of charges producing the internal currents.

This is a second-order effect, unlike the bilinear first-order Ampère interaction. The source gives the differential charge-density change as $\pm\electric{\lambda}\mechanical{v}\mechanical{v}_i/\mechanical{c}^2$. Opposite excess charges make the magnet resemble a charged capacitor (Fig. 10.8c). The source gives the resulting electric field as $\electric{E}_m=\electric{\lambda}\mechanical{v}\mechanical{v}_i/(\mechanical{r}\mechanical{c}^2)$. The Coulomb force on a charge, perpendicular to magnet motion, is written

$$
\mechanical{F}=\frac{2\electric{\lambda}_1\electric{q}_c\mechanical{v}_1\mechanical{v}_m}{4\pi\electric{\varepsilon}_0\mechanical{c}^2\mechanical{r}}=\frac{\magnetic{\mu}_0}{2\pi}\frac{\electric{\lambda}_1\electric{q}_c\mechanical{v}_1\mechanical{v}_c}{\mechanical{r}}=\electric{q}\mechanical{v}\magnetic{B}. \tag{10.42}
$$

The author states that this equals the current–current magnetic force in magnitude for linear unipolar induction, although its physical origin differs. If magnet and conductor move linearly together, the two contributions exactly cancel. He again stresses the distinction between second-order length contraction and bilinear Ampère interaction.

> [!note]
> Several source inconsistencies are retained. The leading expansion of the contraction expression contains a factor $1/2$, absent from the displayed approximation. The source calls the two bipolar charged edges “two positively charged edges”; the stated bipolar interpretation instead requires opposite signs. Its electric-field expression omits the electrostatic prefactor needed in SI units. Equation (10.42) changes the velocity subscript from $m$ to $c$ without explanation.

Magnet motion also changes internal-current velocities through ordinary Galilean addition. The author states that this leaves the average magnetic field unchanged, consistent with the principle of relativity. He claims that separating conductor-motion current interaction from magnet-motion contraction completely resolves general unipolar induction.

Now consider a stationary disc magnet and a conductor moving azimuthally at angular velocity $\signal{\omega}$. The author treats this as the same physical situation as a conductor's linear motion. A moving charge supplies $\electric{q}\mechanical{v}(\mechanical{r})=\electric{q}\mechanical{r}\signal{\omega}$ and interacts directly with the magnet current $\electric{i}_m$. The resulting Ampère or Lorentz force is associated with Fig. 10.9a. The source gives $\electric{q}_c\magnetic{B}\int\mechanical{v}_c(\mechanical{r})\,d\mechanical{r}$ for the EMF and $\electric{q}_c\magnetic{B}\signal{\omega}\mechanical{r}$ for force at a radius.

> [!note]
> With $\electric{q}_c$ a charge, the source's “EMF” expression has units of energy. Dividing it by the charge gives a potential difference.

![[cosmic-relativity-10-09-rotating-unipolar-induction.svg|**Fig. 10.9** The author's proposed solution for rotational unipolar induction, recreated schematically. a: a rotating conductor's charge current interacts with the stationary magnet's internal current and produces an EMF. b: magnet rotation alone changes internal charge densities through contraction; the excess is uniform around the cylindrical boundary, so the interior potential is uniform and there is no EMF. c: common rotation combines the mechanisms, with the boundary excess charge sign reversed as shown in the source. The boundary charge produces no interior electric force, so the current-interaction EMF remains even without relative rotation.]]

The puzzle is a magnet rotating at angular frequency $\signal{\omega}$ below a stationary conductor (Fig. 10.9b). As in the linear moving-magnet case, the author excludes a conductor–magnet current interaction. Locally, the same charge–magnet relative motion exists, but no force acts on a free stationary charge.

The source attributes this difference to cylindrical geometry. Azimuthal contraction, with fractional factor

$$
\frac{\mechanical{v}^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2}=\frac{\mechanical{r}^2\signal{\omega}^2}{2\mechanical{c}^2},
$$

changes the magnet's internal-current charge density. Unlike the linear case, a uniform charge excess at the radial boundary produces a potential $\electric{\phi}_e$ that is uniform inside that boundary. Its gradient $\nabla\electric{\phi}_e$ vanishes, so the conductor's charges feel no electric force. The author uses this to explain the absence of induction despite relative motion.

For magnet and conductor rotating together, with no relative motion, both the conductor-motion current force and magnet-rotation contraction are present. But the cylindrically uniform excess charge does not create an interior EMF (Fig. 10.9c). *The current–current force therefore has no equal and opposite electric force to cancel it*, unlike the author's linear common-motion case. It continues to drive conductor charges.

The source concludes that absolute charge and magnet motion, rather than their relative motion, determines induction. It identifies $\mechanical{v}$ in Maxwell field transformations and the Lorentz force with velocity relative to the unique cosmic preferred frame, and claims the experimental evidence establishes this. It then returns to the common physical mechanisms in linear and circular motion: Ampère forces from magnet and charge currents, and contraction effects from magnet motion.

The author concludes that both mechanisms occur in linear and circular situations but have different manifestations. He describes their linear-motion balance as preserving the principle of relativity, and states that cancellation continues even when the common linear motion accelerates.

![[cosmic-relativity-10-10-annular-magnet.svg|**Fig. 10.10** Basis of the author's new annular-magnet prediction, recreated schematically. a: effective currents circulate in opposite senses at the inner and outer boundaries. b: rotation-induced contraction is proposed to create azimuthally symmetric charge excesses of opposite sign. Each cylindrical boundary has no electric field in its interior but has a nonzero field outside it. The inner boundary can therefore produce a field in the magnet's annular region.]]

For an annular magnet, rotation contracts currents at both boundaries. Because their directions differ, the excess charges have opposite polarity, like those in the linear moving-magnet case. The annulus can be viewed as a linear magnet bent around. The potential of a cylindrically symmetric charge distribution is constant inside its boundary, but an electric field exists outside. The source writes $\nabla\cdot\electric{\mathbf E}=\electric{\rho}$ and predicts a nonzero EMF in the annular part of a rotating disc magnet with a central hole.

For a large radius and thin annulus, it gives a force approximately half the linear moving-magnet value:

$$
\mechanical{F}\approx\frac{\electric{q}\mechanical{\mathbf v}\times\magnetic{\mathbf B}}{2}=\frac{\electric{q}\mechanical{R}\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\magnetic{\mathbf B}}{2}.
$$

Unlike a rotating solid disc magnet, the rotating annular magnet is predicted to drive a nearby charge in a direction perpendicular to the magnet's local motion. The source adds $\mechanical{v}\approx\mechanical{R}\signal{\omega}/2$. The effect is attributed entirely to contraction-induced charge excess at the inner boundary (Fig. 10.10b). If conductor and annular magnet rotate together, the author predicts only partial cancellation of Ampère and contraction-related forces. He proposes such experiments as further tests of Cosmic Relativity and as tests against accounts based solely on relative charge–magnet motion.

> [!note]
> In SI units, Gauss's law for volume charge density is $\nabla\cdot\electric{\mathbf E}=\electric{\rho}/\electric{\varepsilon}_0$; the source omits the permittivity factor. It calls the displayed force an “EMF on a charge” and does not explain whether the later $\mechanical{v}\approx\mechanical{R}\signal{\omega}/2$ is an effective induction velocity or a particle velocity. The preceding force expression instead uses magnet speed $\mechanical{R}\signal{\omega}$ with a separate factor $1/2$.

## Interpretation and checks

A current-current analogy cannot omit the local electric field or stress balance. Check units in each line of the proposed force formula and distinguish force, emf, and potential energy. The small-length-contraction expansion carries a one-half at leading second order; omitting it changes an inferred coefficient. Reversing conductor rotation should reverse the motional emf for a fixed field. Magnet motion alone must be analysed through the actual field pattern.
