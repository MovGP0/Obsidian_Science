> [!TLDR]
> The author interprets transformed metric components as scalar and vector gravitational potentials generated by cosmic matter in relative motion.

## Physical question and model

How is the cosmic potential assigned a finite value? The calculation assumes a Newtonian uniform sphere and an outer radius associated with cosmic causal structure. State its sign, cutoff, and reference zero before using it as a physical energy.

## Lecture account

The metric and line-element language gave a concise derivation, but components $\mechanical{g}_{00},\mechanical{g}_{0i}$ represent potentials from real sources. We must recast the results as gravity of actual cosmic matter. Finite cosmic age permits a potential calculation even if spatial extent is infinite: only finite matter is in causal contact with a point at a given time.

For homogeneous isotropic density $\mechanical{\rho}$, approximate Newtonian potential is

$$
\mechanical{\Phi}_u\approx-\int_0^{\mechanical{R}_u}\frac{\mechanical{G}\,4\pi\mechanical{r}^2\mechanical{\rho}\,d\mechanical{r}}{\mechanical{r}}=-2\pi\mechanical{G}\mechanical{\rho}\mechanical{R}_u^2. \tag{10.9}
$$

Here $\mechanical{R}_u\approx\mechanical{c}\mechanical{T}_u$ is a notional causal size at age $\mechanical{T}_u=13.8$ billion years. With measured critical density $\mechanical{\rho}_c=3\mechanical{H}_0^2/(8\pi\mechanical{G})$,

$$
\mechanical{\Phi}_u\approx-\mechanical{c}^2. \tag{10.10}
$$

The author emphasises that experiments cannot dismiss interpreting all observed motional relativistic effects through the cosmic gravitational factor $(1-\mechanical{v}^2/|\mechanical{\Phi}_u|)^{1/2}$ rather than a ‘Lorentz factor’ $(1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2)^{1/2}$.

Uniform motion relative to the cosmic frame modifies the potential and adds a velocity-dependent one, as in electrodynamics. Kinetic energy increases effective gravitating mass to $\mechanical{m}/\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}$. Thus, $\mechanical{\Phi}'_u(\mechanical{v})=\mechanical{\Phi}_u/\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}$. The velocity-proportional vector potential is

$$
\mechanical{A}_i=-\frac{\mechanical{\gamma}\mechanical{v}_i}{\mechanical{c}}\mechanical{\Phi}_u=-\frac{\mechanical{v}_i}{\mechanical{c}}\frac{\mechanical{\Phi}_u}{\sqrt{1-\mechanical{v}^2/\mechanical{c}^2}}. \tag{10.11}
$$

The source also writes $\mechanical{A}_i=\mechanical{g}_{0i}/\mechanical{g}_{00}$. The potentials obey

$$
\mechanical{\Phi}_u^2=\mechanical{\Phi}'_u(\mechanical{v})^2-|\mechanical{A}_i(\mechanical{v})|^2. \tag{10.12}
$$

In this relation, $\mechanical{\Phi}_u$ is the cosmic potential in a nonrotating frame at absolute rest in the Universe.

> [!note]
> Equation (10.11) gives $\mechanical{A}_i$ the same units as gravitational potential. Identifying it directly with the ratio of dimensionless metric components requires a normalised-potential convention; the source does not state the conversion at this step.

## Interpretation and checks

The shell integral scales with density times radius squared. Its magnitude is sensitive to the cutoff, and a Hubble sphere is not generally a causal horizon. A Lorentz-like scalar/vector invariant also presumes a transformation rule; it is not obtained from the density integral alone. Check the zero-velocity limit and use one normalisation for the potential in the metric and in the particle action.
