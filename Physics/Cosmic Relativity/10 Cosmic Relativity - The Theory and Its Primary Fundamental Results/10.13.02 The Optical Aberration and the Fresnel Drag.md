> [!TLDR]
> The author reproduces Fresnel drag and optical aberration through competing electromagnetic transport by a medium and gravitational dragging by cosmic matter.

## Physical question and model

What part of Fresnel drag is being recovered? Assume a specified refractive index, neglect dispersion if appropriate, and work only to first order in medium velocity. Optical aberration also needs entry and exit refraction through the detector.

## Lecture account

The Chapter 3 phenomena are first-order effects in $\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$. The author therefore attributes them to relative cosmic matter currents, with relativistic potential $-\mechanical{v}\mechanical{\Phi}_u/\mechanical{c}$ represented by metric component $\mechanical{g}_{0i}$.

Stellar aberration can still be treated geometrically because the source is independent of the observer's frame. The author treats light propagation in the absolute cosmic frame as an invariant physical fact. Wavefronts have fixed directions in that frame. Observer motion gives a geometrical transformation into the moving frame, equivalent to wavefront dragging at velocity $-\mechanical{v}$.

For light in a moving medium, such as flowing water or an optical fibre, propagation is tied to the medium at speed $\mechanical{c}/\signal{n}$. A naive addition would give $\mechanical{c}'=\mechanical{c}/\signal{n}+\mechanical{v}$. Fresnel instead obtained

$$
\mechanical{c}'=\frac{\mechanical{c}}{\signal{n}}+\mechanical{v}\left(1-\frac{1}{\signal{n}^2}\right). \tag{10.37}
$$

Fizeau verified this experimentally; see [[03.04 The Speed of Light in Moving Media - Fresnel Drag]]. STR gives the same result for $\mechanical{v}\ll\mechanical{c}$ using relativistic velocity addition.

In Cosmic Relativity, cosmic gravity controls light propagation as it controls other dynamics. A moving medium carries light with it, but the potential $-\mechanical{v}\mechanical{\Phi}_u/\mechanical{c}$ acts opposite to the medium's motion. The author identifies this as gravitational dragging by the relative cosmic matter current. Electromagnetic coupling $\electric{\varepsilon}/\electric{\varepsilon}_0$ carries light with the medium, while cosmic gravitational coupling retards it. The source writes the residual drag as $-\mechanical{v}/\electric{\varepsilon}=-\mechanical{v}/\signal{n}^2$. Hence

$$
\mechanical{c}'=\frac{\mechanical{c}}{\signal{n}}+\mechanical{v}-\frac{\mechanical{v}}{\signal{n}^2}=\frac{\mechanical{c}}{\signal{n}}+\mechanical{v}\left(1-\frac{1}{\signal{n}^2}\right). \tag{10.38}
$$

> [!note]
> In the source's residual-drag expression, $\electric{\varepsilon}$ must mean dimensionless relative permittivity for the equality with $\signal{n}^2$ and the velocity units to hold. The preceding coupling ratio instead uses absolute permittivity divided by $\electric{\varepsilon}_0$.

The same formula is used for aberration in a medium of index $\signal{n}$. A telescope brings light from across its aperture to a central image. Along and opposite the telescope's motion, the relative light speeds are $\mechanical{c}'-\mechanical{v}$ and $\mechanical{c}'+\mechanical{v}$. Light from diametrically opposite aperture points therefore reaches the image centre at different times. The source gives the relative-speed difference as

$$
\delta\mechanical{c}=\mechanical{c}'_+-\mechanical{c}'_-=\frac{\mechanical{c}}{\signal{n}}+\frac{\mechanical{v}}{\signal{n}^2}-\left(\frac{\mechanical{c}}{\signal{n}}-\frac{\mechanical{v}}{\signal{n}^2}\right)=\frac{2\mechanical{v}}{\signal{n}^2}. \tag{10.39}
$$

The angle inside the medium is then

$$
\frac{\delta\mechanical{c}\,t}{2\mechanical{R}}=\frac{\delta\mechanical{c}\,\mechanical{R}}{(\mechanical{c}/\signal{n})\,2\mechanical{R}}=\frac{\mechanical{v}}{\mechanical{c}\signal{n}}.
$$

Refraction reduces the angle by $1/\signal{n}$, so the inferred additional aberration remains $\mechanical{v}/\mechanical{c}$. The author concludes that Cosmic Relativity reproduces moving-frame aberration as a gravitational effect.

## Interpretation and checks

The drag coefficient tends to zero as refractive index tends to one. That is a useful check on a moving-material term. A difference between two directional coordinate speeds must be connected to the actual integrated phase before claiming an observable. The full apparatus can cancel a naive refractive-index dependence. Distinguish an algebraic restatement of the measured coefficient from a derivation of a new microscopic interaction.
