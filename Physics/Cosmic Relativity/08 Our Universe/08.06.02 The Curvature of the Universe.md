> [!TLDR]
> A standard source’s angular size at known distance can test whether spatial geometry is flat, positively curved, or negatively curved.

## Physical question and model

What distance does an angular ruler measure in an expanding Universe? Use angular-diameter distance, defined from a known transverse physical size and its observed angle. It depends on expansion history as well as spatial curvature.

## Lecture account

Light from distant sources probes cosmic geometry in the general-relativistic sense. A standard source of characteristic length $\mechanical{l}$ has angular size $\mechanical{\theta}=\mechanical{l}/\mechanical{d}$. Measuring it at different distances $\mechanical{d}$ can distinguish flat Euclidean space from curvature. In a flat Universe, angular size decreases as $1/\mechanical{d}$, the usual perspective relation. Positive curvature converges light paths, making the source appear larger than in flat space, as if closer than its actual distance. In an open, negatively curved Universe, it appears smaller than $\mechanical{l}/\mechanical{d}$ (Fig. 8.9).

![[cosmic-relativity-08-09-angular-ruler.svg|**Fig. 8.9** Spatial curvature measured with a standard ruler: a light-emitting structure of known intrinsic size l at distance d. (a) Flat Euclidean space, k = 0, gives θ = l/d. (b) Positive curvature, k = +1, gives a larger apparent size (dashed). (c) Negative curvature, k = −1, gives a smaller apparent size.]]

The strongest present indication comes from CMBR fluctuations at different angular scales [18]. The standard rulers are many radiation-temperature patches around $1^\circ$ across, corresponding to sound waves propagating from their centres, a process understood and predicted before observation. The method’s details are not important here. The result is that our Universe is spatially flat and Euclidean, $k=0$, to high precision. Thus, mean gravitating matter–energy density is critical, $\mechanical{\rho}_c=3\mechanical{H}_0^2/(8\pi\mechanical{G})$. The measured Hubble parameter is about $70\,\unit{km/(s\,Mpc)}$. Several methods support a mean cosmic density equal to critical: $\mechanical{\rho}_u/\mechanical{\rho}_c\equiv\mechanical{\Omega}_u=1$.

What accounts for the missing $68\%$? Ordinary atoms and dark matter together give about 30–32% of critical density. Nearly 70% therefore appears to be gravitationally active in the Universe. No direct evidence for it appears in clustered-structure dynamics, even large galaxy clusters, so it must be smooth over much larger scales. This unknown component is called ‘dark energy’ [20]. It might be a light particle, mysterious field, or unfamiliar entity. Its fractional density is $\mechanical{\rho}_{\mathrm{de}}/\mechanical{\rho}_c\approx0.68$.

## Interpretation and checks

Even a spatially flat expanding model need not obey the naive inverse relation with present proper distance at every redshift. Angular-diameter distance can turn over, so the same fixed object can subtend a larger angle beyond a certain redshift. A curvature inference needs standard-ruler calibration and other parameters. Compare predictions at the same redshift and ruler size; otherwise a geometric cartoon can be mistaken for a complete cosmological test.
