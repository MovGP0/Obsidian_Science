> [!TLDR]
> Fundamental relativity and dynamics theories were completed before observations established the scale of the extragalactic Universe.

## Physical question and model

How did early cosmological models connect gravity with astronomical evidence? Keep a historical model's assumed size, static character, and matter content separate from measurements available at that time. A model can be useful before every parameter is known.

## Lecture account

The first 30 years of the twentieth century brought many physics revolutions, even amid a world war’s devastation. Essentially all fundamental dynamics and relativity theories reached their mature form. Astronomy of the solar neighbourhood and Milky Way advanced similarly. Quantum theory of multiparticle systems was applied to stellar evolution, and the beginnings of relativistic effects on stars’ quantum stability were recognised. Yet although stellar astrophysics was mature, extragalactic astrophysics and cosmology were limited.

![[cosmic-relativity-08-01-milky-way-clouds.svg|**Fig. 8.1** The Milky Way and the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds more or less defined the known Universe before 1920. An explanatory schematic replaces the source photograph credited to ESO/Y. Beletsky.]]

Barely a century ago, observational knowledge of matter distribution was limited to less than a thousandth of the Universe’s currently known size. In volume, this is less than a billionth of its total matter. Apart from the Milky Way, identified as the home galaxy of numerous stars, a few nebulae were studied, including the Magellanic Clouds and Andromeda (Fig. 8.1). These lie within about a megaparsec ($3.26$ million light years), while the estimated causally connected Universe exceeds 10 billion light years. By 1920, astronomers were only beginning to identify numerous galaxy-like nebulae. The limitation was mainly a lack of telescopes powerful enough to resolve sources beyond our galactic neighbourhood. Large telescopes with good optics and apertures exceeding a metre became operational only after 1900. The source states that the 100-inch telescope at Mt. Palomar was installed in 1917, preceded by W. Herschel’s 50-inch telescope around 1800 and a 60-inch telescope at Mt. Palomar commissioned in 1908. Photography and classification of atomic spectra also had to advance alongside the telescopes. E. Hubble identified Andromeda as an external galaxy in 1923. In 1929 he announced systematic recession of several identified galaxies [1], marking the beginning of modern observational cosmology.

In contrast, theoretical cosmological modelling was vigorously pursued, pioneered by Einstein immediately after completing the GTR [2, 3]. Models were discussed as solutions to his new gravitational-dynamics equations. In his first paper, Einstein highlighted the instability of a finite Newtonian Universe by adding a constant potential to Poisson’s equation. This became the cosmological constant in a modified Einstein equation. He changed the 1915 form $\mechanical{R}_{ik}-\mechanical{g}_{ik}\mechanical{R}=\mechanical{\kappa}\energy{T}_{ik}$ to

$$
\mechanical{R}_{ik}-\mechanical{g}_{ik}\mechanical{R}+\mechanical{\Lambda}\mechanical{g}_{ik}=\mechanical{\kappa}\energy{T}_{ik}. \tag{8.1}
$$

> [!note]
> The source omits the factor $1/2$ before the scalar-curvature term in both forms here. Compare the field equation (7.2) in [[07.02 General Theory of Relativity as a Theory of Gravity]]. The printed form is retained.

The arbitrary $\mechanical{\Lambda}$ term, without a physical basis, could cancel the matter-density effect to give a finely balanced static Universe. The balance is unstable: a density fluctuation upsets equilibrium with a constant $\mechanical{\Lambda}$.

Einstein introduced this term to fit the apparent static equilibrium of universal matter, twelve years before decisive observations of galaxy recession. Its value was related to average uniform density and the closed Universe’s radius by $\mechanical{\Lambda}=\mechanical{\kappa}\mechanical{\rho}/2=1/\mechanical{R}^2$. All later theoretical models assumed spherical symmetry (isotropy) and homogeneity of space and matter.

W. de Sitter soon showed that the Einstein Universe expands even without matter, $\energy{T}_{ik}=0$ [4], and does so exponentially. In my opinion, the ironic influence of this model was that Einstein accepted a hypothetical model Universe as theoretical evidence against Mach’s principle as a guide to the final equation.

Early significant contributions came from W. de Sitter, A. Friedmann, G. Lemaître, and H. P. Robertson. While de Sitter focused on models with a cosmological constant, Friedmann in 1922 proposed [5] the line element now called Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (FLRW):

$$
d\mechanical{s}^2=-\mechanical{c}^2dt^2+\mechanical{a}^2(t)\left(\frac{d\mechanical{r}^2}{1-k\mechanical{r}^2}+\mechanical{r}^2d\mechanical{\theta}^2+\mechanical{r}^2\sin^2\mechanical{\theta}\,d\mechanical{\phi}^2\right). \tag{8.2}
$$

The factor $\mechanical{a}(t)$ describes uniform expansion or contraction with universal absolute time $t$. Parameter $k$ encodes spatial curvature. Hubble’s discovery of systematic galaxy recession, suggesting uniform expansion, determined theoretical cosmology’s course. Thus, the FLRW metric became the standard big-bang model’s basis. It obeys Einstein’s equation and allows finite ($k=+1$) and infinite ($k=0,-1$) Universes. If $k=0$, as observations suggest, the Universe is spatially infinite but finite in time, with a birth in the past. Its present theoretical age is 14 billion ($1.4\times10^{10}$) years.

Some features may be conceptually uncomfortable but remain true of the standard FLRW model with $k=0$, later called a critical Universe. If $k=0$ now, it always was and always will be. Thus, the Universe was always infinite, even at its big-bang birth. The standard model implies infinite density, heat, and size about 14 billion years ago. Perhaps this is too many infinities in one lifetime; it illustrates the difficulty of consistently describing a physical entity containing everything, including space and time. This book concerns dynamics and relativity, rather than the correct theory of early cosmic evolution. I will identify conceptual issues without discussing their details [6, 7].

## Interpretation and checks

When comparing field equations across the historical discussion, check the trace coefficient and sign convention. Equation (8.1) omits the usual one-half multiplying the metric times scalar curvature, unlike the later standard Einstein equation. Treat it as a source-formula issue rather than a different measured law. Modern conclusions about expansion should not be attributed to an early static model without adding the intervening observations.
