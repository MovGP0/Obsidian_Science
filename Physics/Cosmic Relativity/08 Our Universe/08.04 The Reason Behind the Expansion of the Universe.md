> [!TLDR]
> The author questions a net-force explanation of deceleration in a homogeneous infinite Universe and turns to the time dependence of its uniform gravitational potential.

## Physical question and model

Can a homogeneous expanding Universe decelerate without a spatial potential gradient? In relativistic cosmology, neighbouring comoving geodesics can have changing separation because spacetime curvature evolves. A zero coordinate gradient in one homogeneous description does not remove that geodesic-deviation effect.

## Lecture account

Why does the Universe expand? In finite Newtonian cosmology, a Universe starting with no galaxy recession collapses. An initial outward push, such as an explosion, allows expansion. Almost all textbooks say gravitational attraction slows this expansion, like a thrown stone. Unfortunately, this is incorrect. *In an infinite Universe with $k=0$ and uniform density and pressure, the gravitational potential is the same everywhere and has no spatial gradient.* If $\nabla\mechanical{\phi}=0$, a particle feels no gravitational force. Thus, galaxy recession does not decelerate through attraction and a ‘pull’ from other matter. Yet Einstein’s equation predicts a dynamic Universe. What explains expansion and deceleration with ordinary matter and energy (Fig. 8.5)?

![[cosmic-relativity-08-05-arbitrary-centres.svg|**Fig. 8.5** The author’s argument for an infinite spatially flat Universe. A uniform potential has no gradient. A common Newtonian calculation uses matter inside a sphere centred on an arbitrary point and assumes exterior matter exerts no force. The same argument can be applied to any centre, so the author concludes there is no net force on the body. Attraction between bodies is therefore not the reason for cosmic deceleration; positive density remains crucial.]]

The source caption prints the radial-force relation $\mechanical{F}/\mechanical{m}=-(4\pi/3)\mechanical{\rho}\mechanical{a}$ and a ‘Friedmann equation’ $\ddot{\mechanical{a}}/\mechanical{a}=-4\pi\mechanical{\rho}/3$.

> [!note]
> These two expressions in the source caption omit the gravitational constant $\mechanical{G}$. With mass density in SI units, the corresponding factors are $-(4\pi\mechanical{G}/3)\mechanical{\rho}\mechanical{a}$ and $-4\pi\mechanical{G}\mechanical{\rho}/3$. The caption’s printed expressions are retained above.

A spatially uniform matter density in an infinite $k=0$ Universe determines a spatially uniform potential, so $\nabla^2\mechanical{\phi}=0$. But this does not restrict its time dependence. Newtonian gravity and Poisson’s equation do not address this issue. This is Newtonian gravity’s real incompleteness. Einstein’s equation instead treats relativity and dynamics in space and time. For the highly symmetric cosmological problem, it can be understood as a four-dimensional Poisson equation:

$$
\nabla^2\mechanical{\phi}-\frac{\partial^2\mechanical{\phi}}{\mechanical{c}^2\partial t^2}=4\pi\mechanical{G}\mechanical{\rho}(\mechanical{x},t). \tag{8.18}
$$

With $\nabla^2\mechanical{\phi}=0$, this reduces to

$$
\frac{\partial^2\mechanical{\phi}}{\partial t^2}=-4\pi\mechanical{c}^2\mechanical{G}\mechanical{\rho}(t)=-4\pi\mechanical{c}^2\mechanical{G}\frac{\mechanical{\rho}_0\mechanical{V}_0}{\mechanical{V}(t)}. \tag{8.19}
$$

An increasing volume reduces the rate at which potential changes, so expansion implies deceleration. Since potential is independent of spatial variables,

$$
\frac{d\mechanical{\phi}}{dt}=-4\pi\mechanical{c}^2\mechanical{G}\int\mechanical{\rho}(t)\,dt. \tag{8.20}
$$

*With positive matter density, potential always decreases, implying continuous change in the Universe’s homogeneous density.* Expansion in Einstein’s Universe comes neither from an initial push nor an extra repulsive field. If relativistic evolution is correct, positive density implies a dynamic Universe.

> [!note]
> In current relativistic cosmology, the reason for expansion remains enigmatic. Through an analysis of Einstein’s equation for $k=0$, relating matter density to a spatially homogeneous gravitational potential, I have shown that positive matter–energy density implies a dynamic, decelerating Universe.

With spatial curvature, $k\ne0$, the term $\nabla^2\mechanical{\phi}$ is nonzero. Writing it as $\mechanical{\phi}_0\mechanical{c}^2/\mechanical{R}^2$ shows that it acts like negative density in time evolution. Since $\mechanical{R}=\mechanical{c}\mechanical{T}=\mechanical{c}/\mechanical{H}_0$, free evolution occurs at critical density $\mechanical{\rho}_c\propto\mechanical{H}_0^2/(4\pi\mechanical{G})$.

With a cosmological constant, the Poisson correspondence becomes

$$
\nabla^2\mechanical{\phi}-\frac{\partial^2\mechanical{\phi}}{\mechanical{c}^2\partial t^2}-\mechanical{\Lambda}\mechanical{\phi}=4\pi\mechanical{G}\mechanical{\rho}. \tag{8.21}
$$

For $\nabla\mechanical{\phi}=\nabla^2\mechanical{\phi}=0$,

$$
-\frac{\partial^2\mechanical{\phi}}{\mechanical{c}^2\partial t^2}-\mechanical{\Lambda}\mechanical{\phi}=4\pi\mechanical{G}\mechanical{\rho}. \tag{8.22}
$$

A static Universe follows if $\mechanical{\Lambda}\mechanical{\phi}+4\pi\mechanical{G}\mechanical{\rho}=0$. There is then no time evolution and $\mechanical{\phi}=-4\pi\mechanical{G}/\mechanical{\Lambda}$, Einstein’s result from [[08.03 Einstein's Cosmological Constant|Section 8.3]].

> [!note]
> The source’s last static-potential expression omits density. The preceding balance and equation (8.4) give $\mechanical{\phi}=-4\pi\mechanical{G}\mechanical{\rho}/\mechanical{\Lambda}$. The printed expression is retained above.

For $\mechanical{\rho}=0$,

$$
\frac1{\mechanical{\phi}}\frac{\partial^2\mechanical{\phi}}{\partial t^2}=-\mechanical{\Lambda}\mechanical{c}^2. \tag{8.23}
$$

This integrates to $\mechanical{\phi}(t)=\mechanical{\phi}_0\exp(\sqrt{-\mechanical{\Lambda}\mechanical{c}^2}\,t)$, analogous to de Sitter’s result without matter and with cosmological constant. He found exponential cosmic expansion, $\mechanical{R}(t)=\mechanical{R}_0\exp(\mechanical{H}t)$, with $\mechanical{H}=\sqrt{-\mechanical{\Lambda}\mechanical{c}^2}$. Also,

$$
q_0=-\frac{\ddot{\mechanical{a}}}{\mechanical{a}\mechanical{H}^2}=-\frac{\ddot{\mechanical{\phi}}}{\mechanical{\phi}\mechanical{H}^2}=-\frac{\mechanical{\Lambda}}{(\sqrt{\mechanical{\Lambda}})^2}=-1. \tag{8.24}
$$

This discussion shows that cosmic deceleration or acceleration is unrelated to gravity as spatial attraction or repulsion. No spatial potential gradient means no force. Gravity extends through space and time, as in Einstein’s equation; matter density determines potential’s time evolution. Initially positive density drives potential downward, implying a corresponding change in spatially uniform cosmic matter density. A time-dependent potential is also like a ‘force’, but scalar rather than spatially directed. It may be viewed as *a force directed opposite to time evolution*, which cannot be described as spatial attraction or repulsion.

The discussion treats gravity physically as an interaction, giving geometry no special role. Matter density and potential change, while underlying space has no dynamical role in cosmic evolution. Section 8.7 develops this further.

## Interpretation and checks

The scalar wave equation proposed for a cosmic potential is an added field model, not a consequence of the Newtonian Poisson equation. It needs initial data and a demonstrated relation to the scale factor. Uniformity alone does not establish that relation. Recover the dust and vacuum-dominated Friedmann limits before claiming a unique explanation of expansion. A freely falling galaxy has zero proper acceleration even when the separation from other galaxies has a nonzero second derivative.
