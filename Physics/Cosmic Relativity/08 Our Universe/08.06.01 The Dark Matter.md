> [!TLDR]
> Galactic rotation curves probe the gravitational mass distribution beyond the visible stars and motivate the inference of dark matter.

## Physical question and model

Why does a nearly flat galactic rotation curve suggest additional gravitating mass? In an approximately circular, weak-field orbit, the measured speed fixes enclosed mass through centripetal balance. Converting this to a mass profile assumes the gravitational law and orbital geometry.

## Lecture account

Condensed matter in galaxies gravitationally controls stellar and gas dynamics. Thus, the best probe of gravitating matter distribution is the Keplerian orbital velocity of stars and gas clouds as a function of radius. Such ‘rotation curves’ have been mapped in many nearby galaxies with sufficiently resolved positions (Fig. 8.8). Velocities come from Doppler shifts of identifiable radiation; redshift dispersion measures velocity dispersion. Typical stellar orbital speeds are below $200\,\unit{km/s}$. Rotation is measurable beyond the readily visible stellar boundary using surrounding hot-gas radiation, such as hydrogen’s $21\,\unit{cm}$ radio line.

A galaxy typically has a central bulge containing most of its mass and a disc structure. Newtonian orbital velocity about a central mass is $\mechanical{v}=\sqrt{\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{r}}$. The gravitational potential of a localised mass concentration increases through the matter-filled region, then falls as $\mechanical{G}\mechanical{M}/\mechanical{r}$ outside it. Thus, rotational velocity is expected to peak and then decrease with distance from the centre (Fig. 8.8).

Surprisingly, almost every galaxy has a ‘flat rotation curve’ extending as far as measurements reach. Orbital speed stays roughly constant or even increases slightly. This suggests invisible matter spread much farther than the visible galaxy. Its potential must compensate for the visible matter’s decreasing contribution. The distribution must be extended, with slowly varying density, and have finite extent and a centre to affect orbital velocities. After half a century of searches and debate, its nature remains unknown. The usual belief is an unidentified fundamental particle. If gravity’s law is reliable on galactic scales, much dark matter certainly exists within and around galaxies [19].

There is also strong evidence in large galaxy clusters, where orbital velocities are less well defined. Light from distant sources is gravitationally bent and distorted by the extended cluster, producing lensing. Analysing the source’s distorted image estimates the lensing mass distribution.

These methods put mean dark-matter density at 25–30% of critical: $\mechanical{\rho}_{\mathrm{dm}}/\mechanical{\rho}_c\approx0.27$. Identifying its physical constituent is important, but experiments remain without an answer. Consequently, modifications of gravity or dynamics at very small fields or accelerations have also been proposed.

## Interpretation and checks

Outside a compact fixed mass, orbital speed falls with inverse square root of radius. A roughly constant speed instead implies enclosed mass growing approximately in proportion to radius in a spherical approximation. This is a useful limit check. Gas pressure, inclination, noncircular motion, and visible mass estimates affect the inference. Rotation curves do not alone identify a dark-matter particle; lensing and other independent probes test the broader interpretation.
