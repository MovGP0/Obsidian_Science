> [!TLDR]
> The cosmic microwave background is a nearly uniform radiation field from the early Universe, distinct from light produced by stars and hot gas.

## Physical question and model

What does the CMB spectrum measure, and what does its anisotropy add? A near-blackbody monopole characterises a radiation bath. A moving detector sees a dipole, while smaller anisotropies contain information about perturbations and foregrounds.

## Lecture account

Visible light and other electromagnetic radiation from stars and hot gas clouds contribute about $10^{-14}\,\unit{J/m^3}$, or $10^{-31}\,\unit{kg/m^3}$. This is insignificant compared with the total mean matter density of $10^{-27}\,\unit{kg/m^3}$. Apart from light from stellar nuclear processes, a uniform background from the early Universe exists, called the Cosmic Microwave Background Radiation (CMBR) [17, 18]. Its present peak wavelength is $1.06\,\unit{mm}$, corresponding to a black-body spectrum at $2.7255\,\unit{K}$. Its present energy-equivalent mass density is about $5\times10^{-30}\,\unit{kg/m^3}$, so it is not gravitationally significant.

The CMBR represents early big-bang history. For this book, its most important features are remarkable isotropy down to small scales and a monotonically decreasing uniform temperature. As shown later, *the averaged CMBR serves as a universal time, synchronised everywhere more accurately than one part in a million. It also gives a reference for measuring absolute motion relative to the unique cosmic frame to an accuracy of a kilometre per second* (Fig. 8.6).

## Interpretation and checks

A blackbody peak depends on whether intensity is plotted per wavelength or per frequency, so quote the representation with its peak. Approximate isotropy of the radiation is not exact equality of every local thermometer reading. The radiation energy is small in the current matter budget but was important early in cosmic history. Separate that gravitational role from using the CMB as a practical reference for motion.
