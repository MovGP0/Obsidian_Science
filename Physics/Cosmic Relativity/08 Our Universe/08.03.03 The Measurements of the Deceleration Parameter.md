> [!TLDR]
> The deceleration parameter describes the changing expansion rate and depends on matter, curvature, and a cosmological-constant contribution.

## Physical question and model

What does the deceleration parameter measure? It is a dimensionless combination of the scale factor and its first two time derivatives. The minus sign makes positive values correspond to slowing expansion when the expansion rate is positive.

## Lecture account

The Hubble parameter represents expansion rate. Its changing rate is described by the *deceleration parameter*,

$$
q_0\equiv-\frac1{\mechanical{H}_0^2}\frac{\ddot{\mechanical{a}}}{\mechanical{a}}=-\frac{\mechanical{a}\ddot{\mechanical{a}}}{\dot{\mechanical{a}}^2}. \tag{8.17}
$$

With matter only, the source states $2\ddot{\mechanical{a}}/\mechanical{a}=8\pi\mechanical{G}\mechanical{\rho}/3=k\mechanical{c}^2/\mechanical{a}^2-\mechanical{H}_0^2$, relating deceleration to curvature by $q_0=1+k\mechanical{c}^2/(\mechanical{H}_0^2\mechanical{a}^2)$. Thus it gives $q_0=1/2$ for flat $k=0$, and $q=0$ without matter in the flat Universe. Distant-galaxy redshift measurements can directly give the second-order expansion deceleration term, first stressed by Hoyle and Sandage [16].

> [!note]
> The printed matter-only relations are inconsistent with the preceding equations. Equation (8.16) gives negative $2\ddot{\mechanical{a}}/\mechanical{a}$ for positive pressure-free density. Combining (8.15) and (8.16) gives $q_0=\tfrac12[1+k\mechanical{c}^2/(\mechanical{H}_0^2\mechanical{a}^2)]$. This agrees with the stated flat matter-model value $1/2$; the source’s printed expressions are preserved above.

A cosmological constant changes the situation greatly. With relatively negligible matter, $\mechanical{H}_0^2=\mechanical{\Lambda}\mechanical{c}^2/3$ and $\ddot{\mechanical{a}}/\mechanical{a}=\mechanical{\Lambda}\mechanical{c}^2/3$, giving $q_0=-1$. Adding matter at half the critical density can give $q=0$, mimicking a flat matter-free Universe. Until about two decades ago, astronomers broadly favoured $q_0\geq1/2$. This has changed: distance–redshift measurements using distant supernovae as standard candles give $q_0\approx-0.6\pm0.2$, consistent with a component like the cosmological constant, called dark energy. For example, $70\%$ dark energy and $30\%$ matter give $q_0=(0.3\times(1/2)+0.7\times(-1))\simeq-0.55$, preferred by Planck CMBR observations [18].

The favoured consensus model is spatially flat, $k=0$, with $5\%$ atomic matter, $27\%$ dark matter, and $68\%$ dark energy. The Hubble parameter is between $73$ and $68\,\unit{km/(s\,Mpc)}$. For relativity and dynamics, the relevant point is that average density is accurately critical.

## Interpretation and checks

A negative value indicates accelerated expansion, not contraction. Inferring it from distance observations requires a distance–redshift model and calibration. Check a linear scale factor: its second derivative is zero and the parameter vanishes. Check exponential expansion: the parameter is minus one. Historical estimates should remain tied to their data model rather than interpreted as direct acceleration readings.
